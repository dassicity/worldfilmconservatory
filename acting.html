A-1 · Year 1 · Module One

Foundations — The Instrument

The Trailer

Sanford Meisner defined acting in eleven words: living truthfully under imaginary circumstances. Every school in this track is a different answer to the same problem that definition poses — how a person standing in a lit room, watched by forty strangers and a lens, surrounded by cables and marks, can do something that is actually happening rather than something being indicated. The answer is never talent alone. It is an instrument — a body, a voice, an attention, an imagination — that has been trained until it can be relied upon on a Tuesday afternoon at the eleventh take.

This module is that instrument, before any method claims it. Relaxation, because tension is the actor's first and most persistent enemy and the camera photographs it before it photographs anything else. Concentration, because attention is what the audience actually watches — a person genuinely doing something is interesting and a person pretending to is not. Observation, because the material of all acting is human behaviour, and the actor who does not watch people has nothing to work from. And the definition itself, taken apart word by word: what "truthfully" asks of you, and what "imaginary circumstances" gives you permission to do. Before the methods, the instrument. Before the instrument, the willingness to stop pretending.

Two chapters: relaxation, concentration and observation — the instrument tuned; and living truthfully under imaginary circumstances — the definition, taken apart. Sit down. Do nothing. Notice what that costs you.

Chapters

Three disciplines that precede every method. Relaxation: not looseness but the absence of UNNECESSARY tension — the held jaw, the locked knees, the raised shoulders, the shallow breath that a camera reads as falseness before a single line is spoken (CL-13's close-up from the actor's side: the lens is a lie detector for the neck); the training — the somatic disciplines (Alexander's inhibition and primary control; Feldenkrais's awareness through movement; the constructive rest position), the classical actor-training relaxation sequences (the tension-and-release inventory, the breath re-established — A-5's foundation), and the working practice of a RELAXATION CHECK before every take and every audition; the paradox the module insists on — relaxation is not calm, and an actor can be relaxed while terrified, because the terror is the character's and the tension is the actor's. Concentration: the attention placed somewhere real — on the partner (A-3's Meisner: the other person is the whole job), on a task (the object exercises: making coffee, mending a shirt, doing something that actually requires attention), on a sensory reality (Hagen's sense memory — the cold, the smell, the weight, imagined until the body responds); the CIRCLE of attention (Stanislavski's concentric circles — A-2: the small circle of the hands, the medium of the partner, the large of the room, moved deliberately), the killer of concentration (self-watching — the actor monitoring their own performance, which the camera records as absence), and the trainable skill of returning attention when it wanders, take after take. Observation: the actor's research — people watched and recorded (the walk, the vocal habit, the way a person holds a cup, the thing they do with their hands when they lie), the ANIMAL and OBJECT exercises (the traditional training: a creature or a thing studied and embodied, not to imitate but to find a physical life outside your own — A-4's territory), the journal kept for years, and the ethics (observation as attention, not condescension — the person watched is not a specimen; the caricature is the failure). The instrument's maintenance: sleep, the body kept able, the voice warmed (A-5), the attention practised, the life lived (the actor with no life has nothing to act with — the module's most important and least teachable note). The chapter's law: release the tension, put the attention on something real, and watch people every day — everything the next eleven modules teach is built on an instrument that can do these three things on demand.

The definition, word by word. "Living": present tense — the thing is happening now, not being recalled or reported; the actor is DOING, not showing; the test is whether something is actually occurring between two people in the room (the line said to get something, the look that receives an answer) or whether a result is being produced for an audience (INDICATING — the trade's word for the fundamental fault: signalling grief rather than grieving, playing "angry" rather than trying to get what anger wants). "Truthfully": the impulse that actually arises is the one followed — not the one the actor planned in the trailer, not the one the scene "should" have; the truthful actor is often surprised by their own behaviour, and the camera (A-7) photographs the difference; truthfulness is not the same as naturalism (A-9, A-11 — a Kabuki actor or a screwball comedian can be entirely truthful inside a wildly stylised form: the truth is the impulse's reality, not the behaviour's ordinariness — the track's most important distinction, established here and paid off in A-11). "Under imaginary circumstances": the permission — nothing in the room is real (the gun, the loss, the wedding), and the actor's imagination supplies what the world doesn't (A-2's given circumstances and magic if: not "I am dying" but "if I were in this room, with this news, what would I do?"); the actor does not need to have lived it, and the debate about whether they should (A-3's emotional memory versus Adler's imagination — the track's central methodological argument, previewed here). What the definition forbids: the result played (the emotion aimed at directly — the surest way to miss it), the self watched (CH 1), the general (playing "a cop," "a mother," "sad" rather than this person doing this thing), and the line read (the words performed rather than used). What it gives: a standard by which any performance, in any style, in any cinema, can be judged — is anything actually happening? — and the reason this track surveys many methods rather than teaching one: each is a different route to the same eleven words. The chapter's law: do not play the emotion; do something, to someone, for a reason, under circumstances you have imagined fully enough to believe — and let the feeling arrive as a by-product, or not at all.

A-2 · Year 1 · Module Two

The Stanislavski System

The Trailer

Konstantin Stanislavski spent forty years changing his mind in public. The actor and co-founder of the Moscow Art Theatre wrote the first modern system for training performers, then revised it, discarded parts of it, and spent his last years teaching something — the Method of Physical Actions — that contradicted what the world had already translated and canonised. The three books English readers know as An Actor Prepares, Building a Character and Creating a Role were cut and reorganised by their American publisher; the fuller translation, An Actor's Work, arrived only in 2008, and reads differently enough that the track flags the difference here: much of what is taught as "Stanislavski" in the English-speaking world is a 1930s translation decision.

What survives every revision is a set of questions an actor asks of a scene. What do I want? What is in my way? What do I do about it? What has just happened, and where am I? And the question that opens the whole system: what would I do IF this were true? This module is those questions as working tools — the objective and the action, the magic if, the given circumstances, the vexed inheritance of emotional memory, and the division of a scene into units and beats that makes a page of dialogue into a sequence of attempts. Every later school in this track is a quarrel with this one. Learn the system, then learn what its heirs did to it.

Three chapters: objectives, actions and the magic if — the engine; given circumstances and emotional memory — the world and the vexed inheritance; and units and beats — the scene, divided. Ask what you want.

The Method of Physical Actions — the late work explained
ARTICLES · ESSAYS

Stanislavski's last teaching: the approach through physical action and active analysis rather than through feeling — the correction he made to his own system, and the one his American heirs largely missed. Inside is CH 2's counterweight. You gain the system's own last word on emotional memory. Read two accounts before CH 2.

Chapters

The system's motor is want. The objective: what the character WANTS in this scene, phrased as something another person can give or withhold ("to get her to forgive me," "to make him leave") rather than as a state ("to be sad," "to be powerful" — the unplayable); the SUPER-OBJECTIVE (the want that governs the whole role — the spine the scene objectives serve), the through-line of action that connects them, and the test of a good objective: it is active, specific, achievable in the scene, and it matters enough that failing it costs something. The obstacle: what stands in the way (the other person's opposite want, the circumstance, the character's own fear) — no obstacle, no scene; the conflict the Screenwriting track builds (the scene's engine from the page's side) received by the actor as the reason the objective requires effort. The action: what the character DOES to get it, moment by moment — the ACTION VERB (to plead, to charm, to threaten, to dismiss, to seduce, to shame — transitive, aimed at the partner, changeable when it isn't working); the actor's score of a scene is a sequence of actions (CH 3's units), and the craft is that actions CHANGE — the character tries charm, it fails, they try threat: the tactic shift is where scenes live, and an actor playing one action for three pages is playing a mood. The magic if: the imagination's switch — not "I am a soldier in 1917" (which is false and the actor knows it) but "IF I were in these circumstances, what would I do?" — a question the actor can answer honestly from their own nature, which is why it works; the if is the bridge between the actor's real instrument (A-1) and the imaginary circumstances (CH 2), and it asks for behaviour, not feeling. The late correction: the Method of Physical Actions — Stanislavski's last teaching that the reliable route into a scene is through simple physical action truthfully performed (the actor enters, takes off the coat, pours the drink, and the scene's life follows), because feeling cannot be commanded but action can; ACTIVE ANALYSIS (the scene explored on its feet in improvised approximations before the text is learned) as the method's working form. The chapter's law: name the want, name the obstacle, choose an action to get it, change the action when it fails — and use the magic if to make the circumstances yours, because the actor who knows what they want is never lost.

The circumstances make the want make sense — and the memory question splits the profession. Given circumstances: everything true about the scene before the actor adds anything — the WHO (who am I, who are these people to me, what is our history), the WHERE (the place, its temperature, its sounds, whether I am welcome in it), the WHEN (the era, the season, the hour, how long since I slept), the WHAT (what has just happened — the event five minutes before the scene, which the actor must invent if the script doesn't supply it), the WHY (what is at stake, and what happens if I fail), and the circumstances the script states versus those the actor must fill in; the discipline is SPECIFICITY — "my mother's house" is nothing; the particular house, the smell in the hall, the chair no one sits in, is everything, and the specificity is invented with the imagination (the magic if's material) or borrowed from the actor's life (Hagen's SUBSTITUTION — the real person or place used privately to give an imaginary one weight). The moment before: the single most useful preparation on a film set (A-7 — where scenes are shot out of order and out of context) — what happened immediately before this scene, imagined fully enough that the actor enters already in motion rather than arriving from a trailer. Emotional memory: Stanislavski's early technique — the actor recalls a personal experience with enough sensory detail that its feeling returns, and uses it to fuel the scene — and its four problems: it is UNRELIABLE (the memory that worked yesterday goes flat), it is SLOW (it takes the actor out of the present and into the past, which the partner can feel), it can be HARMFUL (the actor mining trauma nightly; the profession's growing refusal of this on wellbeing grounds — a refusal the track endorses), and Stanislavski himself moved AWAY from it toward physical action (CH 1's late correction); the technique's American career (A-3: Strasberg built a school on it, Adler and Meisner rejected it) is the next module's whole argument. The safer routes to feeling: the given circumstances imagined vividly (the situation does the work), the partner attended to (A-3's Meisner: the other person supplies the impulse), the physical action performed (CH 1), and the imagination used in place of memory (Adler's position) — with the professional's note that an actor who needs to hurt themselves to do the job needs a different technique, not more courage. The chapter's law: build the circumstances until the want is inevitable, invent the moment before every time, and get your feeling from the situation, the partner and the action rather than from your own wounds.

A scene is not one thing; it is a sequence of attempts. The unit: the section of a scene in which the character pursues one objective by one approach — the unit ends when something CHANGES (the objective is achieved or abandoned, the tactic fails and a new one begins, new information arrives, a person enters or leaves); Stanislavski's "bits" (the Russian word that became "beats" in America, possibly through a Russian accent — a piece of trade folklore the track flags as charming and unverified), and the actor's SCORE: the scene marked into units, each with its objective and action verb (CH 1), so that the performance has a shape rather than a temperature. The beat: in the finer American usage, the smallest unit of change — a single impulse, a shift of thought, the half-second where a character receives something and adjusts; the beat is where screen acting lives (A-7: the camera sees the adjustment before it sees the line), and the actor's craft is letting each one land rather than playing through them toward a destination. Scoring a scene: read for the EVENT (what changes in this scene that means it cannot be cut — the Screenwriting track's test, from the actor's side), mark the units, name each unit's objective and action, identify the TURN (the moment the scene pivots), and find the TOP of the scene (what the character walks in wanting) and the BOTTOM (what they leave with — never the same, or the scene didn't happen). Playing the score and abandoning it: the score is preparation, not a cage — the actor prepares it fully and then plays the partner (A-3), letting the other person's actual behaviour change the plan; the score's purpose is that the actor has somewhere to go when the impulse doesn't come, and its danger is the performance that executes rather than lives. Across the role: the units of a whole film (the character's through-line — CH 1's super-objective, tracked scene by scene) and the ARC scored so that out-of-order shooting (A-7) never loses the place: the actor's own continuity document, which on a film set is entirely their responsibility. The chapter's law and the module's: divide the scene into what the character is trying and where it changes, score it fully, then throw the score away when your partner does something you didn't expect — because the score exists to make you free, not accurate.

The American Methods

The Trailer

In 1931 a group of young New Yorkers founded the Group Theatre, and within a few years they had split over how to act. Lee Strasberg taught affective memory, drawn from Stanislavski's early work as translated in America. Stella Adler, unconvinced, went to Paris in 1934, met Stanislavski himself for five weeks of private study, and came back reporting that the master had moved on from memory to imagination and action — Strasberg's reply, in effect, was that he taught his own method now. Sanford Meisner, watching both, built a third thing out of a single exercise in which two people repeat a sentence to each other until something real happens. Three teachers, one quarrel, and the training of most of American screen acting for seventy years.

This module is that quarrel, taught as three working techniques rather than as a partisan history. Strasberg's affective memory and relaxation, and the honest account of why the profession has moved away from it. Adler's insistence that the actor's imagination and the play's given circumstances are larger and safer sources than the actor's own past. And Meisner's repetition — the exercise that trains an actor to stop planning, get out of their head, and put their whole attention on the other person, which is the single most transferable skill in this track. Three answers to one question: where does the impulse come from?

Three chapters: Strasberg and affective memory; Adler, imagination and the given circumstances; and Meisner — repetition, listening and the reality of doing. Pick a fight with all three.

Chapters

The most famous and most contested American technique. The premise: the actor's own emotional past is a reliable, renewable source of real feeling, and a trained actor can summon it on cue; Strasberg took Stanislavski's early emotional memory (A-2 CH 2), made it central, and built the Actors Studio's practice around it. The techniques: RELAXATION (the Method's foundation — the chair exercise, tension located and released part by part; A-1 CH 1's discipline, formalised — Strasberg's students often describe relaxation as the majority of the training), SENSE MEMORY (the sensory reconstruction of an object or condition — the coffee cup, the sunshine, the shower — practised until the body responds to what isn't there; the skill that underlies everything else), AFFECTIVE or EMOTIONAL MEMORY (a past event reconstructed through its sensory details until its feeling returns, then attached to the scene's moment), the PRIVATE MOMENT (behaviour the actor would only do alone, performed in class to break public self-consciousness), ANIMAL and other physical work, and SUBSTITUTION (A-2's, used heavily). What it produced: a generation of screen performances of unprecedented interiority — Brando, Dean, Page, Winters, Pacino, De Niro among the Studio's alumni — and a vocabulary ("the Method") that entered general language, usually inaccurately. The criticisms, stated fairly: the technique is UNRELIABLE (memories fade with use), SELF-DIRECTED (the actor's attention goes inward, away from the partner — Meisner's central objection, CH 3), SLOW for film's conditions (A-7's eleven takes), potentially HARMFUL (the profession's growing consensus on trauma and wellbeing — A-1 and A-2's note, repeated here as the track's position), and not what Stanislavski finally taught (A-2 CH 1's late correction; Adler's report, CH 2); plus the confusion of the technique with "Method acting" as the press means it — the extended off-camera immersion (staying in character for months, the physical extremes) which is a different practice with its own costs and which the track treats separately in A-8. What to keep: relaxation and sense memory are durable, teachable and safe, and they are the Method's real contribution; they are used by actors who would never call themselves Method. The exemplars: On the Waterfront's taxi scene; A Streetcar Named Desire; the Studio generation's screen work — performances whose interiority changed what a camera could photograph. The chapter's law: take the relaxation and the sense memory, understand the affective memory as history and as a risk, and never confuse a technique for producing feeling with the willingness to feel.

The largest disagreement in American acting, and the safest technique in it. The Paris study: Stella Adler's five weeks with Stanislavski in 1934, undertaken because his system as Strasberg taught it was making her miserable — and her report on return that the master now worked through the given circumstances and physical action rather than through recalled emotion (A-2 CH 1's late correction, delivered to New York by an eyewitness); the Group's split followed. The technique: IMAGINATION as the actor's primary organ — "your talent is in your imagination," Adler taught, and the actor who can imagine vividly needs nobody's trauma, least of all their own; the given circumstances (A-2 CH 2) researched and imagined until they are more interesting than the actor's own life — the PLAY IS BIGGER THAN YOU (her insistence that the actor's job is to rise to the play's world, its class, its history, its ideas, rather than to shrink the play to the actor's personal experience); RESEARCH as technique (the period, the profession, the city, the politics — the actor as a reader; her scorn for the actor who would play a Russian without reading a Russian); the SIZE she demanded (the voice, the thought, the appetite — American screen acting's tendency toward mumbled smallness is partly what she was arguing against, and her position is the corrective A-9's heightened text depends on); and the ACTION (Stanislavski's, taught straight — the objective pursued through doing). The safety argument: imagination is renewable, inexhaustible, unlimited by the actor's biography, and it does not require the actor to injure themselves — the track's position, stated plainly here: an actor can build a full and devastating performance without ever using their own grief as fuel, and the great practitioners who say so (Adler, and many working actors since) are the evidence. The students: Brando (who studied with Adler and credited her, complicating the Method's claim on him), De Niro, Duvall, and a long line of screen actors whose work is imagination-led. The limits, fairly put: imagination without discipline is fantasy; the technique demands reading, research and a rigour that is less romantic than the Method's excavations; and Adler's emphasis on size can become theatricality on camera if A-7's scale discipline isn't learned. The chapter's law: research the world, imagine it until it is real to you, rise to the play rather than reducing it — and know that everything the scene needs can be invented, which means your own life remains yours.

The exercise that trains an actor to get out of their own head. Repetition: two people face each other; one says an observation about the other ("you're wearing a blue shirt"); the other repeats it; they continue, repeating, until the repetition CHANGES because something real happened between them — a smile, an irritation, an embarrassment — and the phrase changes to reflect it; the stages (mechanical repetition; repetition from the other's POINT OF VIEW — "you're wearing a blue shirt" / "I'm wearing a blue shirt"; repetition with the instruction to respond to what is actually happening; the addition of INDEPENDENT ACTIVITY — one actor absorbed in a difficult task while the other knocks at the door with an urgent need; then the first words of text). What it trains: ATTENTION ON THE PARTNER (the actor's eyes and ears outside themselves — the cure for A-1's self-watching and for CH 1's inwardness), the IMPULSE followed without planning ("don't do anything unless something happens to make you do it" — Meisner's rule), the behaviour that arises rather than the behaviour that is decided, and the fundamental discovery that the other person will give you everything you need if you are actually looking at them. The reality of doing: the INDEPENDENT ACTIVITY must be genuinely difficult and genuinely attempted (the knot really tied, the task really urgent), because a real task produces real behaviour — the principle A-1's object exercise introduced, made into a training system; "the foundation of acting is the reality of doing." Emotional preparation: Meisner's limited concession to summoned feeling — a private imaginative preparation BEFORE the scene (daydreaming into a condition) so the actor enters already alive, then abandoned entirely once the scene starts and the partner takes over; his distinction from Strasberg's use is that the preparation is never returned to mid-scene. Why it transfers to camera: repetition-trained actors listen, adjust visibly, and don't plan — precisely what A-7's close-up rewards; it is the most commonly recommended training for screen work in the English-speaking world for that reason. The limits: the training takes two years by design and is hard to self-administer; repetition without a teacher can become a game rather than a discipline; and the technique says little about text, style or classical form (A-6, A-9 supply those). The chapter's law and the module's: put your whole attention on the other person, do something that is really difficult, and don't act until something makes you — and the three American schools resolve into one instruction, which is to stop watching yourself.

A-4 · Year 1 · Module Four

Movement & Physical Approaches

The Trailer

Michael Chekhov was Anton Chekhov's nephew and Stanislavski's most gifted student — the teacher called him his most brilliant pupil — and he spent his career arguing, politely, that his master had it backwards. Feeling cannot be summoned from the inside; but a gesture made with the whole body, repeated until it is inhabited, will produce the feeling as a by-product every single time. He called it the PSYCHOLOGICAL GESTURE, fled Russia in 1928, taught in England and then in Hollywood, and left a technique that Marilyn Monroe, Anthony Quinn, Clint Eastwood and Yul Brynner all studied — and that most acting schools still treat as an elective.

This module is the body as the way in. Chekhov's gesture, imaginary centre and quality of movement. Rudolf Laban's efforts — a notation for how a body moves that gives an actor eight ways to cross a room and a vocabulary to discuss it. Anne Bogart and Tina Landau's Viewpoints, which trains an ensemble to compose in space in real time. And the mask, including the neutral mask that Jacques Lecoq used to strip an actor of their habits before giving them anything back. All of them answer a question the previous two modules could not: what do you do when the feeling won't come, or when the character's body is not yours? The inside-out schools start with want. This one starts with weight.

Three chapters: Michael Chekhov and the psychological gesture; Laban and Viewpoints — the vocabularies of movement; and mask and neutral mask — the habits stripped. Stand up.

Chapters

The body commands the psyche, not the reverse. The psychological gesture: a single full-body movement that expresses the character's essential drive — a reaching and closing fist for a grasping man, an opening upward for a generous one, a downward pressing for someone who crushes — made large and slowly with the whole body, repeated with a QUALITY (with force, with tenderness, with hesitation), until the actor's inner life reorganises around it; then MINIATURISED — the gesture shrunk until it is invisible but still being made inwardly while the actor plays the scene naturalistically (the technique's crucial second half, and the reason it works on camera, A-7: the gesture is inside the stillness); the PG for the whole role, and smaller PGs for individual scenes or objectives (A-2's want, given a body). The imaginary body: the actor imagines a body different from their own — taller, heavier, older, with longer arms — and steps into it, so that the character's physical life is inhabited rather than acted; combined with the IMAGINARY CENTRE (the point in or near the body from which the character moves — the chest for the proud, the belly for the sensual, a point six inches in front of the forehead for the intellectual, a point outside the body for the deranged) — the single most immediately useful tool in this module, and one actors use for decades without telling anyone. Qualities of movement: MOULDING (shaping the air, for the deliberate), FLOATING (for the dreamy), FLYING (for the driven), RADIATING (energy sent outward) and RECEIVING (energy taken in) — the four elements and two directions that give any action a texture; ATMOSPHERE (the space's own emotional quality, which Chekhov taught actors to feel and be changed by — the haunted room, the hospital corridor: the given circumstances (A-2) as a physical experience rather than an intellectual one). Why it complements the American schools: it gives the actor something to DO when feeling won't come (the answer A-3 CH 1 lacked), it works from outside without ever becoming indication (A-1 CH 2) because the gesture is genuinely performed, it protects the actor's biography (A-3 CH 2's ethic — nothing personal is required), and its imaginative basis makes it available for characters utterly unlike the actor (A-8's transformation). The students and the lineage: Monroe, Quinn, Brynner, Eastwood, Hurt and a long line since; the technique's quiet persistence in schools that officially teach something else. The chapter's law: find the gesture that is the character's whole drive, make it fully, then make it invisible — and let the body tell the psyche what to feel.

Two systems for describing what a body is doing, so that an actor can choose it. Laban's efforts: Rudolf Laban analysed movement into MOTION FACTORS — WEIGHT (strong / light), SPACE (direct / indirect), TIME (sudden / sustained), and FLOW (bound / free) — whose combinations produce the EIGHT EFFORTS, each a distinct quality of action: PUNCH (strong, direct, sudden), SLASH (strong, indirect, sudden), PRESS (strong, direct, sustained), WRING (strong, indirect, sustained), DAB (light, direct, sudden), FLICK (light, indirect, sudden), GLIDE (light, direct, sustained) and FLOAT (light, indirect, sustained); the actor's use — a character's DOMINANT effort (this man presses; this woman flicks), the effort CHANGING with the scene's tactic (A-2's verb, now with a physical quality), and the effort played against the text (the gliding delivery of a threat); the vocabulary's value is that it is precise and shareable — a director can say "less wring, more dab" and mean something (D-7's direction of actors, from the actor's side). The kinesphere and levels: the space a body can reach without travelling, and the high, middle and low levels through which movement travels — the spatial thinking that lets an actor compose their own physical life inside a frame (CL-9's composition, from inside it). Viewpoints: Bogart and Landau's ensemble training, derived from Mary Overlie's dance work — the nine physical viewpoints (TEMPO, DURATION, KINESTHETIC RESPONSE, REPETITION, SHAPE, GESTURE, ARCHITECTURE, SPATIAL RELATIONSHIP, TOPOGRAPHY) as things a performer can attend to and adjust in real time; the training (the open viewpoints sessions where an ensemble moves together with no leader, composing by mutual attention), COMPOSITION (the structured making of short pieces from the viewpoints), and the skills it produces — an actor who is spatially aware, responsive to the group, and able to make bold physical choices without deliberation (the A-3 CH 3 attention discipline, in space rather than in dialogue). Why a screen actor needs this: the camera photographs physical specificity (A-7); a character who moves in a particular way is legible in a wide shot where no facial work is available; and a vocabulary makes the physical work repeatable across takes and days — the actor who found it once by luck cannot repeat it, and the one who knows it was a sustained direct press can. The chapter's law: learn a vocabulary for movement so your physical choices can be chosen, repeated and discussed — and train with a group so that the choices are made in relation to other bodies rather than in a mirror.

The neutral mask: a featureless, calm, unexpressive mask (Jacques Copeau's invention, developed by Lecoq at his Paris school) worn in silence — the wearer cannot use their face, their habitual expressions or their voice, and discovers how much of their "acting" was mannerism; the exercises (the fundamental journeys: waking, walking, the sea, the forest, the wall — a body encountering the world for the first time), the ECONOMY it teaches (any unnecessary movement is glaring under a mask), and the VIA NEGATIVA (Lecoq's method of removing rather than adding — the actor stripped of habits before being given a style). The expressive masks: the CHARACTER masks (a face that dictates a body — the actor puts it on, looks in a mirror once, and lets the mask's nature take over: the outside-in principle of CH 1 at its most literal), the COMMEDIA DELL'ARTE masks (Arlecchino, Pantalone, Il Capitano and their fixed physicalities — the European comic tradition whose stock types survive in film comedy, A-9), the LARVAL and BOUFFON work (Lecoq's grotesques), and the half-mask that frees the mouth for speech. Why a screen actor learns mask: because it kills mugging (the face-acting that a close-up punishes — A-7's central discipline, taught here by removing the face entirely), because it builds physical expressivity that survives the wide shot, because it teaches the actor to make one clear choice and commit (the mask does not permit hedging), and because the traditions it opens — commedia, clown, the Asian mask theatres (A-11's Noh, whose masks are the world's most refined) — are the historical foundation of stylised performance. Clown and the red nose: Lecoq's "smallest mask in the world" — the training in which the actor finds their own ridiculous self and shows it without protection; the source of a particular kind of comic screen performance (A-9), and one of the few exercises in this track that requires genuine courage. The lineage: Lecoq's school and its graduates across European and international film and theatre; Copeau's legacy; the mask traditions of Bali, Japan and West Africa (A-11's clause: these are living sacred and theatrical practices, not exercises — the actor who studies them does so with teachers and with respect, not by borrowing a photograph). The chapter's law and the module's: put on the neutral mask and discover how much you do that means nothing — then take it off and keep the economy, because the camera is a mask that shows your face.

A-5 · Year 1 · Module Five

Voice & Speech

The Trailer

Kristin Linklater called her book Freeing the Natural Voice, and the title is the whole argument: the voice an actor needs is not a voice to be built but one to be uncovered — the sound a body makes when the habitual tensions of a lifetime stop interfering with breath. Cicely Berry ran voice at the Royal Shakespeare Company for decades on a related conviction, that voice work is not beautification but the way a text's thought gets into a body. Patsy Rodenburg named the three circles of energy. Between them, three teachers built the modern actor's vocal training, and every one of them started from breath.

This module is the instrument's second half. Breath, resonance and articulation — the mechanics, taught as daily practice rather than theory, because the voice is the one part of the actor that will not improve by reading about it. And accents and dialects — the craft of learning to speak as someone else, the phonetic tools that make it teachable, the coaches who do it professionally, and the ethics the profession has been arguing about for a decade: who may play whom, what a bad accent costs a film, and why "the general accent" — the placeless, classless screen voice that many actors are trained into — is itself a dialect with a politics. The body is the instrument; the breath is the bow.

Two chapters: breath, resonance and articulation — Linklater, Berry, Rodenburg; and accents and dialects — the craft, the coach, and the ethics. Breathe out first.

Chapters

Everything starts with the out-breath. Breath: the diaphragmatic breath released rather than pushed — the tension inventory (A-1's relaxation, in the throat, jaw, tongue root and shoulders where voice dies), the spine's alignment as the breath's architecture, the exhalation that carries sound without force, and the CONNECTION of breath to impulse (Linklater's premise: the breath responds to what the person actually feels, so a freed voice is an honest one — the vocal version of A-1's truth test); the screen actor's particular problem — the whisper and the close-mic (S-3's boom from the actor's side: film permits a smaller sound than the stage, which tempts actors to abandon breath support entirely and produce a devitalised mumble that a mixer can capture but an audience cannot receive). Resonance: the body's chambers — chest, mouth, nasal, head — and the sound's placement in them; the range opened (the pitch a character uses is a characterisation choice, not a fixed fact of the actor), the WARMTH and CARRY a resonant voice has even at conversational volume, and the vocal quality as characterisation (A-8: the voice built for a role — the register lowered, the placement moved, the breath rhythm altered). Articulation: the consonants and vowels made by the lips, tongue, teeth and palate — the MUSCULARITY Berry insisted on (the language physically enjoyed and worked, not merely pronounced), the tongue twisters and articulation drills as daily gym, the CLARITY that lets a whispered line land, and the danger of over-articulation (the actor whose consonants sound like elocution — a specific and common British fault, and an American one in period work). Rodenburg's circles: FIRST CIRCLE (energy inward, self-contained — the withdrawn person; a legitimate character choice and a disastrous default), SECOND CIRCLE (energy in genuine exchange with the other person — the actor's working state, and the vocal equivalent of A-3's repetition), THIRD CIRCLE (energy broadcast generally, at no one — the presentational, the bluster, the audition-room over-projection); the diagnosis is instantly usable, and most vocal problems on camera are a circle problem rather than a technique problem. The practice: fifteen to twenty minutes daily — release, breath, touch of sound, resonance ladder, articulation drill, then a text — every day, for years; the module's honest note that this is the one discipline in the track that cannot be crammed. The chapter's law: release the tension, let the breath drop in and out without force, place the sound in the body, work the consonants like muscle, and stay in second circle — and do it every day, because the voice remembers only what it practised.

The craft: an accent is not an impression but a SYSTEM — the vowel shifts (the accent's signature: which vowels move where), the consonant treatments (rhotic or not; the t's, the th's, the l's), the PLACEMENT (where in the mouth and body the sound sits — the physical feel that makes an accent inhabitable rather than imitated), the INTONATION and rhythm (the music, which is what listeners actually recognise), and the STRESS patterns; the method — a PRIMARY SOURCE (a native speaker recorded at length, ideally of the character's exact region, class and generation), the IPA used to notate what is actually heard (the archives and charts of the First Reel), drilling from the source rather than from a "general Irish" template, and the accent practised until it survives emotion, speed and improvisation (the test: can you argue in it without thinking?). A-6 · Year 1 · Module Six

Scene Study & Script Analysis

The Trailer

David Mamet's Practical Aesthetics, as set out in A Practical Handbook for the Actor by a group of his students, reduces the actor's preparation to four questions. What is the literal situation? What is my character doing — the action, phrased as something you could do to anyone? What is that action LIKE in my own life — the as-if? And then: do it. No emotional memory, no character work, no psychology; find the action, find its personal analogue, and go. It is the most austere position in this track, it makes many actors furious, and it produces work of startling directness — which is why the module ends with it after teaching everything it rejects.

Scene study is where the instrument (A-1), the system (A-2), the methods (A-3), the body (A-4) and the voice (A-5) meet a page of text and have to produce something. This module is the analysis — reading a scene for the action and the event, finding the subtext beneath the line, deciding what the character wants and how the scene turns — and the building of a role across a whole script: the arc, the relationships, the backstory that is useful and the backstory that is a hobby. It is also the module where the track's schools are put in one room and made to work on the same page. Every method ends here, at a scene, with a partner, at ten in the morning.

Chapters

The page is evidence; the actor's job is to infer the behaviour that produced it. The literal first: what factually happens — who is present, what is said, what physically occurs (the Practical Aesthetics discipline, CH 3, and the corrective to actors who interpret before they read); the GIVEN CIRCUMSTANCES gathered from the text (A-2 CH 2) and the gaps listed for invention. The event: what CHANGES in this scene — the thing that means the scene cannot be cut (the Screenwriting track's test, received by the actor): information delivered, a relationship altered, a decision made, a refusal given; the actor plays toward and away from the event, and a scene whose event the actor hasn't identified will be played at one temperature throughout. The action: A-2 CH 1's want and verb, now derived from the page — what does my character do to the other to get what they want, line by line; the discipline that the action is TRANSITIVE (done to someone), PLAYABLE (it can be attempted), and CHANGEABLE (the tactic shifts when resisted). Subtext: the difference between what is said and what is meant or wanted — the line "it's fine" doing the work of an accusation, the small talk conducting a negotiation; the actor locates subtext by asking why this line and not silence, and why now (the Screenwriting track's construction, reverse-engineered); the failure modes — subtext INDICATED (the actor telegraphing the hidden meaning, which destroys it) versus subtext PLAYED (the actor pursuing the real want while saying the surface words, which is the whole art). The turn: the pivot where the scene changes direction (A-2 CH 3's unit boundary) — usually a discovery, an arrival, or the moment a tactic finally lands; the actor's map of a scene is its turns. Shurtleff's guideposts as a toolkit: relationship (who is this person to me, precisely), conflict, the moment before, humour (the scene's, however dark), opposites (the impulse against the chosen behaviour), discoveries (what the character learns in the scene — actors systematically under-play these), the mystery, and the rest — a checklist that reliably turns an inert page into playable material and is the fastest analysis under audition pressure (A-12). Practical marking: the script marked with units, verbs, discoveries, the event and the turn (A-2 CH 3's score, applied to real text), in pencil, revised in rehearsal. The chapter's law: read for what literally happens, identify the event and the turns, choose transitive actions, and play the want beneath the line rather than the meaning on top of it.

Five methods, one scene, and the working actor's answer. Practical Aesthetics: the Mamet-derived discipline — for any scene, answer four questions and stop: WHAT IS LITERALLY HAPPENING (the facts, no interpretation), WHAT DOES MY CHARACTER WANT the other to do, WHAT IS THE ACTION (phrased universally: "to get an old friend to admit a betrayal" — something any human could do), and WHAT IS IT LIKE — the AS-IF, a situation from the actor's own life with the same action, used not to summon feeling but to make the action concrete; then DO IT, with full attention on the partner, and let the character emerge from the doing; the position's refusals (no character work, no emotional preparation, no psychology, no "being" the person) and its claim: acting is doing, the text supplies everything else, and the audience assembles the character. The comparison: one page of text approached five ways — Stanislavski (objectives, units, given circumstances — the fullest analysis), Strasberg (relaxation and sense work, the emotional preparation), Adler (research and imagination, the world of the play), Meisner (minimal analysis, maximal attention to the partner, impulse followed), Chekhov (the gesture and centre found first, the body leading), and Practical Aesthetics (the four questions, then go); each produces a different rehearsal process and a recognisably different performance, and the exercise of doing all five on one scene is the single most clarifying week in this track. The synthesis the profession actually uses: almost no working actor uses one school purely — the common working kit is Stanislavski's analysis (objective, obstacle, action, units), Meisner's attention and impulse, Adler's research and imagination, Chekhov's physical centre, Strasberg's relaxation and sense memory, and Practical Aesthetics' as-if when a scene resists — chosen per scene and per problem (A-8's "golden box"); the professional skill is DIAGNOSIS: knowing which tool a particular difficulty calls for (the scene that feels dead needs a clearer want; the one that feels indicated needs a task; the one that won't start needs a physical action; the one that feels false needs research). The track's position: methods are routes, not religions; the eleven words of A-1 are the standard; and the actor who can move between routes is employable in more rooms than one who cannot. The chapter's law and Year 1's close: learn every school well enough to use it, keep what works on Tuesday at the eleventh take, discard the rest without guilt — and remember that the audience cannot see your method, only whether something is happening.

A-7 · Year 2 · Module Seven

On-Camera Technique

The Trailer

Year 2 opens on daylight stock with a piece of tape on the floor. It is four inches long, it is yours, and if you miss it by two inches the focus puller loses your eyes and the take is gone — no matter what happened in it. Michael Caine made a television series about this, and his advice has outlasted most of the books: don't blink, because blinking makes a character look weak; know which lens is on you, because a 25mm means your gesture is huge and a 100mm means a raised eyebrow is a shout; and if you're in a close-up, do less, then do less than that.

This module is everything the first six did not need to know. The camera is closer than any audience has ever been, it is unforgiving of effort, it photographs thought, and it is surrounded by a machine that requires the actor to repeat a spontaneous moment eleven times from four angles across two days while hitting marks, matching a cigarette's length, and finding an eyeline on a piece of tape stuck to a C-stand where a co-star was yesterday. Everything in Year 1 remains true. This module is how to do it inside the apparatus. The stage actor projects to the last row. The screen actor lets the lens come and find them, and does not move.

Three chapters: scale and truthfulness for the lens — the difference from stage; marks, eyelines and continuity — the apparatus; and working with coverage — the same scene, eleven times. Daylight loaded. Find your mark.

Chapters

The camera changes the size of everything except the truth. The proximity: a close-up puts the audience closer than any human conversation permits, and at that distance effort is visible — the pushed emotion, the planned reaction, the tension in the jaw (A-1 CH 1) all read as falseness; the discipline is DOING LESS, which is not doing nothing: the impulse is full, the expression of it is small, and the difference between an actor who is genuinely thinking and one who is showing thought is the entire art of screen acting. The lens as your scale meter: the focal length (CL-3) determines how much of you is in frame and therefore how large your behaviour can be — on a wide lens in a wide shot your body is the performance (A-4's physical work earns its place); on a long lens in a tight close-up an eye movement is a paragraph; the professional asks the operator or the DP what the size is before every take, and adjusts — not the truth, the SCALE (Caine's rule). What the camera photographs: THOUGHT (the audience reads a face as an interior, and an actor genuinely thinking a specific thought is photographable — the actor thinking nothing is a blank, and the actor thinking "I must look worried" is a liar), LISTENING (the reaction shot is half of screen acting — E-6's reaction shot from the actor's side: receiving the other person visibly and changing because of it), STILLNESS (the screen rewards it as the stage punishes it), and the EYES (the audience's focus; Caine's blink advice is contested folklore worth testing rather than obeying, but the underlying observation — that blinks read as instability and small eye movements read as thought — is sound). The stage-to-screen translation: the projected voice reduced (A-5's support kept, volume dropped), the gesture reduced (the hand that was a semaphore becomes a shift of weight), the timing changed (the stage's pause for an audience's laugh is gone; the screen's pauses are for the cut — E-2's blink), and the energy CONCENTRATED rather than diminished (the commonest failure of stage actors on film is not size but dilution: the actor who has been told to "do less" and has therefore stopped wanting anything). What does not change: the want (A-2), the attention on the partner (A-3 CH 3), the given circumstances, the reality of doing — the camera does not require a different kind of truth, only a different amplitude of behaviour. The monitor: watching your own takes — useful for technical checks (the mark, the eyeline, the continuity), dangerous for performance (the actor who directs themselves from the monitor starts watching themselves, A-1's cardinal fault); the professional's compromise is to check the mechanics and trust the director for the rest. The chapter's law: keep the impulse full and make the expression small, ask what the lens is before every take, think a specific thought and let the camera read it — and never confuse doing less with wanting less.

Marks: the tape or sandbag on the floor where you must be for focus and framing — hit WITHOUT LOOKING (the foot finds the T; peripheral vision, spatial memory, and a landmark at eye level; the professional rehearses the walk until the body knows it), the multiple marks in a moving scene (each one a focus change — CL-4's puller from your side: their job is impossible if you float), the mark you can negotiate (say so in rehearsal, not in take four) and the one you cannot (the doorway where the light is). Someone else's body and voice, built deliberately. The vocal decisions: the PITCH and placement (A-5 CH 1 — the register chosen for the character, not inherited from the actor), the BREATH rhythm (short and high for the anxious; low and slow for the settled — the fastest characterisation available and the least used), the TEMPO and rhythm of speech, the ARTICULATION quality (precise, lazy, effortful), the accent (A-5 CH 2), and the VOCAL HABITS (the sound made while thinking, the laugh — invented and kept consistent). The body changed, and its limits: weight gain and loss, extreme training, the physical demands some roles make — and the module's clear position: these are legitimate professional choices with real medical costs, they are increasingly replaced by prosthetics (PD-8) and by the recognition that the audience cannot tell the difference, and no production has the right to require them; the actor's health is not a resource the film owns. The test of a transformation: not whether it is unrecognisable but whether it is CONSISTENT and BEHAVIOURAL — the great transformations are built from a hundred small decided facts that never vary, not from a prosthetic nose; the audience believes a person because they behave like one person, always. The chapter's law: choose the walk, the weight, the breath and the pitch deliberately; build them with the departments and the coaches; keep them absolutely consistent — and never let a production ask your body for something it cannot safely give.

A-9 · Year 2 · Module Nine

Genre & Style Acting

The Trailer

Edmund Gwenn is supposed to have said, on his deathbed, that dying is easy and comedy is hard. The line has been attributed to half a dozen actors and the track flags it as unattributable, but no one in the profession disputes the sentiment. Comedy is the most technical acting there is — a matter of rhythm measured in frames, of absolute commitment to a reality the audience finds absurd, and of never once acknowledging that anything is funny. It is also the genre in which a single actor's timing can be the difference between a hit and a corpse.

This module is style: the recognition that "truthful" (A-1 CH 2) does not mean "naturalistic," and that a performer must be able to find the truth inside a form — a screwball comedy's tempo, a Shakespearean line's thought, a period's manners, a fight's choreography, a motion-capture volume where the actor plays a creature in a grey suit surrounded by cameras. Each has rules the actor must learn as a musician learns a genre, and each punishes the performer who plays it "realistically" without understanding what the form requires. Truth is the standard. The style is the instrument it is played on.

Four chapters: comedy — the most technical acting; heightened and classical text; period, manners and the world's codes; and action, physical acting and performance capture. Commit absolutely.

Chapters

Funny is a mechanism, and mechanisms can be learned. The first law: the character is never trying to be funny — the comic performance is played with total seriousness from inside the character's logic, and the audience laughs at the gap between that logic and reality; the actor who signals the joke has killed it (A-1 CH 2's indicating, in its most lethal form). Timing: the pause before the line, the speed of the reply, the beat held one frame too long — comedy is measured in frames (E-6's comedic timing from the editor's chair; the actor supplies the material and the rhythm, the cut finishes it), and the actor's craft is the sense of tempo that makes a line land: the SETUP delivered cleanly (no signalling), the TURN on the operative word, the pause that lets the audience get there first (and the discipline of not filling it). Commitment: absolute physical and emotional investment in an absurd situation — the higher the stakes the character believes in, the funnier the absurdity; comedy fails from under-commitment far more often than from over-playing. Status: Johnstone's lens — every comic scene is a status transaction (the high-status person humiliated, the low-status person elevated, the see-saw where both move), and playing status deliberately is the fastest route to comic behaviour; the STRAIGHT MAN (the one who plays it real and reacts — the harder job, and the one that makes the comic funny) and the double act's mechanics. The forms: SCREWBALL (overlapping dialogue at speed — the tempo itself is the comedy; a technical skill requiring precise cue pickup), DEADPAN (the affect withheld — the reaction that isn't), FARCE (escalating physical logic played with mortal seriousness), SATIRE (the character who believes what the film mocks), CRINGE (the modern mode — the camera held on a mistake; the actor's discipline is not to apologise for the character), PHYSICAL comedy (A-4's mask and clown training paying off — the fall, the business, the object that betrays), and IMPROVISATION-based comedy (the alt take; the actor who can generate options for the editor — a specific and highly employable skill). The rule of three, the callback, the escalation — the structures the actor plays inside and must recognise in a script. Comedy for the camera: the scale discipline (A-7) applies doubly — a face doing less is funnier; the ensemble's shared tempo; and the alt takes given to the director with variations (P-8's editor will thank you). The chapter's law: play the character's logic with total seriousness, find the rhythm and trust the pause, know your status in every moment — and never, ever let the audience see you find it funny.

Language that is bigger than conversation, played as thought. The principle: heightened text — Shakespeare, the Jacobeans, Greek tragedy, Mamet's stylised rhythms, Sorkin's arias, the formal registers of many world cinemas — is not "poetic speaking"; it is a character THINKING in a form, and the actor's job is to make the thought happen now, on the line, in front of us (Barton's central teaching). Verse as thinking: the IAMBIC line as a unit of thought (the actor breathes at the thought's end, not at the line's — or at the line's when the line is the thought), the ANTITHESIS (the balanced opposition that structures Elizabethan thought: "not X, but Y" — find it and the sense plays itself), the OPERATIVE WORD (the one word the line turns on — one per line, not five), the CAESURA and the line ending (the tiny lift that keeps the verse's shape without sing-song), the SHARED LINE (two characters completing one iambic line — a cue pickup written into the text), the switch between VERSE and PROSE (the character's register changing — always meaningful), and the IMAGE (the picture the character sees as they speak it — the cure for generalised declamation). The argument: a Shakespearean speech is usually a person reasoning toward a decision — the actor tracks the argument's steps and plays each one's discovery rather than delivering a known conclusion (A-6 CH 1's discoveries, at maximum density); the soliloquy is thought made public, and its modern screen problem is where to look (the lens, the space, the self — a directorial decision the actor must clarify). Speaking it on camera: A-7's scale applied to big language — the size of the thought is large and the physical expression may be very small (the close-up soliloquy, whispered, is entirely viable and often better than the shouted one); the breath support (A-5) that lets a long verse sentence arrive intact; and the diction that serves clarity without elocution. Beyond Shakespeare: Greek tragedy's chorus and scale, Racine's alexandrines, the heightened registers of Sanskrit and Japanese classical drama (A-11), Mamet's and Pinter's rhythmic prose (the pause as notation — play the rhythm as written; it is not optional), Sorkin's rhetorical velocity, and the musical-theatre-derived screen forms. The trap: BEAUTIFUL SPEAKING — the actor in love with their own sound, playing music instead of meaning, which every teacher in this chapter's tradition spends their career fighting. The chapter's law: find the thought, find the antithesis, find the operative word, see the image, and let the form carry a person reasoning in front of us — never let it become singing.

Advanced Scene Work

The Trailer

Until about 2015 there was no one on a film set whose job was to choreograph a sex scene. There were stunt coordinators for a punch, armourers for a gun, wranglers for a horse — and for the most exposing thing an actor can be asked to do, there was a closed set and a director's improvisation. A-11 · Year 2 · Module Eleven

Global Performance Traditions

The Trailer

A Noh actor wears a mask that cannot change expression, and the tradition's teaching is that by tilting the head a few degrees — TERASU, to brighten; KUMORASU, to cloud — the mask appears to weep or to smile. The performer is not indicating; the audience is completing. It is the oldest and most exact demonstration of a principle this track has argued since A-1: that truth in performance is not the same thing as naturalism, and that the audience's participation is part of the technique. Western screen-acting pedagogy, which mostly stops at Stanislavski's heirs, has almost nothing to say about the majority of the world's performance traditions.

This module is the correction. The stylised registers of Japanese and Indian screen performance, which are not failures of realism but complete systems with their own rules. The long argument between melodrama and naturalism, which every cinema has had and which the realist consensus of the English-speaking world has mostly declared itself the winner of. The tradition of the non-professional — Neorealism and the Iranian cinema that built a body of the century's greatest films largely without trained actors. And the influence of Kabuki and Noh on screen performance, in Japan and far beyond it. Your acting has a passport. This module stamps it in Tokyo, Bombay, Tehran and Rome.

Japan: the screen inheritance of Kabuki and Noh (CH 4) and of shingeki (the modern realist theatre movement that imported Stanislavski) — a spectrum rather than a style: the JIDAIGEKI's heightened register (the period film's declamatory power, the samurai's formality — Mifune's explosive physicality for Kurosawa as a deliberately theatrical construction), the SHOMINGEKI's extreme restraint (Ozu's actors — A-1's Ryū and Hara — working in a register of stillness, fixed framing and minimal inflection that Western critics initially read as "wooden" and that is in fact an exacting discipline), the MELODRAMATIC register of Mizoguchi's and Naruse's women's films (CH 2), and the modern naturalism of Kore-eda and his contemporaries; the technical features — the tolerance of held stillness, the significance of small gestural changes, the vocal formality, and the DIRECTOR's authority over performance in a tradition where actors often served long studio contracts (P-11 CH 1). The judgment: within each register, the same standard applies — is something actually happening (A-1 CH 2), or is a result being indicated? The chapter's law: learn what contract a tradition's performance has with its audience before judging its scale — and be able to play at least two registers, because the world's screens use more than one.

The argument every cinema has had, and the mode the English-speaking industry taught you to despise. Melodrama defined properly: not "overacting" but a MODE — a dramaturgy of moral clarity, heightened emotion, coincidence and expressive excess, in which feeling is externalised rather than concealed and the audience is invited to weep rather than to observe; its history (the nineteenth-century stage, the silent cinema's foundation, the studio-era woman's picture, the Mexican and Egyptian golden ages, the Indian family film, the Korean and Japanese traditions, the telenovela, the K-drama) makes it the world's majority screen mode by a distance. The performance grammar: emotion PLAYED OUT rather than in (the tears shown, the collapse enacted), the scale calibrated to the mode's own conventions (which are as specific as a screwball's tempo — A-9 CH 1), the moral position legible, the address partly to the audience, and the physical vocabulary (the turn away, the hand to the mouth, the held gaze) that a competent melodramatic performer deploys with as much precision as a naturalist deploys stillness. The naturalist consensus: the English-language industry's post-war default (the Method's generation, the 1960s and '70s realism, the current international art cinema's restraint) and its assumption that concealed feeling is truer than expressed feeling — an aesthetic preference that has been widely mistaken for a standard of truth; the scholarship's correction (the mode's dismissal as "feminine," sentimental and low, and its reappraisal by critics who took it seriously). The actor's practical position: you must be able to play BOTH — the register is chosen by the film, and the performer who can only conceal is unemployable in half the world's production; the technique for melodrama is the same as for anything else (the want, the circumstances, the partner — A-1 CH 2's standard applies exactly) but the EXPRESSION is scaled up and made legible, and the failure mode is not "too big" but "not felt" — a full-scale melodramatic performance that is truthfully wanted is devastating, and the same performance indicated is unwatchable. The hybrid: the modern films that use both (the family drama that plays restraint until the scene that breaks into full expression — Indian, Korean and Latin American cinemas do this constantly), and the actor's skill of moving between them within one film. The exemplars: Douglas Sirk's cast working in a register the 1950s dismissed and the 1970s canonised; Mexico's and Egypt's golden-age stars; Korea's contemporary melodrama; the Indian family film's grand emotional architecture. The chapter's law: melodrama is a mode with a grammar, not a failure of restraint — learn to play it fully and truthfully, because more of the world's audiences weep than observe.

The Business of Acting

The Trailer

The self-tape changed the profession more than any technique since sound. Ten years ago an actor auditioned in a room, with a reader, in front of a casting director who could see them breathe; now the majority of first-round casting for screen happens in a bedroom with a ring light and a phone, judged on a file among four hundred files. The change democratised access for actors far from the industry's cities and simultaneously transferred the cost, the labour and the technical burden onto the performer — a shift the guilds have been negotiating over ever since. It is also, simply, the job: an actor who cannot self-tape well is an actor who does not work.

This final module is the profession the previous eleven trained you for. The audition, in the room and on tape, and cold reading as a skill in itself. The reel, the agents and managers, the casting process seen from the other side of the table. And the career: the conduct on set that gets you hired again, the money and the contracts, the rejection arithmetic that has ended more careers than any lack of talent, and the sustainable working life — the one that is still there at fifty. The track has taught you to live truthfully under imaginary circumstances. This module is about the real ones. The work is the acting. The job is everything else.

Three chapters: auditioning, self-tapes and cold reading; the reel, agents and casting; and on-set conduct and the sustainable career. Roll credits. Then send the tape.

Chapters

The skill that gets you the job is not the same as the skill that does it. What an audition is deciding: not "can this person act" (usually assumed from the reel and the credits) but "is this person THIS character, and are they someone we can work with for eleven weeks" — the casting director is your advocate, not your obstacle, and their job is to bring the director good options; the room's brevity (you are one of many; the decision is often made in the first ten seconds of behaviour) and the corollary — a definite, specific CHOICE beats a safe, correct reading every time, because a choice can be redirected and blandness cannot. The preparation: the sides (the scene pages) analysed fast with A-6's tools — the literal, the want, the relationship, the moment before, one discovery, one turn (Shurtleff's guideposts as a three-minute triage); the CHOICE made and committed to; the memorisation (as much as time allows — enough to lift your eyes off the page, not necessarily perfect), and the questions you may ask (one, brief, about something you genuinely cannot infer). The self-tape: the technical minimums (a clean neutral background, the camera at eye height framed for a medium close-up, soft even light on your face — CL-12's principles in a bedroom, good SOUND above all (S-3: bad audio loses more tapes than bad acting), the phone in landscape or as specified, the file named exactly as instructed and delivered on time in the format asked for — the instructions are a competence test as much as the acting is), the READER (a real human who can act a little, off-camera, close to the lens; a bored flatmate reading badly can cost you the job), the SLATE (name, height if asked, agent — brief and yourself, not a performance), the number of takes (choose one, or two if genuinely different — sending six says you don't know which is best), and the DISCIPLINE of not editing yourself into perfection: the tape should be alive, not clean. Cold reading: the script seen minutes before — the skills (scanning for who wants what, keeping a finger on the line and the eyes up, playing the partner rather than the page, letting the first read be a genuine first read: a character discovering the scene as you are), and the truth that cold reading is a separate trainable skill that improves with reps. The redirect: the note given in the room — the only real information about whether you are a person the director can work with; take it fully, change something actual, and do not apologise or explain. The aftermath: forget it (the rejection arithmetic of CH 3), never ask why, and be the same person in every subsequent room. The chapter's law: make a specific choice, get the technical floor right so the tape is about the acting, play the reader rather than the page, take the redirect fully — and then let it go.
