CC-1 · Module One

Global Film History - Chronological + Polycentric

The Trailer

Paris, December 1895. The legend says that when the Lumière brothers projected a train pulling into La Ciotat station, the audience screamed and dove under their seats. It almost certainly never happened - but the legend survived because it tells the truth anyway: moving images arrived like something that could run you over.

A few months later, Georges Méliès - a stage magician who'd begged the Lumières to sell him a camera (their father refused: "the cinema is an invention without a future") - was filming traffic at the Place de l'Opéra when his camera jammed. When he developed the strip, a bus had transformed into a hearse. Cinema's first special effect was an accident, and a magician was the first to understand what it meant: the cut is a spell.

This module is that story told properly - not as one river flowing out of Hollywood, but as a delta. In 1920s Tokyo, the biggest star in the movie palace often wasn't on the screen but standing beside it. In 1955 Calcutta, an adman pawned his wife's jewellery to finish a film about a boy in a village, and world cinema tilted on its axis. In 1992 Lagos, a trader stuck with a container of blank VHS tapes invented one of the largest film industries on earth just to have something to record onto them. Godard said cinema is truth twenty-four times a second; Brian De Palma answered that the camera lies twenty-four times a second. Both are right, and the history of film is the history of that argument - fought on every continent at once.

Chapters

It starts with a bet. 1878, Palo Alto: railroad baron Leland Stanford wants to know whether a galloping horse ever has all four hooves off the ground. Eadweard Muybridge rigs twelve cameras with tripwires and settles it - yes - and accidentally invents the frame-by-frame analysis of motion. From there, two rival futures: Edison's Kinetoscope, a peepshow for one paying customer at a time, versus the Lumières' projection - cinema as a room full of strangers gasping together. Projection won, and the medium has been communal ever since. Early cinema wasn't primitive storytelling; scholar Tom Gunning calls it a "cinema of attractions" - a fairground of showing, astonishing, and direct address, closer to a magic act than a novel.

In 1920s Japan, the marquee often carried the narrator's name above the film's. The benshi stood beside the screen and performed setsumei - narration, dialogue, commentary, all voices at once - and audiences chose theatres by benshi the way we choose films by director. Top benshi out-earned the actors on screen. This wasn't a workaround for missing sound; it was a different theory of cinema, descended from kabuki and bunraku narration, where telling and showing are partners. When talkies arrived, the benshi didn't fade quietly - they went on strike in 1932. They lost, but their legacy shaped a national cinema unusually comfortable with narration, presentation, and direct address, and it explains textures in Japanese film you can feel all the way to Ozu and beyond.

Three countries, three answers to the same question: how do shots make meaning? The American answer - continuity - hides the seams so the story flows like a dream. The German answer puts the meaning inside the frame: in Caligari the shadows are painted onto the sets, because the world itself is a diseased mind. The Soviet answer says meaning lives between shots: Kuleshov cut the same expressionless face against soup, a coffin, a child - and audiences praised the actor's subtle hunger, grief, tenderness. Eisenstein turned that into a theory of collision: 1 + 1 = 3. And a required honesty: D.W. Griffith's The Birth of a Nation is simultaneously a foundational grammar textbook and a racist atrocity that revived the Klan. This school teaches both truths at full volume - the craft lesson and the harm - because sanitizing either one is a lie.

"Wait a minute, wait a minute - you ain't heard nothin' yet." Al Jolson ad-libs in The Jazz Singer (1927) and an art form panics. Cameras get locked in soundproof booths; the fluid silent camera freezes; careers end over voices ("Garbo talks!" was a marketing event). Chaplin holds out for another decade in open defiance. And sound nationalizes cinema: the silent film traveled with new intertitles, but a talkie speaks one language, so industries bloom everywhere at once - Hollywood's factory reaches its imperial phase, Japan's Shochiku and Toho build their own studio worlds, Shanghai enters a golden age, and in Bombay, Alam Ara (1931) arrives with seven songs and invents the Indian sound film as a musical form from day one. The studio system - everywhere - is the great paradox of this chapter: an assembly line that kept producing art.

Rome, 1945. The studios are rubble, film stock is scrounged, so Rossellini shoots Rome, Open City in the actual streets with whoever is alive and available - and accidentally proves that cinema doesn't need the factory. Bazin builds a theory on it: reality itself, respected, is the style. Fourteen years later a critic who had been literally banned from Cannes for savaging French "quality" cinema returns with The 400 Blows and wins Best Director - François Truffaut, aged 27. His friend Godard cuts Breathless with jump cuts because the film was too long and the rules were boring. The rupture goes global: Oshima torches the Japanese studio film; twenty-six Germans sign a manifesto declaring "Papa's cinema is dead"; and in Hollywood, Easy Rider - made for pocket change - earns a fortune and the studio gates fall to the film-school generation. One lesson, every continent: when the system breaks, the form leaps.

Two rivers from one source. 1955: Satyajit Ray - a Calcutta ad-man shooting on weekends, funds so short that his wife Bijoya's jewellery was pawned - finishes Pather Panchali after the West Bengal government stepped in with money (legend says the grant was filed under road improvement, since the title translates to "Song of the Little Road"; the filing story is likely embroidered, the state funding is fact). It wins at Cannes, and Indian art cinema has its founding text. It also has its founding controversy: actress-turned-MP Nargis later accused Ray of "exporting India's poverty." Meanwhile the other river runs at full flood: the masala film - Mughal-e-Azam a decade in the making, Sholay running five straight years at Bombay's Minerva - with its own architecture: the interval as structural hinge, the song as narration, emotion as spectacle. FTII and the NFDC seed a "parallel cinema" (Benegal's Ankur, Mrinal Sen's Bhuvan Shome), while the southern industries - Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam - build parallel giants that the word "regional" insults. India is not one film culture; it is several, arguing beautifully.

Censorship is supposed to kill cinema. In Iran it did something stranger: it bred a form. Forbidden romance, restricted politics, mandated modesty - so filmmakers turned to children, villages, dust roads, and allegory, and built one of the most morally serious cinemas on earth. The wellspring predates the revolution - Mehrjui's The Cow (1969) - but the flowering is Kiarostami's Koker films and then Close-Up (1990), where a real man who impersonated a famous director is tried in a real court, re-enacting his own story, with the real judge - fiction and documentary collapsing into each other until the distinction stops mattering. Werner Herzog called it the greatest documentary on filmmaking; it might also be the greatest fiction. Iranian cinema's gift to the world is a question: where exactly does the truth of a film live?

Ousmane Sembène worked the docks of Marseille and wrote novels in French before he did the arithmetic: his own people couldn't read the language he wrote in, but everyone watches films. So he trained in Moscow, came home to Senegal, and made Black Girl (1966) - often called sub-Saharan Africa's first feature by an African director - announcing a cinema that would speak for itself rather than be spoken about. FESPACO in Ouagadougou became its parliament; Cissé's Yeelen and Sissako's later work its high poetry. Then the other origin story: Lagos, 1992. Trader Kenneth Nnebue is stuck with a container of blank VHS cassettes. To make them sellable, he produces a film to record onto them - Living in Bondage - straight to video. From that surplus, Nollywood: now producing on the order of two thousand films a year, one of the largest industries on earth by volume, built on speed, direct-to-consumer distribution, and stories its audience actually asked for. Two African cinemas, then: one born of liberation, one born of commerce - and the future belongs to their conversation.

CC-2 · Module Two

Film Theory & Criticism

The Trailer

November 1958. André Bazin - the gentle, stammering critic who co-founded Cahiers du Cinéma and practically adopted a delinquent teenager named François Truffaut - dies of leukemia at forty. It is one day after Truffaut began shooting The 400 Blows. The film that opens the New Wave is dedicated to him. Theory and practice have never been closer than that.

Theory has a reputation: dusty, bloodless, allergic to pleasure. The record says otherwise. Truffaut wrote "A Certain Tendency of the French Cinema" as a polemic so vicious that respectable French cinema never fully recovered. When Andrew Sarris imported auteur theory to America, Pauline Kael answered with "Circles and Squares" - a takedown so personal the two feuded for decades. In 1975 Laura Mulvey published sixteen pages - "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema" - and detonated them under the entire history of how movies look at women; she said later she wrote it "as a manifesto, without nuance," which is exactly why it worked. And two millennia before any of them, the Natyashastra was already theorizing what an audience feels and why - rasa - proving the West arrived very late to a very old conversation.

Theory is not the opposite of loving films. It is what loving films looks like when it starts asking questions - and every fight in this module is still going.

Chapters

The first great fight. The formalists - Eisenstein, Arnheim - say cinema becomes art precisely where it differs from reality: the cut, the frame, the manipulation. Eisenstein wanted montage to work on an audience "like a tractor ploughing a field." The realists - Bazin, Kracauer - answer that the photographic image has a unique covenant with the real, and the filmmaker's job is to honor it: deep focus, the long take, the ambiguity of the world left intact. Bazin went as far as declaring that in certain scenes - where the meaning depends on two things sharing real space - "montage is forbidden." Neither side won. Every shot you will ever design is a vote in this election.

The idea that the director is a film's "author" seems obvious now; it was once a bomb. Truffaut's 1954 polemic attacked France's "tradition of quality" - scriptwriters' cinema - and elevated the metteur-en-scène who imposes a personal vision even inside the studio machine. The Cahiers critics scandalously canonized Hollywood contract directors: Hitchcock and Hawks as artists, not entertainers. Andrew Sarris systematized it for America ("Notes on the Auteur Theory in 1962"); Pauline Kael torched his three concentric circles in "Circles and Squares," calling the fetish for directorial personality a boys' club that excused bad films by favorites. Both were partly right. The mature position: authorship is real but distributed - and, as later critics noted, the auteur is also a brand that studios happily sell back to you. Use the theory as a lens, never a religion.

In the 1960s–70s, theory got systematic. Christian Metz asked whether cinema is a language and built the grand syntagmatique to find out; psychoanalytic critics recast the cinema as a dream-machine and the spectator as its subject. Then Mulvey's 1975 essay fused the two into a weapon: classical cinema, she argued, is built around a male gaze - women positioned as spectacle ("to-be-looked-at-ness"), men as the bearers of the look - and she called for a counter-cinema that would destroy that pleasure to expose it. The essay has been qualified, extended, and fought for fifty years - most importantly by bell hooks' "The Oppositional Gaze," which pointed out that Mulvey's spectator was implicitly white and theorized the Black female viewer's critical look. The gaze debate is not settled; it's the live wire under every casting choice, costume, and camera placement you will ever make.

A genre is a contract: the audience brings expectations, the film decides which to honor and which to betray - and the meaning lives in the gap. Genre theory tracks how these contracts form (the western's frontier myth, the musical's utopian numbers), how they evolve (classical → revisionist → parody), and how they travel: Seven Samurai becomes The Magnificent Seven; the western goes to Italy and comes back as opera, goes to Bombay and comes back as Sholay - the "curry western" - with songs and an interval. Learn to read a genre's history and you can hear an entire culture thinking out loud.

Two aesthetics, two ethics. The montage tradition locates cinema's power in the cut - meaning assembled, imposed, argued. The mise-en-scène tradition locates it in the shot - space, staging, duration, the viewer's freedom to look. The old Cahiers vs. Positif battles were fought on this line, and Bazin's "montage is forbidden" is its realist extreme. Then apparatus theory (Baudry) raised the stakes: never mind the individual film - the machine itself is ideological. The darkened room, the immobilized spectator, the projector behind your head: Plato's cave, industrialized. You don't have to buy the whole argument (many don't) to feel its force the next time the lights go down and you forget you have a body.

Western theory kept asking what a film means. Two older traditions asked what a film does to you. The Natyashastra - Sanskrit performance theory, roughly two thousand years old - built an entire system on rasa: the flavors of emotion (love, heroism, pathos, terror…) that art induces in the spectator. Read Indian melodrama through rasa and it stops being "excess" and becomes precision engineering of feeling - Ghatak's films are rasa machines, and the song sequence is a rasa delivery system. Japan offers mono no aware - the tender ache of transience - and ma, the charged emptiness between things: Ozu's "pillow shots" of hallways and clotheslines are ma given screen time. Paul Schrader mapped part of this territory as "transcendental style." The point is not exotic garnish: these are complete, rigorous theories of the audience - and they were there first.

CC-3 · Module Three

Visual Storytelling & Film Language

The Trailer

Hitchcock put it as a rule: the size of an object in the frame should equal its importance in the story at that moment. Scorsese put it as a koan: cinema is a matter of what's in the frame and what's out. Between those two sentences sits everything this module teaches.

Film language is the grammar nobody voted on. The 180-degree line is an invisible fence that keeps two actors facing each other across a cut - cross it carelessly and the audience's stomach lurches without knowing why. And yet Ozu shot on the "wrong" side of that line for an entire career, cutting through 360 degrees of space, and no one in Japan ever got lost - proof that grammar is convention, not physics. Kuleshov proved the deeper magic a century ago: the same neutral face, intercut with soup, a coffin, a child, and audiences swore they saw hunger, grief, tenderness. The meaning wasn't in the shot. It was in you.

Learn this grammar cold - then learn exactly how the masters break it on purpose. Fluency first, poetry second.

The atoms. Shot size is emotional distance: a wide shot observes, a close-up confides, an extreme close-up interrogates. Angle is power: look up at a character and they grow. Continuity editing is a magic trick performed at feature length: hundreds of cuts, and the audience experiences smooth, continuous action. Sound is half the picture, and the half most students learn last. Michel Chion's key idea is added value: sound doesn't accompany the image, it transforms it - the same shot of a face reads differently over birdsong, traffic, or a heartbeat, and the audience credits the image for the change. Synchresis is the weld: any sound and image occurring together fuse into one event (which is why a melon hit with a hammer can be a punch). Then there's the acousmatic - sound whose source you can't see - cinema's oldest suspense device: the voice of the unseen. Train yourself with Chion's exercise: watch a scene with sound off, then picture off, then together. The three scenes you experience will be different films.

CC-4 · Module Four

Film Analysis Practicum

The Trailer

For decades, Roger Ebert ran a ritual at the University of Colorado at Boulder he called "democracy in the dark." A packed hall, one film, one week - and anyone, anyone at all, could yell "Stop!" The projection froze, and the room argued about the frame on screen until it had given up its secrets. They once spent the whole week inside Citizen Kane and didn't reach the end. Ebert's conclusion after years of this: a film reveals itself completely to anyone willing to actually look - one shot at a time.

His other rule is this module's motto: "A movie is not about what it is about. It is about how it is about it." Anyone can summarize a plot. Analysis is the harder, better thing - noticing that the camera never once moves in a scene, and asking why; noticing whose face the cut favors in an argument; catching the color that has quietly followed a character for ninety minutes. Kubrick claimed a film should be "more like music than fiction" - a progression of moods and feelings, theme arriving after. Analysis is learning to hear that music with your eyes open.

This module has no new theory. It has a chair, a screen, a notebook, and the discipline of stopping the frame. Yell stop.

Chapters

The method: choose a sequence (two to five minutes, not more), and log every shot - size, angle, movement, duration, what the light does, where the cut lands, what the sound is doing against the picture. A sequence analyzed this way stops being a story and becomes a set of decisions, each of which could have been made differently. Professional Foundations

The Trailer

Orson Welles, arriving at RKO at twenty-four with a contract no one has gotten since, looked around the studio and said it was "the biggest electric train set a boy ever had." True - and incomplete. Somebody builds the train set. Somebody rigs it, powers it, insures it, schedules it, feeds it at six a.m., and gets everyone home safe. This module is about them, which means it is about you, because at this school everyone crews for everyone.

The set even talks in riddles until you learn the tongue: the gaffer (chief lighting technician - the name likely from "gaffer" as old slang for a foreman or grandfather) runs the electricians; the best boy is the gaffer's second, and no, nobody fully agrees where that name came from either. In Day for Night - Truffaut playing a director in the best film about filmmaking ever made - the director confesses: making a film is like a stagecoach journey; at first you hope for a pleasant trip, and soon you merely hope to reach the destination. And there is a darker thread that this module refuses to whisper about: sets have killed people - the Twilight Zone helicopter in 1982, the Rust shooting in 2021 - almost always where hierarchy, hurry, and silence lined up. Professionalism is not etiquette. It is the load-bearing wall.

Learn who does what, what everything costs, who owns what, and how a film reaches the world - and you will never again be the person on set everyone works around.

The rule stated plainly at the top of every safety document worth reading: no shot is worth a life. The industry learned it in blood - the 1982 Twilight Zone: The Movie helicopter crash that killed Vic Morrow and two children (and led to prosecutions and reforms), the 2021 Rust shooting - and each disaster traces to the same pattern: fatigue, hierarchy that punished speaking up, and shortcuts under schedule pressure. Two ghost stories every filmmaker should know. First: Nosferatu (1922) was an unlicensed Dracula adaptation; Bram Stoker's widow sued, and a German court ordered every print destroyed. The film survives only because copies had already escaped abroad - a masterpiece that exists today by bootleg. Second: Night of the Living Dead (1968) lost its copyright because the distributor changed the title card and omitted the required copyright notice - the film fell straight into the public domain, and Romero watched the most influential horror film of its era earn fortunes for everyone but its makers. (A gentler cousin: a lapsed renewal is a big part of why It's a Wonderful Life aired endlessly on TV and became a beloved staple.) The lessons compress to: own what you make, license what you don't, and document everything. Chain of title - the paper trail proving you hold every right in the film (script, music, locations, faces, logos) - is what any distributor or festival lawyer will demand before your film goes anywhere. Releases, option agreements, work-for-hire clauses, music sync licenses: unglamorous paper, and the difference between a career and a cautionary tale.
