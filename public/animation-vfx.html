AV-1 · Year 1 · Module One

History & Principles of Animation

The Trailer

In 1981 two of Disney's Nine Old Men, Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston, published The Illusion of Life and set down twelve principles their studio had worked out empirically in the 1930s — squash and stretch, anticipation, staging, straight-ahead and pose-to-pose, follow-through and overlapping action, slow in and slow out, arcs, secondary action, timing, exaggeration, solid drawing, appeal. The principles were derived from drawing on paper at twenty-four frames a second. They apply without modification to a rig in Maya, a puppet on a stop-motion stage, a particle simulation in Houdini and a digital creature in a live-action plate, which is the single best argument for why this track teaches traditional and computer techniques in one building.

This module is the principles and where they came from. The twelve, taught as physics and psychology rather than as a list to memorise — why an audience believes a weight it has never felt, and why a character who moves in perfect linear interpolation looks dead. And the history: the optical toys that proved persistence of vision was the wrong explanation, the cel era's industrialisation, Disney's naturalism and its costs, the Japanese tradition that answered it with limited animation and became the world's largest, the Eastern European schools that rejected both, and the computer's arrival as a new instrument played by people who had to learn all of this first. The tool changes every decade. Weight, timing and appeal do not.

Three chapters: the twelve principles — physics, psychology and the illusion; from optical toys to the cel era and the studio system; and the world's traditions — Disney, anime, Eastern Europe and the computer. Tungsten loaded. Bounce a ball.

Chapters

Twelve observations about how an audience believes a drawing. The mechanism first: animation is not "persistence of vision" (the Victorian explanation, long discredited) but a combination of the PHI PHENOMENON and short-range apparent motion — the visual system constructing motion from discrete samples; the consequence that matters is that the audience is doing the work, and the animator's job is to give them enough to construct something they will believe. SQUASH AND STRETCH: the deformation that communicates rigidity and weight — the ball that flattens on impact is rubber, the one that does not is a bowling ball; the rule of CONSTANT VOLUME (squash wider as you flatten, or the object appears to shrink), and its universal application from cel to rig to simulation. ANTICIPATION: the movement backward before the movement forward — the wind-up, the crouch before the jump; it prepares the audience's eye and it is how a body actually generates force, so it reads as both clarity and physics. STAGING: the presentation of an idea so it is unmistakable — the silhouette test (can you read the pose in black?), one idea at a time, the camera and composition serving the action (CL-9's composition, from the animator's side). STRAIGHT-AHEAD versus POSE-TO-POSE: the two working methods — drawing frame after frame into the unknown (spontaneity, wildness, drift) versus planning key poses and filling BREAKDOWNS and INBETWEENS (control, planning, potential stiffness); most work is both, and the choice is the shot's. FOLLOW-THROUGH and OVERLAPPING ACTION: nothing stops at once — the coat continues after the body, the hand arrives after the arm; DRAG and the OFFSET of parts, which is what separates a live figure from a mannequin. SLOW IN and SLOW OUT: the spacing of drawings clustered at the extremes — the EASE that is the difference between mechanical and organic, and the single principle most directly inherited by the computer (the F-curve, the Bézier handle — AV-5's interpolation is this principle, automated and therefore easy to abuse). ARCS: living things move on curves; the straight-line move is the mark of the machine and of the beginner. SECONDARY ACTION: the gesture that supports the main one without competing (the character walks and wipes their eye), which enriches rather than distracts. TIMING: the number of frames — the principle that governs weight, mood and comedy; a move in six frames and the same move in twelve are different characters (AV-3 CH 3's whole subject). EXAGGERATION: the caricature of truth rather than its distortion — pushing the essence of an action further than reality so that it reads (the principle the medium exists for). SOLID DRAWING: volume, weight and dimension maintained across drawings — in 3D, the rig's equivalent is avoiding the shapes the model cannot make. APPEAL: not prettiness but the quality that holds an eye — clarity of design, interesting shapes, the charisma of a character's construction (AV-2 CH 2). Beyond the twelve: the principles as Disney's naturalistic house rules, and what other traditions add or reject (CH 3 — anime's held pose and smear; the abstract animator's rejection of the whole framework; the limited-animation economies that made different principles out of necessity). The chapter's law: learn the twelve as observations about how bodies move and how eyes believe, apply them in whatever software you are holding — and know that they describe one tradition's answers to universal problems, not the only ones.

How drawings became an industry. Before film: the optical toys — the THAUMATROPE (1825), the PHENAKISTISCOPE (Plateau, 1832), the ZOETROPE, the PRAXINOSCOPE (Reynaud, 1877) and Reynaud's THÉÂTRE OPTIQUE (1892 — hand-painted bands projected to a paying Paris audience three years before the Lumières, which makes projected animation older than projected photography); the MAGIC LANTERN's moving slides, the flip book, and the chronophotography of Muybridge and Marey (the motion study that AV-3 CH 2's walk cycles still consult). The first animated films: Blackton's Humorous Phases of Funny Faces (1906), Émile Cohl's Fantasmagorie (1908 — the first fully animated film by most counts, drawn line by line), Ladislas Starevich's stop-motion insects (1912 — AV-4's ancestor), Winsor McCay's Gertie the Dinosaur (1914 — the first character with personality, performed live by McCay on a vaudeville stage), and the Fleischers' ROTOSCOPE (patented 1915 — live action traced, the technique whose descendants run through Snow White's princess to motion capture, AV-11 CH 2). The industrialisation: the CEL (Bray and Hurd's 1914–15 patents — the transparent sheet that let a static background be reused and the moving parts be drawn separately: the single invention that made animation a business), the PEG BAR and registration, the EXPOSURE SHEET (the dope sheet — the frame-by-frame score that is still the animator's document, and which AV-6 CH 3's lip sync is written on), the DIVISION OF LABOUR (the director, the story department, the layout artist, the ANIMATOR drawing keys, the ASSISTANT drawing breakdowns, the INBETWEENER, the CLEAN-UP artist, the INKER and PAINTER — the last two historically women's departments, at women's wages, a fact the standard histories long omitted), and the assembly-line economics that made the shorts possible. The studio era: Disney's rise (the sync-sound Steamboat Willie (1928) — S-2's history from the animation side; the Silly Symphonies as a laboratory; the MULTIPLANE camera (1937) for depth; Snow White (1937) as the feature's proof), the RIVALS (Fleischer's rubber-hose surrealism and Betty Boop; Warner Bros.' Termite Terrace — Avery, Jones, Clampett, Freleng, and the anarchic comic tradition whose timing (AV-3 CH 3) is the funniest in the medium; MGM's Tom and Jerry; UPA's post-war revolt — Gerald McBoing-Boing and a flat, graphic, limited style that was both an aesthetic argument and a cost decision), the LABOUR history (the 1941 Disney strike — the animators' union fight, the walkout, the studio's permanent change; the industry's recurring labour struggles through to the present, AV-12 CH 3), and the shorts' decline into television's cheaper limited animation. The technical line: cel to XEROGRAPHY (101 Dalmatians, 1961 — the photocopied drawing that skipped the inker and changed the look), to the CAPS digital ink-and-paint system (The Rescuers Down Under, 1990 — the first fully digital feature finish), to the paperless 2D pipeline of today (AV-3 CH 1). The chapter's law: every aesthetic in this history was also an economic decision — the cel, the limited style, the xerox line, the digital pipeline — and the animator who knows which constraint produced which look can choose it deliberately instead of inheriting it.

Four answers to the question of what animation is for. The Disney naturalism: the tradition CH 1's principles describe — the illusion of life as the goal, full animation (a drawing on ones or twos), weight and volume and sentiment, the feature as the form's summit; its inheritors (the studio's own revivals; the American features of the 1990s; DreamWorks and Illumination's comic variants; Pixar's translation of the whole aesthetic into 3D — AV-5), and its critiques (the naturalism that made animation's fantasy realistic rather than free; the princess and the gender history; the cultural representations the studio has apologised for and the ones it has not). Japan: the world's largest animation industry and a genuinely different set of principles — LIMITED ANIMATION as an aesthetic (Tezuka Osamu's Astro Boy (1963) established the television economics — shooting on threes, held cels, reused cycles, banked shots — and the tradition turned constraint into a grammar: the HELD POSE with moving camera, the SMEAR and impact frame, the three-frame beat, the expressive still), the WORK (Ghibli's Miyazaki and Takahata — full animation, the "ma" of the pause, the observational realism of gesture; Kon Satoshi's edit-driven surrealism; Anno's Evangelion; Yuasa's elasticity; the Ufotable and MAPPA generation's compositing-heavy modern style), the PIPELINE (the genga/douga key-and-inbetween division, the production committee financing, the studio system's outsourcing and its labour conditions — AV-12 CH 3's hardest section), and the influence (the global anime market, and the Western animators who now openly work in its grammar). Eastern Europe and the art tradition: the state-funded studios that answered both Hollywood and naturalism — ZAGREB FILM (the Yugoslav school; Dušan Vukotić's Ersatz, the first non-American animation Oscar, 1961; reduced graphic style and satirical intent), the CZECH tradition (Jiří Trnka's puppets — AV-4; Karel Zeman's hybrids; Jan Švankmajer's surrealist objects and stop-motion), POLAND (Walerian Borowczyk and Jan Lenica; Piotr Dumała), RUSSIA and the USSR (Soyuzmultfilm; Yuri Norstein's Hedgehog in the Fog and Tale of Tales — widely voted the greatest animated films ever made, in a technique of cut-out glass layers he has spent decades on), HUNGARY, ESTONIA (Priit Pärn), and the tradition's characteristics (the short as the primary form, the metaphor and the political allegory under censorship, the individual author rather than the studio); plus the parallel independents elsewhere — McLaren at the NFB (the drawn-on-film and pixilation experiments), Len Lye, the Quay Brothers, Caroline Leaf, Lotte Reiniger's silhouette features (The Adventures of Prince Achmed, 1926 — the oldest surviving animated feature). The rest of the world: India's tradition (the Films Division's animation unit; Ram Mohan; the contemporary studios and the vast outsourcing sector — AV-12 CH 3), China (the Shanghai Animation Film Studio's ink-wash animation — a genuinely unique technique; the industry's modern scale), Korea (the outsourcing capital that became an originating industry), France (the Annecy festival as the form's world centre; the bande-dessinée-derived features; Michel Ocelot), Iran, Latin America and Africa's emerging studios. The computer: the arrival (Whitney's analogue experiments; the 1970s labs; Tron (1982) and The Last Starfighter; Pixar's shorts — Luxo Jr. (1986) as the proof that the twelve principles transferred whole; Toy Story (1995)), and the argument this track settles here: the computer is an instrument, not a tradition — every principle in CH 1 and every aesthetic in this chapter is available in it, and the films that fail are the ones whose makers learned the software without the craft. The chapter's law and the module's: study at least two traditions that are not the one you grew up with, learn what economic and political conditions produced each look — and stop treating naturalism as the default and everything else as stylisation.

AV-2 · Year 1 · Module Two

Drawing & Design for Animation

The Trailer

Walt Disney's story department invented the storyboard in the early 1930s by pinning drawings to a corkboard so a sequence could be judged before it was animated, and every film made since — animated or not — has inherited it. It exists because animation cannot afford to discover its film in the edit. A live-action director shoots coverage and finds the scene in the cutting room; an animator who animates the wrong shot has burned weeks. So the whole decision-making process moves forward, into drawings, into the board, and into the ANIMATIC — the boards cut to time with scratch dialogue and temp sound, which is the animated film's first actual edit and the moment its makers find out whether it works.

This module is the drawing and the planning. Gesture drawing and the study of life — the skill under everything in this track, including the 3D modules, because a bad animator with a good rig produces a well-lit mannequin. The character model: design as construction, the shapes that give a character its appeal, the model sheet that lets fifty people draw the same person. And the board and the animatic: sequence planning, staging, the cinematic language borrowed wholesale from live action, and the timing decisions that are made here and honoured for the rest of the production. You draw before you animate. You cut before you draw. The animatic is the film.

Three chapters: gesture, construction and drawing from life; the character model and design; and storyboarding and the animatic. Get a pencil and a sketchbook that fits in a pocket.

Chapters

The skill that every other skill in this track stands on. Gesture: the thirty-second drawing that captures what a body is DOING rather than what it looks like — the LINE OF ACTION (the single curve through the pose that carries its energy; the first mark made, and the one everything else serves), the THRUST and the counter-thrust, the WEIGHT's distribution (which foot carries it; where the centre of mass sits), the ASYMMETRY that makes a pose alive (the shoulders and hips opposed — contrapposto, borrowed from sculpture), and the discipline of drawing the ACTION rather than the model (Stanchfield's insistence: "draw what she's doing, not what she looks like"). Construction: the figure built from simple volumes — the sphere, the box, the cylinder in perspective — so that the drawing can be TURNED in space (the animator's requirement, since the character must hold together across a move; the principle CH 1 called solid drawing), the SKELETON and its proportions, the MASS and its overlaps, and the FORESHORTENING that beginners avoid and animation demands. Anatomy, usefully: not medical anatomy but the animator's subset — the ribcage and pelvis as two boxes with a flexible spine between them, the shoulder girdle's mobility, the joints' actual ranges (the elbow and knee's hinge; the wrist and ankle's compound motion), the muscles that visibly change shape, and the body's overlapping structures that produce follow-through. Drawing from life: the practice — the LIFE-DRAWING session (long and short poses, weekly, for years), the SKETCHBOOK in public (people on trains, in cafés, queuing — A-1 CH 1's observation discipline, from the Screen Acting track, with a pencil), the ANIMALS at the zoo, the SELF filmed and studied (the animator's mirror and phone as the primary reference — AV-6 CH 1), and the MOTION study (Muybridge's plates and modern reference footage — used to understand mechanics, never traced; the difference between reference and rotoscope is the difference between knowledge and copying). Why the 3D animator needs this: the module's argument for its own necessity — the 3D animator poses a rig, and the quality of the pose is exactly the quality of the drawing they would have made; the line of action, the weight, the asymmetry and the silhouette are all rig problems too, and the animators whose CG work has life are, with almost no exceptions, people who draw. The habits: quantity over precision (the thousand bad drawings), the timed gesture (thirty seconds, then two minutes, then ten), the STRAIGHT-AGAINST-CURVE principle (the drawing made of contrasts rather than uniform lines), and the honest self-assessment that improvement in drawing is slow, measurable and entirely a function of hours. The chapter's law: draw from life every week for years; find the line of action first; build volumes that can turn — and know that this is the one skill in the track that no software update will replace.

The exercise that contains the medium. The mechanics: a walk is a CONTROLLED FALL — the body's weight thrown forward and caught by the swinging leg; the four key positions per step — the CONTACT (both feet touching, legs at their widest, the body at mid-height), the DOWN or squash (the weight received, the body at its lowest, the knee bent — the frame that contains the weight), the PASSING position (the free leg passing the standing one, the body at its highest, the hips rotated), and the UP (the push-off, the body rising toward the next contact); the cycle is two steps (left and right) and the standard exercise is SIXTEEN FRAMES for a natural walk — a step per eight frames, or two steps per second at twenty-four. What must be right: the WEIGHT (the down position's timing and depth — the body must appear to be caught, not floated), the HIP and SHOULDER rotation (opposed — the contrapposto of CH 1's gesture, in motion), the ARM swing (opposite to the leg, one frame or two behind — AV-1's overlapping action), the HEAD's vertical path (a smooth arc, not a bounce — the commonest error is the walk that bobs like a puppet), the FOOT's action (the heel contact, the roll, the toe's push, the ankle's flex — the detail that separates a walk from a slide), the SPINE's whip and the body's overlap, and the ARCS everywhere (AV-1). The cycle's mechanics: the character walking ON THE SPOT against a moving background (the traditional cycle — the feet must not slip, which requires the background's move to exactly match the stride), versus the character ANIMATED ACROSS the frame (no cycle; each step drawn — more work, more life), and the digital equivalents (the 3D walk cycle on a rig with a translating root — AV-6 CH 1's decisions). The two variables, examined. The distinction: TIMING is HOW MANY frames an action takes; SPACING is WHERE the drawings sit within it — and confusing them is the commonest conceptual error in the medium: two actions with identical frame counts and different spacing are different actions entirely (the same twelve frames, evenly spaced, gives a mechanical slide; clustered at the ends, an organic ease; clustered at one end, an accelerating fall). The timing chart: the animator's notation on the key drawing — the ladder of tick marks showing where each inbetween sits between two extremes; the EVEN chart (mechanical), the EASED chart (the marks crowded near the extremes — AV-1's slow in and out), the FAVOURING chart (the breakdown placed closer to one key — the subtle control that gives an action its character), and the chart as the instruction the assistant follows, which is why it must be unambiguous. Frame counts as meaning: the practical tables (a head turn in six frames is a snap, in twelve a look, in twenty-four a slow realisation; a blink in three to five; a take's anticipation, hold and snap) — the numbers matter and the professional knows them the way a musician knows tempi; the MOOD produced (the same action at different counts producing urgency, weight, comedy or exhaustion), and the WEIGHT communicated (heavy things accelerate slowly and take many frames to stop; light things change direction in two). Stop-Motion

The Trailer

A stop-motion animator touches the puppet, steps back, presses the trigger, and the frame exists forever. There is no undo, no onion skin except the one the frame grabber gives them, and no second take that does not mean rebuilding the last four seconds. The craft's practitioners describe a state of total concentration sustained for hours, producing between one and five seconds of film per day, in a room nobody may enter. It is the oldest technique in this track, the least changed by computing, and the one whose results are most immediately, physically convincing — because the light really did fall on that object.

This module is animation with objects. Puppets, armatures, sets and the physical craft — fabrication, replacement animation, the rig removal that lets a puppet leave the ground, the surface materials and their behaviour under light. And the traditions: Aardman's clay and its comic timing, the Eastern European puppet schools of Trnka and Švankmajer, the Japanese and Chinese stop-motion, the American studio tradition from Harryhausen's monsters to Laika's 3D-printed faces — plus pixilation, cut-out, sand and paint-on-glass, the sibling techniques the form contains. One frame at a time, by hand, forever.

Chapters

The object must hold a pose, survive a thousand touches and photograph well. The armature: the skeleton — the BALL-AND-SOCKET armature (machined joints with adjustable friction; the professional standard; expensive, repairable, holds a pose exactly and survives a feature), the WIRE armature (aluminium or copper wire, cheap, quick to build, and fatigue-prone — it will break, usually in week three, which is why the wire puppet is built to be rebuilt), the HYBRID, the TIE-DOWN (the bolt through the set floor into the puppet's foot, or the magnet beneath — the method by which a puppet stands still while being touched), and the RIG (the armature support that holds a puppet in the air for a jump or a run — painted green or simply removed in compositing, AV-8 CH 2). The build: the SCULPT (the character from AV-2 CH 2's model sheet, sculpted in clay), the MOULD and the CAST (foam latex — the traditional flexible skin, hot-cast and fragile; SILICONE — the modern standard, durable and translucent, with the SUBSURFACE quality that reads as flesh under light; RESIN for hard parts), the CLAY puppet (Aardman's plasticine over armature — the surface that takes fingerprints and must be constantly reworked, and whose imperfection is the aesthetic), the FABRIC and costume (the cloth that BOILS — the fibres shifting frame to frame; the solution is treated fabric, wire-supported hems, or accepting the boil as the medium's signature), the HAIR (the same problem, worse), and the DUPLICATES (multiple identical puppets for simultaneous shooting units and for damage — a feature builds dozens of each character). The face: the module's central technical problem — the SCULPTED face reworked by hand each frame (clay; the animator's thumbprints as the record), the REPLACEMENT face system (CH 2), the MECHANICAL face (an armature inside the head with paddles moving brows and mouth — the Aardman method, operated with dental tools through the neck), and the HYBRID. The set: a miniature build (PD-4, PD-6 — the scale decided against the puppet, typically 1:6 to 1:12; the MATERIALS chosen for how they read at that scale, which is the miniature-maker's whole craft: the wood grain that looks like planks and not like matchsticks, the texture that survives a macro lens), the ACCESS (the set built in sections that lift out so an animator can reach a puppet, which governs the whole design), the RIGIDITY (nothing may shift between frames — the set is bolted, weighted and no one walks near it), the SCALE of movement (a puppet's step is a centimetre, so the set's distances are small and the camera's is smaller), and the LIGHTING (CL-12's principles in miniature — the tiny fixtures, the fibre optics, the absolute stability required since a bulb's shift is a flicker; LED and DMX control via the capture software made this immeasurably easier). The camera: locked absolutely (the tripod weighted, the floor isolated — a footstep in the next room is a bump in the shot), the DSLR or cinema camera tethered to the frame grabber, the deep stop for depth of field and the consequent light requirement, and MOTION CONTROL for camera moves (the rig that repeats a move one frame at a time, which is how stop-motion gets a dolly). The chapter's law: build the puppet to be touched ten thousand times, bolt the set to the floor, light it so nothing flickers, and design every set so the animator's hands can reach the middle of it.

Surface and motion artefacts: STROBING (the move too large per frame, which reads as a stutter — the cure is more frames or motion blur), the ABSENCE of motion blur (stop-motion's frames are sharp; fast moves look staccato, which is either a bug or the aesthetic — the GO-MOTION rig (Phil Tippett's ILM system for Empire Strikes Back and Dragonslayer) moved the puppet during the exposure to create real blur, and the modern answer is added in compositing), BOILING surfaces (CH 1), FINGERPRINTS in clay, and the LIGHT that must not shift. The rate: one to five seconds of finished film per animator per day is the industry norm, which is the arithmetic every stop-motion schedule and budget starts from. The chapter's law: plan the shot completely before the first frame, mark your positions physically, animate the eyes last, and never touch anything you did not intend to move — because every frame is a take you cannot retake.

Four or five national schools, each with its own idea of what a puppet is for. The origins: Ladislas Starevich (the Russian-Polish entomologist who animated dead beetles in 1912 and went on to a career in France — the technique's first master), Willis O'Brien (The Lost World, KING KONG (1933) — the armature creature composited into live action via rear projection and glass painting, which makes him the ancestor of both this module and AV-7's whole pipeline), and George Pal's Puppetoons (the replacement-body system, CH 2). Czech and Eastern European: the region's puppet theatre inheritance turned to film — Jiří TRNKA (the Czech master; the puppets with carved, unchanging faces whose expression comes entirely from lighting, posture and staging — his A Midsummer Night's Dream and the bitter final short The Hand (1965), a puppet coerced by a giant hand, made three years before the Prague Spring's end; the tradition's argument that a fixed face is more expressive than a mobile one because the audience projects), Karel ZEMAN (the hybrid of live action, puppets and engraving-style design — Invention for Destruction), Jan ŠVANKMAJER (the surrealist; object animation, meat and clay and found things, Alice and Faust and Dimensions of Dialogue — the tradition's most influential figure on later Western animators, and the argument that stop-motion's proper subject is the uncanny life of objects), Hermína Týrlová, and the Polish, Hungarian and Russian puppet studios. Britain: AARDMAN (Peter Lord and David Sproxton's studio; Nick Park's Wallace and Gromit and Creature Comforts — clay with mechanical mouths, comic timing derived from the British sitcom and from recorded-interview soundtracks, and a house style built on plasticine's imperfection; the studio's features and its enormous commercial influence), the British children's television tradition (the Trumptons and Clangers of the analogue era), and the Quay Brothers (the American-born, London-based makers of decayed puppet surrealism in the Švankmajer line). The United States: Ray HARRYHAUSEN (the creature animator whose DYNAMATION — the puppet animated between a rear-projected plate and a foreground miniature, all in camera — put monsters into live action for thirty years; Jason and the Argonauts' skeleton fight as the form's technical summit and AV-7's true ancestor), Rankin/Bass's television specials, Will Vinton's claymation, Henry Selick (CH 1's features), Tim Burton's productions, LAIKA (the modern studio — CH 2's 3D printing, digital augmentation and a commitment to the form as a feature medium), and Phil Tippett (go-motion, the creature shop, and Mad God — thirty years of solo work). Japan and elsewhere: Kihachirō Kawamoto (who studied with Trnka and brought Czech puppet technique home to a Japanese sensibility), the Japanese puppet tradition's bunraku inheritance, the Chinese studios' puppet and paper-cut work (the Shanghai studio again — AV-1 CH 3), and the contemporary international short-film scene where stop-motion remains one of the most viable independent forms. Why it persists: the light is real (the integration problem AV-10 CH 3 spends a chapter on does not exist), the imperfection reads as life, the technique is available to an individual with a table and a camera, and audiences respond to objects that were physically there — the same argument PD-6's practical builds make against full CG. The chapter's law and the module's: know that the fixed face and the mobile one are two complete philosophies, study the Czech school for what staging and light can do without a change of expression — and remember the craft is available to you alone, in a room, with a lamp.

AV-5 · Year 1 · Module Five

3D / CGI Fundamentals

The Trailer

A 3D character does not exist. What exists is a list of vertex positions, a set of instructions for how light should bounce off the surfaces between them, a hierarchy of transforms that deform those vertices when numbers change, and a program that solves a rendering integral for every pixel. Everything in the next six modules is built on that stack, and the reason this module comes after four modules of drawing, timing and puppets is that the stack is a delivery mechanism for the craft, not a substitute for it. The industry is full of people who can model and cannot animate, and the difference is always the first four modules.

This module is the CG foundation. Modelling — topology, polygons, subdivision, the edge flow that decides whether a face can smile. Texturing and shading — UVs, maps, PBR materials, and the physics that modern shading is trying to obey. And rigging — joints, skinning, deformers and controls, which is the discipline that turns a static mesh into something an animator can act with, and the most underappreciated craft in the pipeline. Plus the pipeline itself: what Maya, Blender and Houdini are each for, and the open standards that now hold the whole industry together. Learn the stack. Then forget it and animate.

Chapters

A mesh is a proposal about how a surface will deform. The representations: the POLYGON mesh (vertices, edges, faces — the universal working format; QUADS preferred over triangles because they subdivide predictably and deform cleanly; n-gons avoided in deforming areas), SUBDIVISION SURFACES (Catmull–Clark — the low-polygon CAGE that the renderer subdivides to a smooth limit surface; the standard for character and hard-surface film work, and the reason a film model can be edited at low density and rendered smooth), NURBS (parametric patches — largely superseded in film, still standard in industrial design), SCULPTING (the digital-clay workflow — millions of polygons pushed and pulled like maquette clay; ZBrush and Blender's sculpt mode; the organic route in, followed by RETOPOLOGY), and VOLUMES and point clouds (AV-9's territory; photogrammetry and scanning as a source for AV-11's digital humans). Topology: the module's real subject — the arrangement of edges that determines how a surface behaves when deformed; EDGE LOOPS (continuous rings of edges — around the eyes and mouth in concentric loops that mirror the orbicularis muscles, because a face deforms along those lines and a mesh built any other way tears or pinches), the POLES (vertices where three or five edges meet — unavoidable, but placed away from deforming areas), the EDGE FLOW that follows the anatomy, the DENSITY distributed where deformation happens (dense at joints and face, sparse on a flat chest), the SILHOUETTE's resolution (enough geometry to hold the profile at the shot's closest size), and the difference between a model built to be LOOKED AT (a hero prop, a statue) and one built to be DEFORMED (a character — a different and harder problem). The character model: built from AV-2 CH 2's orthographic sheet, in a NEUTRAL pose (the A-pose or T-pose, with the modern preference for a relaxed A-pose that puts the shoulder near its rest position), with the JOINT areas given enough geometry to bend, the face's loops planned around the expressions the rig will need (CH 3), and the scale set to real-world units from the start (the pipeline discipline that prevents a week of confusion later). Hard surface: the different craft — bevels on every edge (nothing in the world is perfectly sharp, and a razor edge reads as CG immediately), panel lines, the support loops that hold a subdivision's crease, boolean operations and their cleanup, and the CAD-derived workflows for vehicles and machinery. Practical matters: UV-friendly construction (CH 2), the model's LOD versions for games and crowds, the SYMMETRY workflow and where to break it, the naming and grouping conventions the pipeline requires (CH 4), and the BLOCKOUT-first discipline (proportions and silhouette established in primitives before any detail — the modelling equivalent of AV-2's thumbnail). The relationship to drawing: the model is a three-dimensional version of AV-2 CH 1's construction — volumes that must read from every angle — and modellers who draw produce better silhouettes, which is the module's second argument for Year 1's order. The chapter's law: block the proportions first, build in quads with loops that follow the anatomy, put your density where the deformation is — and model for how it will move, not for how it looks in the viewport.

Telling the renderer what the surface is made of. UVs: the unwrapping of a 3D surface into 2D space so that images can be painted onto it — the SEAMS placed where they will not be seen (the inside of an arm, a hairline, a panel join), the DISTORTION minimised and checked with a chequer map, the UV SHELLS packed efficiently, the TEXEL DENSITY kept consistent (so a detail is the same physical size everywhere — the commonest beginner failure), UDIMs (the multi-tile layout that gives a film-resolution character many 4K tiles), and the alternatives (PTEX, triplanar projection, procedural texturing that needs no UVs at all). The maps: the images that drive a shader — BASE COLOUR or albedo (the surface's colour with no lighting information baked in — the discipline that a photographic texture with shadows in it will fight the render), ROUGHNESS (how polished or diffuse the reflection is — the single most important map for realism, because the real world's variation in roughness is what makes surfaces look used), METALNESS (the binary-ish distinction between metals, which tint their reflections and have no diffuse, and everything else), NORMAL and BUMP (the faked surface detail that perturbs the shading without adding geometry), DISPLACEMENT (real geometric detail added at render time — the difference visible at silhouettes), AMBIENT OCCLUSION, and the specialist maps (subsurface, transmission, sheen, anisotropy, emission). PBR: PHYSICALLY BASED RENDERING — the modern convention that shading models should obey physics: ENERGY CONSERVATION (a surface cannot reflect more light than it receives), the FRESNEL effect (every dielectric surface becomes mirror-like at grazing angles — the single physical fact that most improves a render's believability), the MICROFACET model (a surface as a distribution of tiny mirrors, whose statistical roughness produces the specular highlight's shape — GGX as the standard distribution), the BRDF as the formalism, and the practical consequence — PBR materials behave correctly in any lighting, which is why the same asset can go from a studio HDRI to a night exterior without being re-authored (AV-10 CH 1's whole workflow depends on this). Specific materials: SKIN (subsurface scattering — light entering the surface, bouncing beneath and exiting elsewhere; the reason skin does not look like plastic, and the single hardest common material — AV-11 CH 2's digital humans), HAIR and FUR (the curve primitives, the specialised shading models, the grooming tools), EYES (the refraction through the cornea, the wet meniscus — a small object with an outsized effect on whether a character reads as alive), CLOTH (sheen and fibre-level detail), GLASS and LIQUIDS (transmission, refraction, absorption — AV-9), and VEGETATION (translucency). The texturing workflow: the SCULPTED detail baked to maps (high-poly to low-poly baking), the 3D PAINTING tools (Substance Painter as the industry standard, with Blender and free alternatives), the PROCEDURAL approach (noise and pattern networks that generate detail without images — resolution-independent, and the modern preference for surfaces like rock and metal), the SCAN-based library (the photogrammetric material libraries that made photoreal surfacing an asset-purchase rather than a craft, for better and worse), and the LOOK DEVELOPMENT process (the asset lit in standard conditions and reviewed under several HDRIs before it goes near a shot — AV-10 CH 1's lookdev turntable). Colour management: the discipline that every texture must be tagged with its colour space (albedo maps in sRGB, data maps like roughness in linear), and the pipeline's OpenColorIO/ACES configuration (CL-18's colour science from the CG side — the single commonest source of "why does it look different in the render" problems). The chapter's law: unwrap cleanly with consistent texel density, author maps that obey PBR's physics, get the roughness variation right because that is where realism lives — and tag every texture's colour space, every time.

The apparatus that turns a mesh into an instrument. What a rig is: a hierarchy of TRANSFORMS (joints), a system for binding the mesh to them (skinning), a layer of DEFORMERS that correct and enhance what skinning cannot do, and a set of CONTROLS an animator can grab — the whole assembled so that a performer can think about acting and not about mathematics; the RIGGER's craft is invisible when it works and is the most common reason animation is bad when it doesn't. Joints and hierarchy: the skeleton placed to match the anatomy (the joint at the actual rotation centre — a shoulder placed wrong will never deform correctly no matter what else is done), the ORIENTATION conventions (consistent axes so rotations are predictable), the PARENT hierarchy, and the naming and mirroring discipline. Skinning: binding vertices to joints with WEIGHTS (each vertex influenced by several joints in proportion), the smooth-bind's failures (the CANDY-WRAPPER twist at the wrist and forearm — solved with twist joints; the volume loss at bent elbows and knees), WEIGHT PAINTING as a craft, and the modern additions (dual quaternion skinning, delta mush, corrective systems). IK and FK: FORWARD kinematics (rotating each joint in turn down the chain — the natural choice for arcs and free motion, and the reason arm animation is usually FK), INVERSE kinematics (the solver that positions a chain's end and computes the rest — the natural choice for a foot planted on the ground or a hand on a surface), the IK/FK SWITCH and its blending (a rig without one is a rig that fights the animator), the POLE VECTOR for knee and elbow direction, and the SPACE SWITCHING (a hand that can be parented to the world, the hip or a prop — the feature animators most ask for). Deformers and correctives: BLEND SHAPES (sculpted target shapes blended in — the face's primary mechanism and the basis of FACS-derived rigs), CORRECTIVE shapes driven by joint angles (the bicep that bulges when the elbow bends), LATTICE and wrap deformers, the SQUASH-AND-STRETCH systems that let a 3D character obey AV-1's first principle (which a naive rig cannot do at all), and the muscle and tissue simulation layers of high-end creature work (AV-9). The face: the rig within a rig — the blend-shape library (a production face rig may carry hundreds of shapes), the FACS basis (the Facial Action Coding System's action units as a shared vocabulary — the standard that makes facial capture and hand animation interoperable, AV-11 CH 2), the joint-based face rig for games and real-time, the CONTROLS (on-face controls versus a control panel — the animator's ergonomics), and the mouth's corner and lip systems where most facial rigging effort goes. Controls and ergonomics: the shapes an animator grabs (visible, colour-coded, on sensible planes), the ATTRIBUTES exposed (finger curls, eye aims, the switches), the SELECTION sets and picker, the rig's SPEED (a rig that plays back below ten frames a second will not be animated well — the proxy mesh and the rig's evaluation performance as a real deliverable), and the documentation the animator gets. The relationship: the rigger and the animator must talk — the rig is designed for the shots the film needs, and the AV-6 animator's complaints are the rigger's specification. The process that protects the craft from the software. The brief: the shot received — the animatic's timing (AV-2 CH 3), the dialogue track (recorded first), the layout and camera, the director's intention, the surrounding shots' continuity, and the SHOT LENGTH which is already fixed; the animator's first act is to understand what the shot is FOR in the sequence (A-6 CH 1's event, from the animator's side). Planning: the THUMBNAILS (small, fast drawings of the key poses and the shot's arc — the single most valuable half-hour in the process; the animator who skips it loses days), the REFERENCE (the animator filming themselves performing the shot — the profession's universal practice; the camera placed at the shot's angle, several takes, chosen and studied, never traced — AV-2 CH 1's reference-versus-rotoscope line), the observed reference (the animal, the mechanism, the person in the world), the BEAT list (what happens on which frame — the shot's timing decided on paper before the rig is touched), and the acting decisions (CH 2). Blocking: the key poses set in STEPPED tangents (no interpolation — the computer shows only the poses, so the animator sees exactly the drawings they have made and nothing the software invented; this is AV-3's key-and-breakdown discipline transplanted, and the reason studios insist on it), the pose-to-pose build (the storytelling poses first, then the breakdowns that determine the path — the breakdown's power again), the TIMING refined in stepped (the frame counts moved until the shot reads as a series of stills), and the BLOCKING REVIEW (the shot approved in stepped form before any smoothing — the checkpoint that prevents a week of polish on a shot whose acting is wrong). The spline pass: the conversion to interpolated curves and the immediate problem — the computer's smooth easing makes everything FLOAT (AV-3 CH 3's characteristic 3D failure); the work is fixing what the interpolation invented: the GRAPH EDITOR discipline (the curves read and shaped directly — the animator who cannot read an F-curve is working blind), the tangents adjusted per channel, the OVERSHOOTS and settles added, the arcs corrected (the tracking arcs checked by drawing on the screen or with a motion trail — the computer does not produce arcs unless told), and the BREAKDOWNS added where the spline cheated. Polish: the passes — the ARCS, the overlapping and follow-through (the offsets staggered so nothing arrives at once — AV-1's principle, applied channel by channel), the SETTLE and cushion, the EYES and blinks (CH 2), the fingers and secondary detail, the face, the moving holds (nothing dead), the CONTACTS (feet not slipping, hands not floating through props), and the final pass against the sequence rather than in isolation. The review loop: the PLAYBLAST (the fast preview) watched in a loop and at speed, the DAILIES (the shot shown to the supervisor and director with the rest of the sequence — the animation department's version of E-3's screening), the notes taken and executed, and the shot's versions. The rate: two to five seconds of finished character animation per animator per week on a feature is the industry norm — the arithmetic AV-12 CH 2's budgets rest on. The chapter's law: thumbnail it, shoot reference, block in stepped and get the acting approved before you spline — and when the spline pass makes it float, that is not the software's fault, it is the work.

The principle first: a dialogue performance is carried by the BODY, the EYES and the TIMING of the thought; the mouth's job is not to draw attention to itself by being wrong — perfect lip sync on a dead performance is worthless, and a slightly loose mouth on a thinking character is invisible; this ordering is the chapter's most important instruction and the one most often inverted by beginners. The plate: the photographed image the effect will be built into — shot with the VFX in mind (the CLEAN PLATE: the same framing without the actors or the element to be removed, which costs ninety seconds on the day and saves days of painting; the EXTENSION plate; the ELEMENT plates of smoke, fire, water and debris shot separately for AV-8's compositing; the TILE or panoramic plates for set extension), the FRAMING discipline (shoot wider than the final frame if the shot will be stabilised or repositioned; agree the extraction with editorial), and the technical specification (resolution, codec, dynamic range — the log or RAW capture that CL-18's pipeline requires; the frame rate; the shutter angle, which determines the motion blur the CG must match). Matchmove: solving, from the plate's image alone (plus the data of CH 2), the virtual camera whose position, rotation and lens exactly match the real one frame by frame — so that CG placed in that space stays locked to the plate; the PROCESS (the plate UNDISTORTED using the lens grid; FEATURES tracked across frames — corners, markers, high-contrast detail; the SOLVE computed — the software finding the camera path and a sparse point cloud that best explains the tracked features' motion; the SCALE and orientation set from the set survey's measurements, because a solve is scale-ambiguous without them; the solve VERIFIED by placing test geometry in the scene and watching for slip), the ERROR budget (a solve is good when CG sits without swimming; sub-pixel accuracy is the standard), and the HARD cases (the locked-off camera that needs no solve but does need a lens; the pure PAN or nodal move which cannot triangulate depth and is solved differently; the long lens whose perspective barely changes; the plate with no parallax, no features, or motion blur; the handheld shot in a featureless white room, which is why markers exist). Compositing

The Trailer

Compositing is where every other department's work is judged, because it is the only place the audience ever sees it. The image: pixels as floating-point numbers, with values above 1.0 preserved (the HDR discipline — a highlight at 8.0 must survive the pipeline so that a defocus or a glow blooms correctly; clamping to 1.0 is the beginner's silent destruction of a shot), the CHANNELS (R, G, B, A, plus arbitrary named channels — depth, motion vectors, normals, IDs — carried in OpenEXR; AV-10 CH 2's AOVs), LINEAR light (compositing must happen in a linear-light space because addition and multiplication of light are only physically meaningful there — the single most important technical fact in the module; the display transform applied only for viewing, and CL-18/ACES as the framework), and BIT DEPTH and the reasons for 16-bit half float. The operators: the OVER operation (Porter and Duff's algebra: foreground plus background times one-minus-alpha — the composite's fundamental equation), the requirement of PREMULTIPLIED alpha for it to be correct (the colour multiplied by its own alpha; the UNPREMULT before a colour correction and the re-premult after — the single most common error in the craft, visible as dark or bright edge fringing), the other merges (plus, screen, multiply, min, max and their correct uses — PLUS for additive elements like fire and light, OVER for occluding ones), and the TRANSFORM operations with their filtering (and the discipline of CONCATENATING transforms so an image is resampled once, not five times). The toolkit: COLOUR operations (grade, offset, gamma, saturation, and the difference between working on a plate and on a render), the CHANNEL operations (shuffling, copying, extracting), BLURS and convolutions (and the physically-correct defocus versus the box blur), TIME operations (retiming with motion vectors; frame offsets), the TRACKER and STABILISE (2D tracking in comp, distinct from AV-7 CH 3's 3D solve), CORNER PIN, and the 3D system inside a compositor (the card and camera setup that lets 2.5D projections and set extensions be built in comp — the projection workflow that does half the world's set extension). 2.5D and projections: the technique of projecting a matte painting or a photograph onto simple geometry from the solved camera, so a still image gains correct parallax — AV-10 CH 4's matte painting delivered, and the cheapest convincing environment work there is. Working practice: the SCRIPT organised in sections, the render caching, the comparison against the plate constantly (the A/B), the viewer's exposure and gamma checks (looking at the blacks with the exposure raised is how edges and matte problems are found), and the delivery (the correct colour space, the handles, the naming — AV-7 CH 4). The chapter's law: work in linear, understand premultiplication and unpremult before every colour correction, use OVER for occlusion and PLUS for light, concatenate your transforms — and keep the script legible enough for a stranger.

AV-9 · Year 2 · Module Nine

Simulation & Effects

The Trailer

An effects artist does not animate smoke. They construct a system — a solver, a set of forces, an emitter, a resolution, a hundred parameters — and then run it, look at the result, change something and run it again, sometimes for days, until the physics produces something that is both plausible and dramatically correct. The craft is part fluid dynamics, part procedural programming and part art direction, and its central tension is that the simulation is solving a physical problem while the director is asking for a narrative one. "Make the smoke angrier" is a real note, and the effects artist's job is to translate it into parameters.

This module is the physics department. Particles and dynamics — the point systems, rigid bodies and forces that underlie everything. Fluids, smoke and fire — the volumetric solvers that produce the medium's most recognisable effects and consume its largest render budgets. Destruction — fracturing, constraints, debris, and the Houdini-centred procedural approach that dominates it. And creature effects — the cloth, hair and muscle simulation that sits on top of AV-6's animation and makes a character's surface behave. You do not animate it. You build the system that produces it, then you art-direct the physics.

Four chapters: particles and dynamics; fluids, smoke and fire; destruction and fracturing; and creature effects — cloth, hair and flesh. Cache everything.

Chapters

Points with velocities, and the forces that act on them. Particles: the point system — the EMITTER (the source geometry, volume or point from which particles are born, with a birth rate that is the system's first parameter), the ATTRIBUTES each particle carries (position, velocity, age, life, size, colour, mass, and any custom attribute the artist invents — the procedural approach's power is that anything can drive anything), the FORCES (gravity, wind, turbulence and noise fields, drag, attractors and repellers, vortex and curl noise — the last being the single most useful tool for making motion look organic rather than computed), the COLLISIONS (with geometry, with each other, with the ground, and the bounce, friction and stick parameters), the LIFE and death rules, and the RENDERING (particles as points, sprites, instanced geometry or as a source for volumes). Rigid bodies: solid objects that do not deform — the SOLVER (the collision detection and constraint resolution that keeps objects from interpenetrating), the collision GEOMETRY (the simplified proxy the solver uses — convex hulls, spheres, the mesh itself; the performance/accuracy trade-off), MASS and inertia, FRICTION and restitution, CONSTRAINTS (the joints, hinges, springs and glue that hold objects together — the basis of CH 3's destruction), SLEEPING (the objects that stop being simulated when they settle), and the ACTIVE/PASSIVE distinction. Soft bodies and springs: the mass-spring systems that deform (the jelly, the trampoline), the FEM approaches that do it with proper material models, and their role as CH 4's simpler ancestor. The craft: the module's real subject — SIMULATION IS ITERATION (run at low resolution, judge, change, re-run; the artist's working loop, and the reason a fast preview matters more than a perfect solver), the CACHING discipline (every simulation written to disk so downstream work is deterministic and does not re-solve — the cache as the pipeline's unit for FX; AV-5 CH 4's publishing applied), the RESOLUTION strategy (the low-res version for timing and art direction, the high-res for the final, and the painful truth that they do not always look the same — the "sim does not scale" problem that experienced artists design around), ART DIRECTABILITY (the recurring demand: the simulation must do what the shot needs, so the artist builds in controls — force fields keyframed to shape the sim, the sim guided by animated geometry, the "cheat" that is the actual craft), and LAYERING (a convincing effect is never one simulation — it is a base sim, a secondary sim driven by the first, a debris pass, a dust pass and a lighting pass, each cached and composited — AV-8). The procedural mindset: Houdini's model — everything is a node network that can be re-run with different inputs, so the artist builds a SYSTEM rather than a result, which means a change to the shot's timing does not mean starting again; this is why the effects discipline is the most programmatic in the track and why an FX artist who can write VEX or Python is worth several who cannot (AV-5 CH 4). The chapter's law: build a system rather than a result, iterate at low resolution, cache everything, layer your passes — and keep enough hand control that you can art-direct the physics when the note arrives.

Breaking things convincingly, which is mostly about what happens after they break. The fracture: the geometry pre-broken before simulation — VORONOI fracturing (the standard approach: points scattered through the volume, the space partitioned into cells, the object cut along the cell boundaries — with the scatter's density and distribution controlling the debris size and its variation), the MATERIAL-driven fracture (concrete shatters differently from wood, which splinters along a grain, which differs from glass's radial-and-concentric pattern — the realism comes from matching the fracture pattern to the material, and this is the step most often skipped), the CLUSTERING (cells grouped into larger chunks so the object does not disintegrate into uniform gravel — the difference between a building collapsing and a building dissolving), the INTERIOR detail (the fractured faces need their own texture and displacement — the concrete's aggregate, the rebar; a clean interior face reads as CG instantly), and the PROGRESSIVE or dynamic fracture (breaking driven by the simulation's stresses rather than pre-computed — more expensive, more accurate). The simulation: the CONSTRAINT network (the pieces glued together with constraints whose strength determines when and where the object breaks — the art direction happens here: a constraint map painted so the building fails where the story needs it to), the ACTIVATION (pieces waking as forces reach them, which is what produces a collapse's progressive quality), the STRUCTURE (buildings that fall because their supports fail, not because every piece was pushed — the difference between a demolition and a shove), and the SOLVER's practical limits (piece counts in the millions; the instancing and proxy strategies). The secondary layers: the module's recurring principle at its most important — a destruction shot is the rigid-body sim, plus the DUST (a volume sim sourced from the pieces' motion — usually the element that sells the shot entirely), the DEBRIS (small particles instanced with geometry, emitted from impacts), the SMOKE, the SPARKS or fire, the deformation of bending materials (metal that crumples rather than shatters — a soft-body or FEM layer), and the ATMOSPHERE; the professional rule that if a destruction looks wrong it is almost always the dust, not the physics. Art direction: the sequence's beats hit (the wall must fall on frame 62 — achieved with keyframed forces, animated constraint breaking and the guided-simulation techniques of CH 1), the readability (a destruction the audience cannot follow is noise — AV-1's staging, applied to chaos), the SCALE again (the debris that is too uniform or falls too fast reads as miniature — the classic tell, and the same physics problem as CH 2's water), and the CAMERA's role (the shot designed so the destruction reads — AV-7 CH 1's previz). The practical alternative: the miniature (PD-6's practical builds and the model-unit tradition — still used, still often better, and the reason AV-12 CH 1's supervisors ask "can we shoot it?" first). The chapter's law: fracture according to the material, cluster so it breaks into pieces rather than gravel, drive the failure through constraints so the collapse is progressive — and put your effort into the dust, because that is what the audience is actually looking at.

Cinematography with no physics to fight. The light types: the AREA light (a rectangle, disc or sphere that emits — the workhorse, because its SIZE relative to the subject determines shadow softness exactly as a real source does; CL-12's inverse-square and source-size relationships hold precisely), the POINT and SPOT, the DISTANT or sun light (parallel rays), the MESH light (any geometry made emissive — the practical lamp, the screen, the fire), the PORTAL (the optimisation that tells the renderer where the light enters an interior), and the IBL or environment light (CH's core — an HDRI mapped to a sphere, illuminating everything from every direction with real-world intensity ratios; the AV-7 CH 2 capture becoming the shot's light). The rig: the KEY (direction, size, colour, intensity — the decision that makes the image; CL-10's principles applied), the FILL (ambient, bounce, or a soft source at low intensity — and the CG-specific temptation to fill until nothing has shape), the RIM or kicker, the BOUNCE (in a physically-based renderer bounce happens automatically via global illumination, which is the single biggest difference from pre-2000 CG lighting — but the lighter still places bounce CARDS to shape it), the PRACTICALS in shot, and the LIGHT LINKING (the CG-only power to make a light affect one object and not another — indispensable and the road to incoherence if abused; the discipline is to use it for correction, not for construction). The machine that resolves the light, and what it costs. Path tracing: the modern production algorithm — rays traced from the camera into the scene, bouncing according to the surfaces' BRDFs (AV-5 CH 2) until they find light, with the pixel's value being the AVERAGE of many such random paths; the consequence that renders are NOISY and converge slowly (halving the noise requires four times the samples — the square-root law that governs every render budget), the RAY TYPES (camera, diffuse, glossy, transmission, shadow) and their separate sample counts, the BOUNCE limits (the number of bounces before a path is terminated — and the visible consequence of setting it too low in interiors), and RUSSIAN ROULETTE and the other unbiased-termination tricks. Sampling and noise: IMPORTANCE SAMPLING (sending rays where the light and the BRDF actually are, rather than uniformly — the single technique that makes path tracing feasible), MULTIPLE IMPORTANCE SAMPLING, the LIGHT sampling strategies (many lights, and how a renderer chooses), the ADAPTIVE sampling that spends effort where the variance is, the CLAMPING that kills fireflies at the cost of energy, and DENOISING (the machine-learning denoisers that now let a production render at a fraction of the samples — the biggest practical change in rendering in a decade, with its own artefacts: the smeared detail, the temporal flicker, and the discipline of denoising with albedo and normal guide passes). The renderers: the production path tracers (RenderMan, Arnold, V-Ray, Karma, Redshift, Cycles) and their differences in practice; the GPU shift (the renderers that run on graphics hardware — an order-of-magnitude speedup with memory limits that matter on heavy scenes); and the REAL-TIME renderers (AV-11 — rasterisation plus hardware ray tracing, now good enough for final-pixel work in some contexts). The costs: the RENDER FARM (the facility's compute; the shot's frames distributed; the queue and the priority), the per-frame TIME as the number every decision is judged against (a shot at two hours a frame across 200 frames across 30 iterations is the arithmetic that kills schedules), the MEMORY ceiling (the scene that will not fit — solved with instancing, procedurals loaded at render time, and level-of-detail), the DEEP images and the decisions made to keep comp flexible, and the sustainability question the industry has begun to ask about render-farm energy. AOVs: the Arbitrary Output Variables — the separate channels a render writes alongside the beauty: the LIGHT GROUPS (each light or group rendered separately so comp can rebalance the rig without re-rendering — the single most valuable thing a lighter can deliver to a compositor), the component passes (diffuse, specular, transmission, subsurface, emission — direct and indirect, which sum to the beauty), the DATA passes (depth or Z, position, normals, motion vectors, UVs), the ID and CRYPTOMATTE passes (AV-8 CH 3's object isolation), and the discipline of agreeing the AOV set with compositing BEFORE the shot is rendered, because re-rendering for a missing pass is the pipeline's most avoidable waste. The chapter's law: understand that noise falls with the square root of samples, use importance sampling and denoising rather than brute force, watch the per-frame time like a budget — and deliver light groups and data passes so the compositor can fix things without your farm.

Matching the light that was actually there. The method: the lighter's job on a live-action shot is not to make a beautiful image but to make an image that BELONGS in a photograph somebody else took — the process: the HDRI from AV-7 CH 2 loaded as the environment light (the light of that moment, in that place, at that intensity), the CHROME BALL rendered in the same lighting and compared against the photographed one (if the highlights do not match in position, size and intensity, the rig is wrong), the GREY BALL likewise for intensity and colour (the two-ball check is the discipline; a lighter who skips it is guessing), the LINEAR workflow and correct colour space (AV-8 CH 1 and CL-18 — a mismatch here makes everything downstream unfixable), and the EXPOSURE matched to the plate's. The refinements: the KEY isolated from the HDRI and replaced with a real area light (an HDRI's sun is a few pixels and gives noisy, soft-edged shadows — the standard practice is to remove the sun from the environment map and add a matched directional or area light, which gives control and clean shadows), the BOUNCE from the plate's environment (the geometry from AV-7 CH 3's survey, textured with projected plate imagery so the CG picks up colour from the real surroundings), the SHADOW CATCHER and holdout geometry (the ground that receives the CG's shadow and renders only that contribution, so comp can place it), the INTERACTIVE light (the CG that must illuminate the plate — rendered as a separate pass and added), and the REFLECTION of the plate in the CG (the environment map plus projected geometry, which is what makes a CG car in a street look like it is in that street). The verification: the shot rendered and taken to comp, where AV-8 CH 3's checklist applies — and the specific tests: the shot flicked against the plate, the CG's blacks compared, the shot viewed at half-size and squinted at (the oldest trick: gross errors of value and colour survive squinting; detail does not), the shot shown to someone who has not seen it, and the 2 A.M. test. The hard cases: SKIN in a plate (AV-11 CH 2), WATER and glass (the refraction of a real environment), SNOW and white surfaces (where exposure errors are unhidable), the MOVING light (the shot where the light changes across the take — the HDRI is a single moment and the rig must be animated to match), the MIXED colour temperature plate, and the plate shot on a day that does not match the shot either side of it (the continuity problem that becomes VFX's to solve). The stylised counterpart: the integration of CG into a stylised or graded plate (the film with a strong look — the CG must match the LOOK, not reality, which means the grade or a LUT must be in the lighter's viewer — CL-18's show LUT, and the commonest cause of "it looked fine in my render"). The department's relationship: the lighter and the compositor as one unit (many facilities combine them; the best work happens when the lighter knows what comp will do and delivers for it — CH 2's AOVs), and the supervisor's eye as the arbiter. The chapter's law and the module's: light from the captured HDRI, replace the sun with a real light, render the two balls and compare them against the photographed ones, work in the show's colour space with the look applied — and judge your integration squinting, at half size, tomorrow.

Virtual Production

The Trailer

In 2019 a Star Wars television series was shot against a curved wall of LED panels displaying a real-time rendered environment, reacting in perspective to the camera's position, and the resulting images went into the show largely as photographed. The technique was not new — rear projection did the same job in 1930, and Gravity's light box did it with light in 2013 — but the combination of game-engine rendering, camera tracking and LED brightness made it good enough to be the final image rather than a reference. Virtual production is the convergence this whole Conservatory has been circling: the animation pipeline, the VFX pipeline and the shooting floor, in one room, at the same time.

And on-set virtual production more broadly: the virtual camera, the simulcam, the scouting in VR, and what it means for the crafts of cinematography, production design and directing when the set exists as a file. Chapters

The set that is a screen, and the render that is the light. How it works: a wall (and often a ceiling) of LED panels displays a real-time rendered environment; the CAMERA is tracked in three dimensions; the engine renders the environment from the camera's exact position and perspective into the FRUSTUM (the region of the wall the camera can see, rendered at high quality with correct parallax), while the rest of the wall displays a lower-fidelity version whose job is to LIGHT the scene and appear in reflections; the actor stands in a physical set that blends into the wall, lit by the wall's own emitted light — so the environment, the lighting and the reflections are all captured IN CAMERA. What it is good at: the REFLECTIVE subject (the car, the helmet, the glass — where green screen is a nightmare and the volume is trivial), the LIGHTING (the environment's colour and direction fall on the actor for real — CL-17's argument; this is the single biggest quality gain and the one that persists even when the wall's image is later replaced), the ACTOR's experience (A-7 — the performer sees the world rather than a blue wall), the DIRECTOR and DP seeing the shot on the day, the CONTROLLED environment (any time of day, any weather, on demand, without travel), and the SCHEDULE (locations that would take a unit move achieved between setups). What it is bad at: the MOIRÉ and the panel's pixel pitch (the wall must be far enough away and the lens long enough, or the panels resolve — the constraint that governs the stage's design), the PARALLAX limits (only the tracked camera sees correct perspective; a second camera does not, so multi-camera work is constrained), the FRUSTUM's edge, the WIDE shot (the wall's resolution and the volume's physical size run out — the volume is a medium-and-tight tool), the COLOUR (the panels' primaries are not a camera's — CL-18's colour pipeline is genuinely hard here, and the on-set colour science is a specialist job), the COST (a volume is an expensive facility whose economics only work at scale or for specific content), and the CONTENT problem (the environment must be BUILT to final quality BEFORE the shoot — the schedule inverts, which is the module's biggest practical consequence: the money and the decisions move to preproduction, and a production that cannot commit early should not use a volume). The real-time engine: the game engine (Unreal predominantly) as the renderer — rasterisation plus hardware ray tracing at 24–60 frames per second, with the LUMEN/GI and virtual-texture systems that made real-time environments credible; the ASSET pipeline (AV-5's models optimised for real time; the LOD and instancing; the difference between an offline-render asset and a real-time one, which is a real conversion cost), and the same engine's other uses (previz — AV-7 CH 1; final-pixel rendering for animation, which some productions now do). The new roles: the VIRTUAL ART DEPARTMENT (the environment built by artists working months ahead — PD-10's designers now building in an engine), the BRAIN BAR (the engine operators on set who change the environment live — "move the sun ten degrees" as a real-time request, which is the technique's most seductive and most dangerous capability), the on-set colour scientist, and the stage's own engineering crew. The chapter's law: use a volume for reflections, interactive light and medium shots, keep the camera far from the wall and the lens long, build the content to final quality before the shoot — and remember the schedule inverts, because everything must be decided early.

The hardest problem in the medium, and the most contested. Performance capture: the chain — the MARKERS (retroreflective dots tracked by an array of infrared cameras, giving precise positions at high rates) or the MARKERLESS systems (computer vision solving the body from ordinary cameras — improving rapidly), the VOLUME (the capture stage, or the on-location capture of A-9 CH 4's suits), the SOLVE (the marker positions converted into a skeleton's joint rotations — the step where noise, occlusion and sliding markers become the animator's problem), the RETARGETING (the human's motion mapped onto a character of different proportions — the ape, the giant, the child; where naive retargeting produces sliding feet and broken contacts), and the ANIMATOR's role (the persistent truth of the field: capture data is a starting point that animators clean, correct and often substantially re-perform — the credit question A-9 CH 4 raises, answered honestly as a collaboration). Facial capture: the HEAD-MOUNTED CAMERA rig, the facial SOLVE (the performance mapped to the character's FACS-based rig — AV-5 CH 3; the shared vocabulary that makes this possible), the 4D SCANNING (the actor's face captured in motion at high resolution to build the character's shape library), the EYES and the tongue (solved separately and usually hand-animated), and the UNCANNY VALLEY (Mori's hypothesis — the dip in affinity as a synthetic human approaches realism without reaching it; the practical causes are almost always the eyes, the skin's subsurface behaviour, the micro-expressions and the timing, and the field's progress has been a decade of chipping at each). The digital human's pipeline: the SCAN (photogrammetry and light-stage capture of the actor — the reflectance data that makes a skin shader accurate), the skin (AV-5 CH 2's subsurface at the limit), the eyes, the hair (AV-9 CH 4), the muscle and flesh (AV-9 CH 4), and the RENDER. The applications: the DIGITAL DOUBLE (the stunt-double replacement, the wide shot, the impossible action — routine and unremarked), DE-AGEING (the performance captured and the face rebuilt younger — AV-7 CH 4's Benjamin Button, and the later films whose de-ageing was the subject of the criticism), the POSTHUMOUS or unavailable performer (the completed film; the recreated character — where the ethical and legal questions are sharpest), the DIGITAL CROWD, and the SYNTHETIC performer generated rather than captured. The likeness question: the module's necessary section — the ACTOR's rights in their scanned face, voice and performance (the guild agreements that now govern consent, compensation, and the use of a digital replica; the requirement of informed consent for specific uses rather than blanket grants — A-9 CH 4's "read what you sign"), the POSTHUMOUS use and the estate's position, the GENERATIVE systems trained on performances, the DISCLOSURE question (does the audience have a right to know?), and the LABOUR question (the digital double that replaces a stunt performer; the synthetic extra that replaces a background artist — AV-12 CH 3); the track's position is that the technology is neutral and the consent, compensation and disclosure are not optional, and that a craftsperson should know the terms they are working under. The craft's honest state: a digital human in a controlled shot is now solvable; a digital human in an unconstrained close-up, talking, for minutes, remains the frontier — and the best results are still capture plus enormous quantities of animator hand-work. The chapter's law: capture is a starting point that animators finish, the face is solved through a shared FACS vocabulary, the eyes and the skin are where the uncanny lives — and never work on a likeness without knowing what the performer consented to.

The most meritocratic hiring process in the film industry, and how to survive it. The reel's rules: BEST WORK FIRST (the reviewer decides in ten to fifteen seconds — the first shot is the reel), SHORT (sixty to ninety seconds for most disciplines; longer only if the work sustains it; the reviewer watches dozens per sitting), TARGETED (a reel for one discipline — a compositing reel, an animation reel; the generalist reel that shows six things badly is the commonest self-inflicted wound; make separate reels if you must), ONLY YOUR BEST (the reel is judged on its WEAKEST shot, because the reviewer assumes the weakest is your average — the hardest and most important rule), NO MUSIC-DRIVEN montage (the work, not the edit; and no lengthy title card), and the FORMAT (a link that plays instantly, with the shot breakdown available). The breakdown document: the required companion — for every shot, exactly what YOU did ("modelled, textured and lit; animation by another artist"; "comp only, CG supplied"), the software, and the context; the honesty is non-negotiable and the industry is small enough that misrepresentation is discovered — the reputational consequence is permanent. What each discipline's reel shows: ANIMATION (character acting — a dialogue shot, a physical performance, a creature; the playblast preferred over a rendered version because it shows the animation; the planning and reference sometimes included), COMPOSITING (the before-and-after, the breakdown of layers, the key and roto quality shown at raised exposure, the integration — AV-8 CH 3's checklist demonstrated), FX (the sim layers broken out, the art direction shown, a variety of element types), LIGHTING (the same asset under several conditions, the plate matches with ball comparisons — AV-10 CH 4, the AOV work), MODELLING (turntables with wireframes, the topology shown, both organic and hard surface), RIGGING (the range-of-motion tests, the controls demonstrated, the deformation at extremes), and MATCHMOVE/ROTO (the accuracy shown — test geometry locked, mattes at exposure). The other materials: the PORTFOLIO site (simple, fast, work first), the CV (one page; software, credits, education — and the industry's tolerance for the self-taught, which is genuine), the ONLINE presence (the artist communities where work is posted and critiqued — a real recruitment channel), and the SCHOOL question (the specialist schools' value is real and their cost is a serious decision; the free-and-self-taught path is entirely viable in this field and is how many working artists arrived). The application: applying to the role that exists rather than the one you want, the STUDIO research, the cover note that is short and specific, the follow-up, and the recruiter relationship. The interview and the test: the technical and art tests some facilities set (the paid and unpaid question — an unpaid multi-day test is a real industry issue and worth knowing you may decline), the portfolio walkthrough (be able to explain every decision in every shot), and the collaboration questions (the industry hires for temperament as much as for skill because the work is done in teams under pressure). The realities: the project-based EMPLOYMENT (contracts of weeks or months; the gap between shows; the financial planning this requires — P's and A-12's freelance arithmetic, applied here), the CREDIT question (who gets credited and how — a live grievance in VFX), and the persistence the entry requires. The chapter's law: lead with your best shot, cut everything below your average, target one discipline, write an honest breakdown — and remember the reel is judged on its weakest moment, which means cutting is the skill.

The job that decides whether the work is possible. The two supervisors: the PRODUCTION-side VFX supervisor (hired by the film; present from prep through delivery; designs the approach with the director and DP, scopes and budgets the work, runs the on-set data capture (AV-7 CH 2), awards and manages the vendors, and is the film's technical conscience) and the FACILITY-side supervisor (runs the vendor's team on the show; the client's counterpart; responsible for the work's quality and the facility's margin) — two different jobs with the same title, and the relationship between them is where a show succeeds or fails. The supervisor's work in prep: reading the script and BREAKING IT DOWN (every shot that needs work, categorised and counted — the count sheet), the METHODOLOGY decision per sequence (practical, in-camera, volume (AV-11), full CG, or a hybrid — and the supervisor's most valuable contribution: "we can shoot that" saves more money than any pipeline optimisation), the BID process (CH's core — the shots described and sent to vendors, who estimate in days per department; the bids compared and negotiated; the AWARD), the SCHEDULE (the post period back-planned from the release date, with the shoot's overrun already assumed), and the on-set PLANNING with the AD and DP (P-5, CL). Budgeting: the SHOT as the unit (the bid built from days-per-department per shot, multiplied by rates, plus overheads, plus render and storage, plus a contingency), the COMPLEXITY tiers (the simple wire removal versus the hero creature shot — a factor of a hundred between them), the ASSET costs amortised across shots (a creature built once for forty shots), the RESEARCH-AND-DEVELOPMENT line for anything new (AV-9's novel solver), the OVERAGE mechanisms (the CHANGE ORDER — the shot that changed after award, which is how most overruns occur and the thing every vendor's business depends on being paid for), and the FIXED-BID structure (the industry's characteristic contract, in which the vendor absorbs the cost of iteration beyond the bid — the structural cause of CH 4's labour problems, stated here plainly). Running the show: the CADENCE (the weekly vendor calls, the dailies and client reviews — AV-7 CH 4), the NOTE (specific, actionable, and delivered with an understanding of what it costs — the supervisor who says "try something" has just spent four artist-days), the TRIAGE (as the deadline approaches: which shots get the remaining time, which are good enough, which are quietly simplified — the craft nobody teaches and everybody does), the relationship with EDITORIAL (the lock, the changes, the versions — AV-7 CH 4's largest waste), and the DELIVERY and DI attendance (CL-18, P-8). The supervisor's judgement: knowing what is achievable in the time and money available, protecting the artists from impossible notes while protecting the film from bad work, being honest about what is not working early rather than late, and the diplomatic core of the job — the supervisor talks to the director, the DP, the producer, the editor, five vendors and two hundred artists, and each conversation has a different vocabulary. The path to it: from artist to lead to CG supervisor to supervisor; the credit and the responsibility; and the honest note that it is a management job with a technical foundation, and that some of the best artists are miserable in it. The chapter's law: break it down and count it before you promise it, decide the methodology per sequence and ask what can be shot, bid in days per department with a contingency, know what every note costs — and be honest early.

The anime industry: the world's largest animation sector by output — the PRODUCTION COMMITTEE system (a consortium of publishers, broadcasters, distributors and toy companies financing a series and sharing rights, which spreads risk and leaves the animation STUDIO with a production fee rather than an ownership stake — the structural reason the industry's economics are as they are), the studio system (a few hundred studios, extensive subcontracting, the genga/douga apprenticeship), the LABOUR conditions (the widely documented low piece rates for inbetweeners, the long hours, the freelance precarity, the reporting on burnout and on the industry's own alarm about its pipeline of trained animators — a subject this track states plainly because the work it produces is among the medium's finest and the conditions that produce it are not defensible), the reform efforts and the unionisation attempts, and the streamers' money entering the system with mixed effects.
