PD-1 · Year 1 · Module One

The Designer's Role & Vision

The Trailer

The credit did not exist until 1939. William Cameron Menzies had storyboarded Gone with the Wind so completely — every shot drawn, the burning of Atlanta planned on paper before a frame was exposed — that David O. Selznick invented a title to describe what he had done: "production designed by." Before that, the job was "art director," a studio craftsman who built sets to order; after it, the production designer was the person who imagined the film's whole visible world and handed the director a plan for it. The department this track teaches was born of a credit written to honour a stack of drawings.

The job's other founding legend is a room that didn't exist. Ken Adam designed the War Room for Dr. Strangelove — a black triangle under a ring of light, a table like a poker game for the apocalypse — and the story that a newly inaugurated Ronald Reagan asked to be shown it, believing it real, is told by Adam himself and repeated so often the track files it as the trade's favourite apocrypha. Its truth is the module's thesis: a designed space can be more real to an audience than the real one, because it was built to MEAN something. This module is the designer's role — reading a script for the world it needs, writing the visual concept that the whole department will execute — and the department itself: the art director who runs it, the set decorator who furnishes it, the props master, the construction coordinator, and the crews who turn a drawing into a place an actor can walk into. The designer imagines the world. The department builds it. Learn the difference, and both jobs.

Two chapters: reading the script for design and the visual concept — the world the story needs, in one sentence and a hundred drawings; and the art department hierarchy — who does what, from designer to greens. Open the script. Then open the door.

Chapters

The designer reads the script three times, each for a different thing. The first read is for the STORY — what it feels like, what it's about, what world it lives in — without a pencil; the emotion the design must serve (the Cinematography track's CL-14 asks the DP the same question; the designer's answer is in space, surface and object). The second read is the BREAKDOWN: every location listed (interior and exterior, day and night, the number of scenes in each), every set implied but unnamed (the corridor between two rooms; the street outside the window), every period detail, every prop the script depends on, every character's environment — the list that becomes the set list (PD-12's budget), the schedule's spine, and the department's first argument with the producer. The third read is for the CONCEPT: the visual idea that will govern every choice — a sentence (the CL-14 discipline shared: "a world where nothing is new," "a house that watches," "a city built on its own ruins") — and the metaphors, contrasts and progressions the design will carry (the two worlds of a Pan's Labyrinth; the shrinking apartment of a marriage's collapse; the colour that arrives when a character does); the concept is tested against the director's vision (D-1's territory, met from the designer's chair) and becomes the brief the whole department works from. The designer as author of space: the job is not decoration but DRAMATURGY — where a character stands in a room is staging (D-3), and the room decides the staging; the door's position, the window's light (CL-12's alliance), the table's size all write the scene before the actor arrives. The war room principle: the designed space must be TRUER than the real one — built to mean, not to match; Adam's triangle told the audience about power and paranoia in a single wide shot, and a location that merely looked like a war room could not have. The deliverables of the concept: the concept statement, the research (PD-2), the first sketches (PD-3), and the conversation with director and DP (PD-10) that turns a sentence into a plan. The chapter's law: read for the world the story needs, not the world the script describes — the script says "a kitchen"; the designer decides whose.

The department, role by role. The production designer: head of department, hired by the director and producers, responsible for the film's whole visible world — sets, locations, props, and (in collaboration) costume and makeup — the concept's author and the department's face to the director and DP. The art director: the department's manager — runs the office, the budget, the schedule and the crews; turns the designer's drawings into working plans; supervises construction and decoration; on large films a SUPERVISING art director over several; the job the track's PD-12 teaches as a career in itself, and the historical name for the whole role before Menzies. The set designer / draughtsperson: produces the technical drawings (PD-4) — plans, elevations, sections — from the designer's sketches; the concept made buildable. The set decorator: furnishes the set — every object that isn't structure and isn't handled by an actor: furniture, drapes, lamps, the layers of a lived-in room (PD-6); runs the buyers and the swing gang; the credit that shares the Oscar with the designer, and the craft the SDSA exists to defend. The props master: everything an actor touches (PD-9) — hero props, weapons, food, paper — with the props department under them. The construction coordinator: builds the sets — carpenters, plasterers, painters, riggers — from the drawings, on the schedule, to the budget (PD-6). The scenic artist: paints and finishes surfaces — aging, faux marble, backdrops (PD-6). The greens: plants, trees, landscapes — the department that makes a stage a forest. The standby art director (UK) / on-set dresser: the department's representative on set during shooting, handling changes in real time. The concept artist and illustrator (PD-3), the model maker (PD-4), the graphic designer (PD-9's paper props), the storyboard artist, the art department coordinator and the art PAs — the office's first rung. The regional variations: the UK's art-director-heavy structure and standby role, the US's set designer classification, India's art director as the traditional head title (the production designer credit arriving later). The chapter's law: the designer imagines and the department builds — and the designer who cannot name every role, and what it costs, is designing a film no one can make.

PD-2 · Year 1 · Module Two

Research & Concept

The Trailer

Stanley Kubrick prepared Barry Lyndon by assembling a library: Gainsborough, Hogarth, Zoffany, Chardin — the paintings of the century the film was set in, catalogued so that every room, costume and candlelit table could be checked against what the period's own eyes had recorded. Ken Adam, who designed it, described the research as the film's true screenplay. Forty years later Hannah Beachler designed Wakanda for Black Panther from a bible of several hundred pages — a nation's architecture, transport and writing system derived from research into Zulu, Dogon, Basotho and Tuareg traditions — and Ruth Carter dressed it from the same sources. The invented country won the Oscar because its research was real.

Research is the designer's first craft and the one most often skipped. This module is about looking before drawing: the mood board that gathers a world's images, the period and cultural accuracy that separates a designed world from a costume party — and the ethics of that accuracy, which this track's worldwide mandate makes non-negotiable — and the worldbuilding that turns research into a coherent invented place with rules the audience can feel. Every great designed world in the screening rooms below was researched into existence; the question the module asks of every choice is where did you see that? — and the answer must never be "in another film."

Three chapters: visual research and mood boards — gathering the world; period and cultural accuracy — the truth of the details, and their ethics; and worldbuilding — the design look as a coherent invented place. Look first.

Chapters

Research is the difference between designing a world and remembering one. The sources: PRIMARY — paintings and photographs of the period (the era's own eyes; CL-13's period discipline shared), architecture (visited, measured, photographed — the research trip is the designer's location scout), vernacular photography (family albums, trade catalogues, street photographers — how people actually lived, not how they were painted), material culture (museums' furniture and objects), maps, newspapers, advertisements; SECONDARY — histories, memoirs, documentaries; and the source to distrust — OTHER FILMS, whose designs are already interpretations, and whose repetition is how a period becomes a cliché (the sepia trap of CL-13, in three dimensions). The research binder: organised by location, character and period, every image sourced and captioned (where, when, why it matters), growing through prep into the department's shared memory — the art director and decorator work from it as much as the designer. The mood board: the research CURATED into an argument — twenty images that agree (the CL-14 discipline: fewer, better), organised by set or by theme, with palette, texture and light implied, presented to director and DP as the concept made visible; the board is not a collage of everything found but a selection that defines what the world IS and, by omission, what it isn't. The research trip: the place itself — its light, scale, smell, the details no photograph carries (the door's height, the paint's grain) — measured and photographed for the drafting to come (PD-4). Research for the invented: the imaginary world researched as hard as the historical — Wakanda from real African traditions, the Nostromo from real industrial spaces — because invention that isn't rooted reads as generic (CH 3). The chapter's law: every image on the board must answer "where did you see that?" with a source that isn't a movie — and the board's edits are the concept's first decisions.

Accuracy is a craft and an ethic, and the module treats them as one. Period accuracy: the details that READ — a 1940s room is not a 1940s room because of one radio but because of its wallpaper's pattern, its light switch's shape, the newspaper's typeface, the photograph on the mantel's frame; the designer's research (CH 1) supplies the details, and the discipline is CONSISTENCY — nothing in the frame from after the story's date (the anachronism hunters are the audience's most alert members) and nothing from too far before unless the character would own it (a 1940s room contains 1920s furniture; a 1940s room built only from 1940s catalogues is a showroom). Legibility versus authenticity: the real 18th-century room was darker, dirtier and stranger than audiences expect; the designer chooses where to be true and where to be legible (Barry Lyndon's candlelit accuracy was a decision to be true and let the audience adjust), and the choice is a concept decision, not a compromise. Cultural accuracy: the worldwide mandate's design clause — a culture not the designer's own must be researched from its own sources, with its own people consulted (the CULTURAL CONSULTANT as a standing role on serious productions), its meanings understood (the colour that means mourning; the object that is sacred; the architecture that encodes hierarchy — CL-10's passports, in three dimensions); the failures are ORIENTALISM (the exotic assembled from surfaces — the "Asian" room made of a screen, a lantern and a fan; the "African" village made of whatever the studio had) and the PICTURESQUE (the poverty made pretty, the colony made charming); and the successes are Beachler's Wakanda — invented, but from the real traditions of many African peoples, credited — and Nitin Desai's Mughal palaces, built from the architecture's own grammar. The research into how people lived: class, region and decade in the same country differ more than the outsider's eye allows; the 1950s kitchen in Kolkata and in Kansas share a decade and nothing else; and the designer's honour is to know the difference. The chapter's law: the details are the truth, the truth is researched, and a world not your own is depicted from its own sources, with its own people in the room — or it is a costume party with a budget.

The invented world obeys rules the audience can feel. The principle: a designed world is coherent when every object, surface and space follows the same LOGIC — a history (how did this place get like this?), an economy (who made these things, from what?), a technology (what can it do, and what does its wear reveal?), and a culture (what do its people value, and how does that show?); Blade Runner's "retrofitting" (Syd Mead's term — old buildings with new technology bolted on, a city built on its own ruins) is a rule that generated ten thousand details; Star Wars' "used universe" (Ralph McQuarrie and the design team's dirt, dents and repairs) is another; and a world without rules is a pile of cool drawings. The design bible: the rules written down — the world's history, materials, palette, typography, transport, dress — before the sets are drawn, so that a hundred artists can build one world (the "Art of" books are the bibles published); for a small film, the bible is a few pages; for a franchise, hundreds. The look: the world's visual signature — the palette (PD-5), the silhouettes, the recurring forms, the light it's designed for (PD-10) — consistent enough that a single frame identifies the film and varied enough that the world feels inhabited. Scale of invention: the fully invented world (a future, a fantasy), the ALTERNATE world (our own with one rule changed — the retrofuture, the counterfactual), and the HEIGHTENED world (our own with its details tuned — the Wes Anderson or Jeunet register), each with its own research discipline (CH 1: invention rooted in the real). The comic and the graphic: worlds derived from illustration (The Fifth Element from Moebius and Mézières; Dick Tracy from the strip) and the translation problem — a drawn world's flatness made three-dimensional and walkable. The classic case: Metropolis — a city invented in 1927 from Manhattan's skyline and Expressionist geometry (PD-11), whose rules (verticality as class) every future city since has borrowed. The chapter's law: write the world's rules before drawing its rooms — and the rooms will design themselves from the rules.

PD-3 · Year 1 · Module Three

Drawing & Visualization

The Trailer

Ridley Scott storyboards his own films in a shorthand his crews call Ridleygrams — quick, dark, thumbnail frames that show the shot, the light and the set in a few strokes. Before Alien, he drew the Nostromo's corridors; before Blade Runner, the street. He was an art director before he was a director, and his drawings are why. Syd Mead, whom Scott hired for Blade Runner, drew futures so persuasively that clients bought them as products; Ralph McQuarrie's paintings sold Star Wars to a studio that could not read the script. The drawing is the designer's first language and the department's currency, and a designer who cannot draw is a writer who cannot spell.

This module is that language, taught as craft rather than talent: freehand sketching and the two perspectives that make a room believable on paper; the concept illustration that sells a world to a director, a producer and a hundred crew; and the continuity sketch and storyboard — the designer's version of the director's shot plan (D-2), where the set is drawn in the frame it will be photographed in. Menzies drew Gone with the Wind into existence; Saul Bass drew Psycho's shower; Harold Michelson drew The Birds. The designer thinks with a pencil. This module puts one in your hand and does not take it back.

Chapters

The hand first, then the geometry. Freehand: the thumbnail as the designer's thinking — fast, small, many (a page of twenty rooms beats one careful drawing), the sketchbook carried everywhere (the research trip drawn — PD-2), the habit of drawing what is SEEN (Edwards's contour and negative space: the chair's shape is the space around it) before what is imagined; line weight for depth (heavier near, lighter far), the figure for scale (a human silhouette in every room sketch — the door is only a door beside a person), and tone for light (the room's window drawn as the light it admits — CL-12's alliance begins in the sketchbook). One-point perspective: the horizon line at the viewer's eye height, a single vanishing point, all depth lines converging — the corridor, the room seen straight on, the symmetrical space (Kubrick's grammar, drawn); the construction: horizon, vanishing point, the front plane drawn true, depth lines to the point, measured depth by the diagonal method. Two-point perspective: two vanishing points on the horizon, the room seen from a corner, verticals true — the standard designer's view because it shows two walls and the volume between; the construction and its traps (points too close produce distortion — the wide-lens look, which is sometimes wanted). The camera in the drawing: eye height IS lens height (a low horizon is a low camera — the Ozu view; a high one a crane), the vanishing points' spread is the lens's width (CL-3's optics in a pencil), and the designer who draws in perspective is already framing the shot the DP will take (PD-10). Three-point and the tools: the third vanishing point for looking up or down; the grid, the ellipse, the shadow constructed from a light source; and the digital tools (perspective grids in drawing apps) that speed the geometry without replacing the understanding. PD-4 · Year 1 · Module Four

Drafting & Technical Design

The Trailer

Jacques Tati built a city. Tativille — the office blocks, streets and airport of Playtime — went up on a field outside Paris from drawings so complete that the buildings had working escalators, and so expensive that the film bankrupted him. The set was drafted like architecture because it was architecture; and every set that has ever been built, from a single wall to a city, has passed through the same documents: the ground plan that shows where the walls stand, the elevation that shows what they look like, the section that shows how they're built, and the scale that makes a drawing on a desk into a room a crew can raise.

This module is the designer's engineering. The sketches of PD-3 sell the world; the drawings here BUILD it — and between them sits the white card model, the small paper room in which the director walks a finger through the blocking, the DP finds the lens, and the designer discovers the door is on the wrong wall before construction has bought a single sheet of plywood. Digital drafting has made the plans faster and the models virtual; it has not made the discipline optional. A set is a drawing a carpenter believed. Draw it so they can.

Chapters

The set's four documents. The ground plan: the set from above — every wall, door, window, step and fixed piece of furniture in position, drawn to scale on the stage's floor plan (the stage's dimensions, doors and pillars shown, so the set fits and the crews can move around it); the plan is the department's master document: construction builds from it, the decorator lays out from it, the DP and director stage on it (CL-12's plot discipline — the DP's lights on your plan), and the camera positions (PD-3's storyboard) are drawn onto it to prove the set works. Elevations: each wall seen straight on — heights, openings, mouldings, the finishes noted — the drawing the carpenters and scenic artists build the wall from. Sections: the set cut through — how the walls are constructed (flats, their framing), how a ceiling piece hangs, how a staircase rises — the engineering drawing. Scale: the conventions (1:50 or 1:25 metric; ¼-inch or ½-inch to the foot imperial; the scale rule as the designer's constant companion), with dimensions in the trade's units and the set's real size checked against the actor (a door is 2 metres; a table 750 mm high; the human figure drawn on every elevation). The conventions: line weights (heavy for walls, light for furniture, dashed for above), symbols (doors' swings, windows, stairs' rise), the title block (set name, scene numbers, scale, date, revision), north arrow and camera positions, and WILD WALLS marked — the walls built to be removed for the camera (PD-3's storyboard tells you which). Film's differences from theatre: no proscenium, so the plan is 360 degrees; the set's height is decided by the lens's ceiling (PD-10), not the sightline; and the plan must show what ISN'T built (the window's view is a backing — PD-6; the corridor stops where the lens does). The chapter's law: a set exists when its plan, elevations and sections agree to the millimetre — and a carpenter who has to guess builds the guess.

Colour, Materials & Texture

The Trailer

Eiko Ishioka designed Mishima: A Life in Four Chapters as a colour system: the novelist's life in black and white, his novels in saturated stage colours — a golden temple, a pink and grey Kyoko's House, an orange-red Runaway Horses — each palette a chapter's whole meaning, built as sets so stylised they were declared theatre. Set Construction & Decoration

The Trailer

The ship was ninety percent to scale. Peter Lamont's Titanic rose in a tank in Rosarito, Mexico — the starboard side built full-size, the port side omitted because the camera would never see it, the whole thing rigged to tilt and sink on hydraulics — and the construction crew that built it was the size of a town. This module is the set MADE: what gets built and what gets found, what the decorator layers, and what the painter's brush does to a wall the camera will believe. The drawing was the promise. This is the room.

PD-7 · Year 2 · Module Seven

Costume Design

The Trailer

Year 2 opens on daylight stock with a sari. In 1983 Bhanu Athaiya became the first Indian to win an Academy Award, for the costumes of Gandhi — three decades of a nation's dress, from Kathiawari turbans to khadi, researched and sourced across a subcontinent for a film with three hundred thousand extras in one scene. She had begun as a painter in the Bombay Progressive Artists' Group and had already dressed Guru Dutt's and Raj Kapoor's films; the Oscar arrived for a craft she had practised for thirty years in an industry the Academy had barely noticed. Costume design is the art department's most intimate branch — the only design the audience sees on a body, moving — and its history is as global as the module's screening rooms.

The craft has its own founding legends: Edith Head's eight Oscars and her sketches for Hitchcock's blondes; Ruth Carter's Wakanda, the first Black winner in the category, built from the same research as Beachler's sets (PD-2); Emi Wada's colour-coded armies for Kurosawa's Ran; Sandy Powell, Colleen Atwood, Jenny Beavan and the generation who made the costume a character's whole biography. This module teaches the costume designer's process: reading the script for what each person wears and why, the silhouette that reads at distance and the period that must be researched not remembered, the sourcing and building that make a wardrobe, and the traditions across cultures that the worldwide mandate insists are the subject, not the spice. The set is where the story happens. The costume is who it happens to.

Four chapters: the script breakdown for character — the costume plot; silhouette and period — the costume as storytelling; sourcing and building — the costume department; and costume across cultures and eras. Daylight loaded. Fit the first look.

Hair & Makeup Design

The Trailer

In 1931 Jack Pierce spent hours each morning building Boris Karloff's flat skull and bolted neck from cotton, collodion and greasepaint — a design Universal copyrighted, so that the Frankenstein monster's face belongs to a makeup artist. Fifty years later Rick Baker won the first Academy Award for makeup for turning David Naughton into a wolf on camera, and Dick Smith — who had aged Dustin Hoffman to 121 and built Linda Blair's possession — ran a correspondence course from his home that trained a generation. Kazu Hiro made Gary Oldman into Churchill and then, having retired from prosthetics, came back for Bombshell. The face is the art department's smallest set and its most photographed, and the people who design it have their own Oscar, their own guild, and a history that runs from stage greasepaint to silicone.

The module's third chapter is its conscience. The Cinematography track told the Shirley-card story (CL-13) — film stock calibrated to a white reference — and makeup has its own version: foundation ranges that stopped at "medium," kits that could not match half the cast, and hair departments untrained for textured hair, until the advocacy of the past decade forced the change. Mia Neal and Jamika Wilson's Oscar for Ma Rainey's Black Bottom was the first for Black women in the category, and Camille Friend's Black Panther department wrote a syllabus in public. Makeup for all skin tones is not a specialism. It is the job, and the module treats it as such.

PD-9 · Year 2 · Module Nine

Props & Set Dressing

The Trailer

The Maltese Falcon was a prop: a lead statuette, several were made, and one sold at auction for four million dollars — "the stuff that dreams are made of," as the film's last line and its prop master could both attest. Annie Atkins, who designs the paper props for Wes Anderson's films, has described making a single pastry box for The Grand Budapest Hotel in a dozen versions until the one the camera saw was right; the audience never reads the label, and the label is why they believe the box. Props are the objects actors touch, and the props department is the art department's most intimate branch after costume: every letter, gun, cup, phone and briefcase that a character holds, in the number of copies the story needs.

The module's second chapter is its gravest. On the set of Rust in 2021, a cinematographer was killed by a prop firearm that should never have held a live round; on the set of The Crow in 1993, an actor died the same way. Weapons on set are the props department's heaviest responsibility, governed by the armourer, the safety bulletins and a protocol that exists because people died when it was skipped. The module teaches the craft — hero props and graphic props, weapons and specialty pieces, the prop master's department — and the safety as the craft's first clause. The prop is what the actor holds. Make it right, make it safe, and make six.

Three chapters: hero props and graphic props — the object designed; weapons and specialty — the armourer, the safety, and the practical effect; and the prop master's craft — the department, the truck, the continuity. Handle with care.

The prop is an object with a job in the story. The categories: the HERO prop (the one the camera sees closely and the story depends on — the Falcon, the briefcase, the ring: designed, built or found to the highest finish, in multiples for safety), the STUNT or ACTION prop (the duplicate that can be dropped, thrown or broken — the rubber knife, the breakaway bottle), the BACKGROUND prop (the objects that fill a world without being handled — the props department's share of the decoration, negotiated with the decorator: the line is whether an actor touches it), and the PERSONAL prop (the character's own — the watch, the wallet, the cigarette case — chosen as costume is, for biography). Chapters

The set exists only where the lens looks. The lens's field: a wide lens sees more of the set and exaggerates its depth (CL-3's optics from the designer's chair: the room that reads as vast on a 21mm is a room that must be built to its edges; the long lens compresses and sees a narrow slice — the set that can be a single wall); the designer asks the DP the lens set (CL-14's shot list overlay) before the set is drawn, because the set's SIZE is a lens decision. Camera height: the low camera sees the ceiling (Kane's muslin; Ozu's tatami view — the set needs a top), the high camera sees the floor (the floor becomes a surface to design — PD-6), and the eye-level camera sees neither, which is why studio sets historically had no ceilings and lights hung where they'd be. Forced perspective: the set built to lie to one camera position — Trauner's shrinking desks (PD-4), the hobbits' scale doubles (The Lord of the Rings' forced-perspective sets and moving rigs), Elf's oversized furniture and small-scale North Pole — the designer's oldest trick, and one that works only from the planned position; a step to the side breaks it. What needn't be built: the fourth wall the camera never turns to, the corridor that stops at the frame line, the second floor implied by a staircase's first six steps, the exterior seen only through a window (PD-6's backing) — the storyboard (PD-3) and the plan with camera positions (PD-4) are the designer's licence to leave things out, and the budget's largest saving. Movement: the set built for the camera's path (CL-11's moving master needs a room lit and dressed for 270 degrees; the dolly needs floor; the crane needs no ceiling where it rises; the oner needs wild walls timed to the move — PD-6 CH 1's Rope), and the set that must SURVIVE a move (the wall that flies between setups, the practical door the Steadicam passes through). The chapter's law: ask the lens, the height, the move and the ratio before drawing a wall — and then draw only the walls the answers require.

The Art Department in the Industry

The Trailer

Cedric Gibbons designed the Oscar. MGM's head of art for three decades, credited on some fifteen hundred films whether he'd drawn a line or not, he sketched the statuette in 1928 — a knight on a reel of film — and then won eleven of them. The Art Directors Guild was founded in 1937, in the studio era's shadow; today it is IATSE Local 800, and its members' credits run from the art PA who fetches the samples to the production designer whose name follows the cinematographer's in the crawl. The department's industry is old, organised, and expensive: the art department is typically a film's largest below-the-line spend after the cast, and its budget is a breakdown of every set, prop, costume and wig the script implies.

This final module is that industry: the breakdown that becomes a budget and a schedule, the path from the art office to the production designer's chair, and the art director's craft of running a department — the job PD-1 introduced as the department's manager and this module teaches as a career. Twelve modules ago you learned that the credit was invented for a stack of drawings. This module tells you what the drawings cost, who is paid to build them, and how to become the person whose name they carry. Design the world. Then budget it, schedule it, staff it, and strike it.

Three chapters: budgeting and scheduling the art department — the breakdown; the ADG path — guilds, societies, assistants and portfolios; and running a department as art director — the manager's craft. Roll credits. Then read the art department's section.

The job: PD-1 CH 2 named it — the department's manager: the office (coordinator, assistants, the research and drawing rooms), the BUDGET (CH 1's breakdown tracked daily against actuals — the art director knows what every set has cost so far and what it will cost to finish), the SCHEDULE (construction's calendar against the AD's shooting schedule — CH 1's backward planning, run live as both schedules move), the CREWS (construction, scenic, decoration, props, greens — coordinated through their heads; the art director's authority is over the plan, the coordinator's over the build), the VENDORS (the prop houses, the fabricators, the rental houses — the relationships that get the impossible piece by Thursday), and the DRAWINGS (the designer's sketches turned into buildable plans by the set designers, checked, revised, versioned — PD-4 CH 3's discipline as the art director's responsibility). The standby and the set: the on-set representative (the standby art director in the UK; the art director or on-set dresser in the US) handling the day's changes — the wall that must fly, the practical that must move, the director's new idea at 7 a.m. — with the authority to say yes, the judgment to say what it costs, and the crew to do it in minutes. The supervising art director: on large films, the manager of several art directors, each running sets or units; the department at the scale of a small company. Leadership: the department's culture set by its manager — the briefings that give the WHY with the what (CL-18's electric-department lesson, in the art office), credit distributed to the crews whose hands built the world, safety absolute (the construction site, the paint shop, the weapons — PD-9 CH 2), the calm that keeps a shop working when the schedule slips, and the honesty upward — the designer told what the drawing will cost, the producer told what the schedule can hold.
