E-1 · Year 1 · Module One

History & Theory of Editing

The Trailer

Sometime around 1918, in a Moscow workshop starved of film stock, Lev Kuleshov took a single close-up of the actor Ivan Mosjoukine - expressionless, borrowed from an old print - and cut it three times: against a bowl of soup, against a child in a coffin, against a woman on a divan. Audiences, he reported, praised the actor's hunger, his grief, his desire. The footage is lost; the accounts of it come from Kuleshov and Pudovkin decades later and disagree on details; the track flags the experiment as the trade's founding legend. What it demonstrated is not in doubt: meaning in cinema lives BETWEEN shots, and the person who chooses what comes next is writing the film a third time.

Everything in this track descends from that gap. Edwin Porter discovered, or stumbled into, cross-cutting; D.W. Griffith industrialised it into a grammar; Eisenstein turned it into a philosophy of collision and then a theory of intellectual montage where two shots could think; Vertov strapped a camera to the century and cut a city into a symphony. And against all of them, Hollywood built a system so smooth that audiences never noticed it was there - continuity editing, the invisible cut, the grammar this track's Year 1 will teach as craft and Year 2 will teach you to break. The cut is the only thing cinema invented. Learn where it came from before you make one.

Four chapters: from splicing to non-linear - the tools and their century; Soviet montage - Eisenstein, Pudovkin, Vertov; the Kuleshov effect - the legend, the evidence, the law; and continuity versus montage - the two grammars and their long argument. Load the reel.

Chapters

The tools shaped the grammar. The scissors era: early films were single shots until Porter's Life of an American Fireman and The Great Train Robbery (1903) joined scenes and, arguably, cross-cut - the versions dispute (was the rescue intercut or shown twice?) is the trade's first editing controversy; Griffith at Biograph turned cutting into a system - the close-up, the cutaway, the last-minute rescue's parallel action - and Intolerance (1916) braided four stories across centuries. Film was cut by hand with scissors and cement splices, viewed frame by frame against light. The machines: the Moviola (1924) - an upright viewer with a foot pedal, the editor's instrument for half a century, still in some rooms into the 1990s; the flatbed (Steenbeck, KEM) - horizontal, multi-track, quieter, the European standard and the 1970s American conversion; the synchroniser and the bins of hanging trims; the assistant's world of logging, coding and finding the shot. Videotape: linear tape editing - copying from source to record deck, no insertion without re-laying everything after - dominated television; the CMX 600 (1971) was the first non-linear system, a disk-based curiosity too expensive to matter. Non-linear: EditDroid (Lucasfilm, 1984) and Montage, laserdisc-based experiments; the AVID Media Composer (1989) - digitised footage, random access, the cut as a database - adopted through the 1990s until The English Patient (1996) won the first editing Oscar cut digitally, and Lightworks carried the European wing; then Final Cut Pro's democratisation, Premiere's return, Resolve's all-in-one, and the cloud - the tools E-3 teaches. What changed and what didn't: the cut became reversible (E-2's blink can be tried a hundred ways), footage became searchable, and the assistant's craft changed from physical to digital custody - but the decision, the gap between two shots, is exactly what Kuleshov found in 1918. The chapter's law: every tool since the scissors has made the cut cheaper to try and no easier to get right.

The revolution's film school theorised the cut as the cinema's essence. Kuleshov's workshop (CH 3's legend) trained a generation to think of shots as bricks and montage as the building. Pudovkin: LINKAGE - shots as units joined to build meaning smoothly, the cut as construction (his Mother assembling revolt from faces, ice and marching feet) - the school's classical wing, closer to what Hollywood would systematise. Eisenstein: COLLISION - meaning generated by the CONFLICT of adjacent shots, dialectically, a thesis and antithesis producing a synthesis in the viewer's mind; his five methods, ascending: METRIC (cut by absolute length regardless of content - a pulse), RHYTHMIC (cut by the content's movement - the Odessa steps' soldiers versus the fleeing crowd), TONAL (by the dominant emotional tone - Potemkin's mists), OVERTONAL (the interplay of all tones), and INTELLECTUAL - shots that produce an IDEA (October's Kerensky intercut with a mechanical peacock; the god sequence descending from Christ to idol): editing as argument, the montage of attractions as a machine for thought. Vertov: the KINO-EYE - the camera as a perfected organ and montage as its consciousness; Man with a Movie Camera cutting a day in the city into rhythms, the editor (Svilova, on screen at her bench) as the film's true author, superimpositions and freeze-frames declaring the cinema's material. Dovzhenko's lyric wing, and the school's suppression under socialist realism. The inheritance: every montage sequence (E-5), every video essay, every advertisement cut for meaning by juxtaposition descends from Eisenstein; every smooth construction from Pudovkin; every self-aware cut from Vertov. The chapter's law: the Soviets proved the gap between shots can THINK - and the rest of the century decided how often to let it.

The effect: viewers attribute to a face the emotion implied by the shot that follows it - the same neutral close-up reads as hunger, grief or desire depending on its neighbour - and, more generally, viewers infer spatial and causal relationships between shots that were never filmed together (Kuleshov's "creative geography": a man walks in Moscow, a woman in another city, they meet on a staircase that exists in neither, and look up at the White House). The evidence problem: the original footage is lost, the accounts are late and inconsistent, and modern replications find the effect real but smaller than the legend - audiences do read context into faces, measurably, but the "spellbound" reaction may owe something to the telling. The track's position: the LAW is true and the STORY is a story. Hitchcock's demonstration: on television, using his own face - a close-up, a woman with a baby (the kindly old man), the same close-up, a woman in a bikini (the dirty old man) - the effect explained by the director who built Rear Window on it. The law in practice: the REACTION SHOT (E-6) is Kuleshov applied - the listener's face reading whatever the line before it meant; the EYELINE and POV structure (E-4) is creative geography - a look, a cut, and the audience builds the room; the CUTAWAY carries meaning by adjacency; and the editor's power to change a performance in the cutting room (E-6's whole module) rests on the face's willingness to mean what its neighbours say. Pure cinema: Hitchcock's term for meaning made by juxtaposition rather than by what is in the shot - the track's first definition of editing as authorship. The chapter's law: a face on screen means what the next shot tells the audience it means - and the person who chooses the next shot has been handed the actor's performance to rewrite.

Two answers to the same question - how should shots relate? Continuity: the classical system, perfected in Hollywood by the 1920s and taught in E-4 as craft - the 180-degree rule keeping screen direction stable, the establishing shot then closer views, shot/reverse-shot for dialogue, the match on action hiding cuts inside movement, eyeline matches building space from looks, and the cut on the audience's interest so the join is INVISIBLE; its goal is a continuous fictional world the viewer never sees stitched, and its ethic (Bazin's later praise for the long take is the extreme form) is respect for the scene's space and time. Montage: the Soviet answer (CH 2) and its descendants - cuts that DECLARE themselves, meaning made by juxtaposition, time compressed or fractured, the viewer's mind as the site of the film; its goal is thought or feeling generated by the gap, and its ethic is the cinema's freedom from the theatre's continuous stage. The argument: Bazin against Eisenstein - the long take's ambiguity and respect for reality versus montage's manipulation (E-11 stages the debate in full); the New Wave's jump cuts as a third position (continuity broken on purpose to show the hand); and the modern synthesis Bordwell named INTENSIFIED CONTINUITY - the classical system's rules kept, but with faster cutting, closer framings, more camera movement and rack focus (the Bourne films' chaos is still continuity: axis honoured, eyelines matched, just at 1.5 seconds per shot). The craft judgment: most scenes are continuity; most sequences of time-passing or meaning-building are montage; and the editor's first decision on any scene is which grammar it speaks - the invisible cut that serves performance, or the visible cut that serves an idea. The chapter's law and the module's: continuity hides the editor; montage reveals the editor; and the great ones know exactly when to be seen.

E-2 · Year 1 · Module Two

Why Cuts Work

The Trailer

Walter Murch noticed it while cutting The Conversation: Gene Hackman blinked at the exact moments Murch wanted to cut - and when Murch cut where Hackman blinked, the cuts felt right. Years earlier, John Huston had told an interviewer that the blink is the mind's own cut, the punctuation between one thought and the next; Murch put the two together and wrote the trade's shortest, most useful book. In the Blink of an Eye argues that a cut works when it happens where the audience would have blinked anyway - at the end of a thought, on the turn of an emotion - and that the editor's job is to find those moments, because the audience will feel every one that is missed.

The book's other gift is a list. Murch's Rule of Six ranks what a cut must serve, with percentages he admits are half-serious: emotion first, by a wide margin; story second; rhythm third; and only then the technical concerns - where the eye is looking, the two-dimensional plane, the three-dimensional space - that film schools teach first. An editor who satisfies the top of the list and breaks every rule at the bottom has made a good cut; one who satisfies the bottom and misses the top has made a smooth mistake. This module is the trade's theory of the cut itself - why one frame is right and its neighbour wrong - built on Murch, on the physiology of blinks and attention, and on the eye-tracking science that has since confirmed most of what he guessed. A cut is a blink the film makes for you. Learn where you'd blink.

Three chapters: the Rule of Six - emotion over technique; the blink as cut-point - thought's punctuation; and eye-trace - where the audience is looking, and cutting to meet them there. Feel the frame.

Chapters

The list, and why its order matters. Emotion (51%): does the cut serve what the audience should FEEL at this moment? - the only criterion that outweighs all the others combined, and the one beginners rank last because it is the hardest to name. Story (23%): does it advance the story - reveal, withhold, clarify, complicate? Rhythm (10%): does it land where the scene's pulse wants it - the beat, the breath, the phrase (E-5's whole module)? Eye-trace (7%): does it respect where the audience is looking (CH 3)? Two-dimensional plane of screen (5%): does it honour the axis - the 180-degree line, screen direction (E-4)? Three-dimensional space of action (4%): is the spatial relationship between people and objects consistent? The method: the ideal cut satisfies all six; when it can't, SACRIFICE FROM THE BOTTOM - break the axis before you break the rhythm, break the rhythm before you break the story, and never break the emotion to save the geography; the technical rules exist to serve the top of the list and are worthless when they don't. The corollary: a scene edited for perfect continuity that leaves the audience unmoved has failed by Murch's arithmetic, and a scene that crosses the line three times to keep a performance alive has succeeded - which is why the greatest editors' work is full of "mistakes" no one notices. The practice: watch dailies for the moment that MOVES you and cut toward it; ask of every cut what feeling it produces before asking whether it matches; and audit your assemblies against the list - most weak cuts fail at the top, not the bottom. The chapter's law: technique is the servant; emotion is the master; and the percentages are a reminder of how lopsided that relationship should be.

The physiology first: humans blink not only to wet the eye but at the BOUNDARIES OF THOUGHT - at the end of a sentence, after a decision, when attention shifts - and, remarkably, audiences watching the same film blink in synchrony at the same moments, as if the film were conducting them. Murch's claim: a good cut lands where the audience's blink would - at the natural boundary of an idea or emotion - so the cut is perceived as their own punctuation rather than the film's interruption; a bad cut interrupts a thought in progress, and the audience feels it as a stumble without knowing why. The actor's blink: performers blink at their character's thought-boundaries (Huston's observation), which is why "cut on the blink" is more than a rule of thumb - the actor has marked the beat for you; the practical corollary is to watch for the blink in dailies as a candidate cut-point, and to notice when an actor DOESN'T blink (the unnerving stare that marks a character as alien or predatory - a performance choice editors protect). Leading and lagging: the cut can arrive exactly on the boundary, a few frames EARLY (the audience pulled forward - urgency, anticipation), or a few frames LATE (the audience allowed to settle - reflection, weight); the difference is measured in frames, and the whole craft of CH 2 is the decision between them, shot by shot; Pepperman's manual trains the frame-level eye. The dialogue cut: cutting on the end of a line is the beginner's default; cutting on the THOUGHT - before the line ends, when the listener has already understood, or after it, when the speaker realises what they've said - is the professional's; and the reaction cut (E-6) lands where the listener's thought turns. The action cut: the match on action (E-4) works because movement itself is a blink-mask - the eye tracks the motion across the join and misses the cut. The chapter's law: the film blinks for the audience; the editor decides when; and the decision is always a few frames wide.

The audience's attention has a position on the screen, and the cut either meets it or makes it travel. The science: eye-tracking studies (Smith's work, and the visualisations that followed) show that viewers of the same film look at nearly the same points - faces, moving objects, the brightest or most contrasted region - with a synchrony that makes the editor's job possible: if you know where they're looking at the end of shot A, you can place the point of interest in shot B at the same screen position and the cut vanishes, or displace it and force a saccade the audience will feel as a jolt. E-4 · Year 1 · Module Four

Continuity & Coverage

The Trailer

In Pulp Fiction, when Jules and Vincent enter Brett's apartment, the bullet holes are already in the wall behind them - the shots that will miss them minutes later have been fired in the wrong order by the schedule, and the script supervisor's log did not save the wall. The error is famous because it is rare: the continuity system works so well that audiences have been trained for a century not to look, and a mismatched glass or a jumped eyeline is noticed only when the scene has already lost them. Continuity is not the point of editing. It is the floor the point stands on.

The system was built by directors and codified by editors: the axis of action that keeps two people looking at each other across a cut, the match on action that hides the join inside a gesture, the eyeline that lets a look build a room, and the master-and-coverage method by which a scene is shot several times from several angles so the editor can choose. Dede Allen, who cut Bonnie and Clyde, called the rules "grammar" and spent a career bending them for feeling; Anne V. Coates, who cut Lawrence of Arabia, made the most famous match cut in cinema by removing a dissolve. This module teaches the grammar first - action, direction, the match, the eyeline - and then the daily labour of assembling a scene from what the director shot, which is where every editor's career actually begins. Learn the rules as a language, not a law. You will break them; you should know when.

Chapters

The two rules that keep a scene's world stable across cuts. The axis: the 180-degree line - an imaginary line through the two subjects of a scene (or along a movement's path) - with all cameras kept on one side so that screen positions and directions stay consistent: A looks right, B looks left, and the cut between them preserves the sense that they face each other; CROSSING the line reverses the geography and the audience feels the room flip; the exceptions are a shot ON the line (neutral), a visible camera move across it (the crossing shown, so accepted), a cutaway that resets it, or a deliberate reversal for disorientation (a fight, a confrontation's turn). Screen direction: movement keeps its direction across cuts - a chase running left-to-right stays left-to-right through every angle, or the pursuer appears to have turned around; a NEUTRAL shot (movement toward or away from camera) lets the direction change; and the convention runs deep enough that geography can be lied about (travelling "west" in a Western means moving screen-left for a century of audiences). The match on action: a cut placed IN a movement - the hand reaching, the body turning, the door swinging - with the action continuing seamlessly in the next shot, the motion masking the join (E-2's blink logic in the body); the craft is overlapping action on set (the actor repeats the movement in each angle) and choosing the cut-point inside it - usually just after the movement begins, so the next shot carries its momentum; and the discipline is matching speed, position and detail (the glass in the same hand, the head turned to the same degree) so the eye sees one gesture. The lined script: the script supervisor's on-set record of every angle's coverage of every line, plus continuity notes - the map the editor reads before cutting a frame. The chapter's law: the axis keeps the world facing the right way; the match on action lets you cut inside it invisibly - master both, and every rule-break in Year 2 will be a choice.

Two cuts that do more than continue. The match cut: a join that connects two shots by a shared shape, movement, colour or idea - the graphic match (a bone thrown becomes a satellite; a flame blown out becomes a desert sunrise), the movement match across contexts (a spin in one place continued in another), the conceptual match (an image answered by its rhyme) - continuity's tool turned into meaning: the audience reads the resemblance as a claim (evolution in one cut; a life's leap; a hidden connection); the craft is the shapes' alignment (screen position, scale, motion - E-2's eye-trace at its most exact) and the restraint to use it where the rhyme means something. E-5 · Year 1 · Module Five

Rhythm & Pacing

The Trailer

Lou Lombardo cut The Wild Bunch's final gunfight from footage shot at several frame rates - slow motion intercut with normal speed, some 3,600 cuts in a film whose contemporaries averaged a few hundred - and Sam Peckinpah's ballet of death became the most influential action sequence of its decade because its RHYTHM was new: time stretched and snapped, the eye given a beat and then robbed of it. Fifteen years earlier, at the other pole, Ozu had cut Tokyo Story at a pace so steady that critics called it a heartbeat. Neither editor was fast or slow. Both were exact.

Rhythm is the third item on Murch's list and the one editors talk about most, because it is the one they can least explain: the beat a cut lands on, the tempo a sequence breathes at, the acceleration that makes a chase a chase and the pause that makes a death a death. Karen Pearlman's Cutting Rhythms - this module's required text - argues that "cutting by feel" is a trained physical intuition, learned in the body and improvable by practice. This module trains it: tempo, the montage sequence that compresses time, the cross-cut that braids it, and the dramatic build that makes a scene arrive. Every cut is a beat. The editor is the drummer the audience never sees.

Four chapters: tempo - shot length, acceleration, and the beat; the montage sequence - time compressed with meaning; parallel action and cross-cutting - braiding time for suspense; and the dramatic build - escalation, withholding, and the arrival. Count it in.

Chapters

Tempo is the rate of cuts, and the rate is a feeling. The measure: AVERAGE SHOT LENGTH - the century's curve running from ten-second shots in the studio era to two or three in the modern blockbuster (Cinemetrics' data), with the caveat that a film's feel depends less on the average than on the VARIATION: a sequence of equal-length shots reads as mechanical (Eisenstein's metric montage - a pulse), one of shortening shots reads as acceleration, one of lengthening as release. The beat: the cut lands on a pulse the audience feels without counting - on a line's end or thought (E-2), on a movement's impact, on a musical bar (E-9), on the scene's own breathing - and Pearlman's insight is that this pulse is PHYSICAL: editors feel it in the body, and the training is to notice where your own attention wants the next thing. Acceleration and deceleration: the chase that starts with five-second shots and ends with half-second ones; the death that starts fast and slows until a single shot holds; the trade's most reliable tool for tension is the shortening interval, and its most reliable tool for weight is the lengthening one - and the art is the CURVE, never a constant. Holding: the shot held past comfort - the pause the audience must sit in - is tempo's most powerful move and the beginner's greatest fear; a cut that arrives late on purpose makes the audience wait with the character, and a film that never holds has no way to make silence matter. Tempo across the film: sequences have tempos; films have trajectories of tempo (Pearlman's cycles of tension and release) - the fast sequence means nothing without the slow one before it, and the editor plans the film's rhythm as a composer plans movements. Frame rate as tempo: slow motion and speed ramps (The Wild Bunch's intercut rates; CL-15's Hong Kong crank) change the tempo inside the shot - a tool this module's genre chapter (E-8) develops. The chapter's law: tempo is the shape of the intervals, not their average - and the great editors are known by their curves.

Editing Performance

The Trailer

Year 2 opens on daylight stock in a room where an actor is being built from parts. Thelma Schoonmaker has described watching every take of every angle of a De Niro scene and assembling the performance the audience sees from a look in take three, a line in take seven, and a silence the actor gave only once - a Frankenstein the actor never played and would recognise instantly, because every piece was his. The editor is the last director of the performance, and the kindest: the actor's worst takes will never be seen, and the moment they got right only once will be the one that lasts.

The module's laws come from the faces. Kuleshov's law (E-1) means the listener's face can carry a scene the speaker's can't; the reaction shot - cinema's most underestimated unit - is where the editor writes the audience's feeling by choosing whose face receives the line. And comedy, the most unforgiving test of the cut, lives or dies in frames: the beat before the punchline, the hold after it, the whip-pan that IS the joke. Edgar Wright's editors have described cutting a comedy to a metronome that only the audience's laughter can confirm. You are not cutting shots. You are cutting people - and the audience will forgive a bad match long before a dead face.

The performance the audience sees is chosen, not recorded. Watching dailies: every take of every angle (E-4's discipline, now for a different purpose) - looking for the moment that is TRUE (the flicker before the line, the breath after, the take where the actor forgot the camera), noting where each take is alive and where it dies, and building selects by MOMENT rather than by take (the best take rarely exists; the best moments are scattered). The eyes rule: the audience watches the eyes; a take with a technical flaw and living eyes beats a perfect take with dead ones; and the editor's first question of any shot is whether the eyes are thinking. The Frankenstein: a performance assembled across takes and angles - the line from one, the reaction from another, a gesture from a third - held together by the grammar (E-4's matches and eyelines), by sound (S-5's dialogue editor smoothing the joins), and by the editor's ear for a CONSISTENT INNER LIFE: the assembled performance must be one person's, and the tell of a bad Frankenstein is an emotional discontinuity, not a visual one. Comedy is editing's most unforgiving test because the audience grades it out loud. The beat: a joke has a setup, a pause and a punch, and the pause is the editor's - too short and the punch arrives before the audience is ready; too long and it's telegraphed; the right beat is measured in frames and found by screening to people who don't know the joke. The stages, and what each is for. E-8 · Year 2 · Module Eight

Editing by Genre & Form

The Trailer

Jackie Chan's editors will show you the same hit twice. A fall through a table, a kick that lands - cut from one angle, then again from another, the impact repeated so the audience feels it doubly and the timing (shot a few frames under speed, so playback is quicker) reads as superhuman fact. It breaks every rule of continuity in Year 1 and is exactly right, because action editing is a genre with its own grammar, built in Hong Kong from the crank and the constructive cut. Frederick Wiseman, at the other end of cinema, shoots a hundred hours for every one he keeps and spends a year in the room finding the film that the institution he filmed didn't know it was; his cut has no script and no narration, and its structure is invented entirely from the footage.

Every form has its own editing. The musical cuts to bars; the Indian song sequence cuts to the playback the actors mimed to; the dialogue drama cuts to the face (E-6) and the walk-and-talk's tempo; the documentary cuts toward a story no one wrote. This module is four grammars - action, documentary, the song, the dialogue scene - each an argument that the craft of Year 1 is a foundation, not a style. The rules are the same. The grammar is what you do with them when the film knows what it is.

Four chapters: action - Hong Kong's kinetic cutting and the undercrank; documentary - finding story in footage; the musical and the song sequence - cutting to the bar; and dialogue-driven drama - the face, the tempo, the room. Roll the genre.

Chapters

Action editing has two schools and the module teaches both. The Hong Kong school: LEGIBILITY first - the fight shown so the audience sees the technique (D-9's staging rule from the cutting room's side): wider shots held longer than Hollywood dares, the CONSTRUCTIVE EDIT (a hit shown from two angles, the impact repeated - continuity broken for feeling, E-2's Rule of Six applied to violence), UNDERCRANKING (shot at 20–22 frames, played at 24, the speed added in the camera and the cut timed to the altered clock - CL-15's chapter, from the bench), the PAUSE–BURST–PAUSE rhythm (stillness, explosion, stillness - the cut rate dropping to zero, then spiking, then resting, so the audience can read each exchange), and the rhythm of the exchange itself - a fight cut like a conversation, blow and answer. The Hollywood school: coverage and speed - the fight assembled from many angles cut fast (intensified continuity, E-1), chaos as immersion (the Bourne method: the audience feels the fight rather than reads it), with its virtue (visceral confusion) and its vice (the fight no one can follow, cut to hide that no one could fight). The synthesis: the modern clean action film (John Wick's long takes and clear geography - the Hong Kong lesson relearned by Hollywood stunt teams) cut for legibility with Western polish. The craft points: cut on the impact (E-2's transient logic), let the wide breathe when the performer is real, use the repeat for the hits that matter, build the fight's tempo like a chase (E-5), and never let the cut do what the performer couldn't. The chapter's law: action is cut to be READ - the fight the audience can't follow was never a fight.

The Trailer

The picture editor's sound grammar. The split edit: the J-CUT (sound leads - the next scene's audio begins under the current picture, so the cut arrives already heard) and the L-CUT (sound lags - the current audio continues under the next picture, so the leaving scene lingers), the talking picture's native tools (E-8's dialogue chapter): a listener's face receiving a line whose speaker we've left, a scene entered through its noise before its image; Allen's audio-first habit generalised into the trade's default - most dialogue cuts are split, and the straight cut (sound and picture together) reads as emphasis. Chapters

The tools, and the theory they assume. The axes: hue, saturation, luminance (CL-10's three dials) - every grade a movement on three axes, and the scopes (waveform for luminance, vectorscope for hue and saturation, the parade for channel balance - CL-4's instruments, now yours) the arbiter, because the eye adapts and lies (CL-2's constancy is the colourist's enemy). Primaries: corrections applied to the whole image - LIFT (shadows), GAMMA (midtones), GAIN (highlights) as the classical three-way, the log wheels' offset/contrast/pivot alternative for log footage, CURVES for precise tonal shaping, and the order of operations: balance first (neutral where neutral should be - white balance corrected, the black point set, the exposure normalised so every shot starts from the same place), then contrast, then colour; a grade built on an unbalanced shot is a look built on sand. Secondaries: corrections applied to PART of the image - QUALIFIERS (keys pulled on a hue, saturation or luminance range: the sky, the skin, the one red coat), WINDOWS (power windows - shapes masking a region: the face brightened, the wall dropped, the vignette), TRACKING (the window following the face through the shot), and the LAYERING of nodes so each secondary is isolated and reversible. Skin: the colourist's first responsibility (CL-13's chapter from the grade's side) - the vectorscope's skin line, the qualifier that isolates faces so the look can be pushed elsewhere without touching them, and the diplomacy of a room where every face on screen is someone the film needs the audience to believe. Theory in the grade: complements and harmonies (CL-10 CH 2) as the colourist's palette decisions - the teal-orange hegemony born in this chair, and the discipline to choose it rather than default; saturation discipline; the restricted palette as the grade's most reliable route to "cinematic." The chapter's law: balance, then contrast, then colour; scopes over eyes; skin before everything - and every secondary a decision the DP will ask you to justify.

E-11 · Year 2 · Module Eleven

World Editing Traditions

The Trailer

Breathless was too long. The story - told by Godard and retold so often the track flags its details - is that Jean-Pierre Melville advised him to cut not whole scenes but the middles of shots, and the result was the jump cut: a shot resuming a few seconds later with the actor in a new position, the edit declaring itself, continuity's founding taboo turned into a style. Fifty years earlier, André Bazin had argued that the long take and deep focus respected reality in a way montage never could; a decade later, Ozu was cutting to a vase for six seconds while a daughter wept offscreen. Every cinema on earth has its own answer to the question of when to cut, and this module visits four of them.

The worldwide mandate is the track's spine, and this is where it lands hardest: the long-take-versus-montage debate that shaped film theory; the New Wave's jump cuts that shaped every music video since; the ellipsis in Ozu and Bresson - the cut that omits the event and shows its aftermath - that shaped a century of art cinema; and the rhythm of Indian commercial cinema - the interval as structure, the mass moment as cut, the song's clock - that shaped the world's largest audience and is almost never taught. Continuity is one tradition. The others are not mistakes.

The debate that shaped film theory, staged for the cutting room. Bazin's position: the long take and deep focus (Welles, Renoir, Wyler) preserve the ambiguity and continuity of reality - the viewer chooses where to look, time is not fractured, the world is respected - while montage (E-1) imposes meaning, fragments space and tells the viewer what to think; the ethic is humility before the real. Eisenstein's position (E-1 CH 2): the cut is cinema's essence - meaning made by collision, the viewer's mind the site of the film - and the long take is theatre photographed. The synthesis the trade actually practises: most films cut most of the time (continuity, E-4), and choose the long take for specific reasons - performance (the actor allowed to build without interruption; E-6), space (the geography shown whole; D-3's staging), time (real duration as tension or meditation), and DECLARATION (the oner as a statement - Children of Men's car ambush, Russian Ark's museum, the stitched oners of Birdman and 1917 whose invisible cuts are the module's joke on the debate: a long take made of cuts). The editor's role in the long take: choosing WHICH take (the oner is one shot, many takes - the selection is the edit), deciding where it begins and ends (the trim is the cut), and, in the stitched oner, hiding the joins (E-4's match on action at its most demanding - a pan across a wall, a body crossing the lens). The modern critique: the oner as spectacle for its own sake, and the answer - the long take earns itself when cutting would spend something the scene needs. The world's long-take traditions: Tarr's durations, Tarkovsky's, the Taiwanese masters (Hou, Tsai), Angelopoulos - cinemas where the cut is rare and therefore enormous. The chapter's law: the long take is an editing decision - the decision not to cut - and it is only as strong as the cut you refused.

Continuity's founding sin, made a grammar. The jump cut: a cut between two shots of the same subject from the same (or nearly the same) angle, so the subject appears to jump - the continuity system's prohibition (E-4: change the angle by thirty degrees or the join shows), and Breathless's method: whole scenes shortened by cutting the middles of shots, Belmondo and Seberg jumping through Paris, the edit declaring the film a made thing. The New Wave's cut: the jump cut as one tool among many - Truffaut's freeze frame (The 400 Blows' ending, the cut that stops), Resnais's memory-cuts (Hiroshima Mon Amour's flashes of a lover's hand becoming a dead soldier's - the cut as involuntary memory), Varda's real-time (Cléo's ninety minutes structured by clocks and chapters), Godard's inserts, titles and direct address - a generation of editors (Cécile Decugis, Agnès Guillemot, Henri Colpi) cutting against the grammar the studios had perfected, because the grammar had become the studios. What the jump cut means: TIME compressed and shown to be (the scene's dead middle gone, the audience told it's gone), NERVOUSNESS and energy (the jitter of a subject unable to sit still), ALIENATION (the film as artifact, the viewer as viewer - Brecht by way of the cutting room), and, after the New Wave, a REGISTER - the jump cut as the sound of youth, documentary honesty, the confessional, the vlog. The inheritance: the music video's whole grammar, the American indie of the 1990s (Soderbergh's jump-cut experiments), the modern television confessional, and the discipline that the jump cut is now a choice like any other - used for meaning, or it's a mistake again. The editor's craft in the jump cut: which frames to remove (the cut lands on the subject's stillness so the jump reads as time, not as error), how many per scene (one is a jolt; five are a rhythm; fifty are a style), and whether the audience is meant to notice (Breathless wanted them to; a modern interview doesn't). The chapter's law: the jump cut is continuity's rule broken with a purpose - and the purpose is what the New Wave added to the grammar forever.

The most powerful cut is the one that leaves the event out. The ellipsis: the omission of an action, a scene, a climax - the cut from before to after, the audience given the cause and the consequence and left to build the middle; every film uses it (the walk not shown, the night skipped), and two masters made it a philosophy. Ozu: the wedding never shown (Late Spring's daughter dressed, then the father alone - the event omitted, the aftermath the film); the PILLOW SHOTS (Bordwell's "intermediate spaces" - a vase, a corridor, a train, held between scenes as the ellipsis made visible: time passing, the world continuing, the cut's gap given a picture; S-11's ma from the bench), the 360-degree space that refuses the axis (E-4's rule ignored on principle - Ozu's characters look "wrong" across cuts and the audience adjusts within a film), the low camera and the matched framings that make cutting feel like breathing, and a rhythm so even that grief arrives without announcement. Bresson: show the effect, not the cause - the hands that do the deed while the face is elsewhere (Pickpocket's thefts as choreography of wrists), the cut away from the event to its trace (a door closing, a sound), the "model's" blank face given meaning by the cut (Kuleshov's law as Bresson's whole method - E-1), and the ethic that cinema is writing with omissions: what is left out is what the audience must supply, and supplying it is the film's meaning. The ellipsis as world tradition: the Iranian masters (Kiarostami's offscreen events), the Dardennes' cuts away from violence, Haneke's withheld climaxes, the Taiwanese new cinema's gaps - a global art cinema built on the cut that doesn't show. The craft: choosing the last shot before the omission (the moment that implies), the first shot after (the trace that confirms), and the length of the gap (a pillow shot's duration is the ellipsis's weight); and the discipline to trust the audience with the middle. The chapter's law: the cut that omits makes the audience the author of the event - and the traditions that mastered it made cinema's most devastating scenes out of what they refused to film.

The film becomes a file. The final conform: the locked cut rebuilt at full resolution (E-3's online), graded (E-10), the sound's printmasters received (S-8's stems and masters), titles and credits laid in, subtitles and localisation versions prepared - the picture, sound and text married into the MASTER. The DCP: the Digital Cinema Package that replaced the 35mm print - the DCI specification's frames (JPEG 2000, at the theatrical resolution, in the XYZ colour space converted from the P3 grade), the audio (uncompressed, in the theatrical channel layout - S-8's printmaster), the subtitles as a separate track, the whole wrapped in MXF and delivered on a drive or by network; and the KDM - the key that unlocks an encrypted DCP for a specific projector for a specific window - the reason a cinema can't play a film it wasn't sent the key for. The other masters: the IMF (Interoperable Master Format) for streaming - a component-based package from which each platform's version is rendered, with its own colour, audio and subtitle specifications; the broadcast masters; the ProRes or DNx mezzanine; the archival masters (the uncompressed grade, the stems, the project files) that let the film be re-versioned in a decade. QC: every master checked - sync (S-4's vow, tested one last time), dropped frames, colour and audio against specification, subtitles against timing, the DCP ingested and played on a real server before it ships; the QC report as the delivery's proof. Versions: the territories' cuts, the airline version, the accessibility tracks (descriptive audio, closed captions), the dubbed versions (S-8's M&E finally used) - each a delivery with its own checklist.
