D-1 · Year 1 · Module One

The Director's Role & Vision

The Trailer

October 1950. The Directors Guild of America is tearing itself apart over a McCarthy-era loyalty oath, and Cecil B. DeMille's faction is trying to unseat the Guild's president. After hours of speeches, an old man in the back stands up and begins: "My name's John Ford. I make Westerns." The room goes silent - because everyone knows exactly what those five words contain: four Best Director Oscars and total moral authority. He then defends his opponent, buries DeMille's coup in two minutes, and sits down. That is the strange power of this job: a director is only ever as large as the films behind them - and yet on a set, their conviction is the gravity everything else orbits.

What does a director actually do? Kurosawa gave the honest, unglamorous answer: with a good script, a good director can produce a masterpiece, and a mediocre one can still make a passable film - "but with a bad script even a good director can't possibly make a good film." The director doesn't generate most of the raw material. The writer wrote it, the actors embody it, the cinematographer lights it, the editor assembles it. What the director owns is the thing nobody else can hold: the whole - the single unified answer to Sidney Lumet's first-morning question, the one he asked before choosing a lens, a coat, or a doorknob: "What is this movie about?" Not the plot. The theme. Lumet swore that every craft decision on his films - camera height, costume palette, cutting rhythm - was downstream of that one sentence.

And here is the paradox this module opens with: the director as visionary author is a recent invention. For decades the job was a craft position - brilliant employees like Michael Curtiz shooting a hundred studio assignments (screaming "Bring on the empty horses!" in glorious broken English on the set of a cavalry picture) and somehow leaving us Casablanca. Then critics decided directors were authors, and the job changed forever - twice.

Before you design a single shot, you must be able to say what the film is about in one sentence, and why you - specifically you - are the person who has to make it. This module is about earning that sentence.

Chapters

A script is not a film; it is sheet music. Interpretation is the director's first act of authorship - and it starts with separating three things students constantly blur. Plot is what happens. Premise is the engine underneath - the arguable proposition the story tests (Lajos Egri's formulation: "great love defies even death" is Romeo and Juliet's premise, provable by its events). Theme is what the film is finally about at the level of human meaning - Lumet's one sentence. When Lumet made Dog Day Afternoon, he decided the theme was that "freaks" are not the freaks we think they are - ordinary people pushed to extremity - and that single decision dictated a documentary shooting style, no musical score, and real summer sweat. Same script, different theme sentence, and you'd have a heist thriller instead of a tragedy.

Read Alongside
Making Movies - Lumet, "The Script" and "Style" chapters

Lumet names the theme of a dozen of his own films in one-liners and shows the style each dictated. The most concrete demonstration in print of interpretation becoming craft.

Then comes the director's pass: reading the script the way a conductor reads a score. Beat by beat, scene by scene, you annotate - what is this scene for, what changes across it, whose scene is it, what must the audience feel at its end that they didn't feel at its start? Coppola famously built The Godfather inside a hand-made "prompt book" - the novel dismembered and pasted into a giant binder, every scene annotated for its core ("the times," "imagery and tone," "pitfalls" - including his recurring margin note warning himself when a scene risked cliché). That binder, he has said, was more useful on set than the screenplay.

Read Alongside
The Godfather Notebook - Francis Ford Coppola

The prompt book itself, published in facsimile. Watching Coppola argue with his own material in the margins teaches script interpretation better than any manual - you see vision being manufactured, page by page.

Tone is the final interpretive commitment: the contract you sign with the audience about how to feel. Comedy or dread from the same events is a directing decision, and tone, once set, is brutally expensive to change. The chapter's discipline: write your theme sentence, your premise, and three tone adjectives before any visual planning. Everything in D-2 onward assumes you have them.

The director's statement is the most despised document in filmmaking and the most secretly useful. Festivals require it, funds require it, producers request it - a page or two answering, in your own voice: why this story, why now, why you, and how will it look and feel? Students treat it as marketing homework. Working directors know better: being forced to articulate the vision in prose is a diagnostic. If the statement is vague, the vision is vague, and the shoot will find that out expensively. The standard anatomy: the personal connection (what wound or obsession makes this yours), the relevance (why an audience needs it now), the approach (visual language, tone, performance style, sound - in specifics, not adjectives), and the feasibility whisper underneath it all (this person can actually deliver this film).

Read Alongside

Read the actual instructions from the institutions that will read yours - what they ask for, what they warn against. The genre's rules, from the genre's judges.

The statement has a grander lineage than grant paperwork: the manifesto. Dogme 95's "Vow of Chastity" - ten rules signed by Lars von Trier and Thomas Vinterberg, banning props, music, tripods, and "director credit" itself - is a director's statement weaponized, and it produced The Celebration. Werner Herzog's "Minnesota Declaration" declares war on cinéma vérité's "accountant's truth" in favor of what he calls ecstatic truth - twelve theses that explain every strange, staged moment in his documentaries. And the modern mutation of the statement is visual: the lookbook and the proof-of-concept short. Damien Chazelle couldn't get Whiplash financed, so he shot the toughest scene as an eighteen-minute short, won the Sundance short jury prize, and had feature money within the year. That short was the director's statement - delivered in the director's actual language.

The director's job has been reinvented three times, and knowing the history inoculates you against romantic nonsense about it. Era one: the studio system, where the director was a skilled employee in the middle of an assembly line - often assigned the script on Friday, shooting Monday, with the producer (the Thalberg model) holding final cut. And yet: Michael Curtiz directed over a hundred films on assignment, one of them Casablanca - shot with the ending famously unsettled, script pages arriving warm. Howard Hawks moved between screwball comedy, noir, war, and westerns, defining excellence with a shrug: "A good movie is three great scenes and no bad ones." These were craftsmen of terrifying competence, and the auteur critics' great insight was that some of them - Ford, Hawks, Hitchcock - smuggled a personal signature through the machine anyway.

Read Alongside
The Genius of the System - Thomas Schatz

The definitive history of studio-era filmmaking as a system - producers, units, house styles - arguing the machine itself deserves the authorship credit critics later gave directors alone. The essential counterweight to auteur romance.

Era two: the auteur ascendancy. Post-war, post-Cahiers, post-television, the director became the film's official author - culminating in the 1970s New Hollywood, when directors briefly held the keys (and, as Biskind's history shows, drove several studios off cliffs). The possessory credit - "A Film By" - became a literal battleground between the DGA and the WGA: a fight over whose name owns the work, still unresolved. Era three: now. The franchise era re-industrialized authorship - directors on IP films can be hired and fired like the studio days (ask the several directors replaced mid-production on major franchises) - while streaming and festivals sustain a parallel auteur economy. Most working directors live in Coppola's compromise, "one for them, one for me." This chapter's mature lesson: the auteur and the craftsman are not opposites. They are the two hands of the same job, and every era just changes which hand holds the pen.

D-2 · Year 1 · Module Two

Visual Storytelling & Shot Design

The Trailer

Alfred Hitchcock liked to claim he never looked through the viewfinder. Why would he? "The film is already finished," he'd say - made in his head, drawn on paper, months before a single actor arrived. The shooting was just transcription, and famously boring to him. The legend is exaggerated - scholar Bill Krohn dug through the production records and found plenty of on-set invention - but the exaggeration points at a real philosophy: a film can be designed, shot by shot, the way an architect designs a building, and the design is where the directing happens.

On the other side of the world, this philosophy became a nickname. South Korean crews call Bong Joon-ho "Bongtail" - Bong plus detail - because his storyboards are so precise that Parasite's finished frames match his pencil panels almost cut for cut; the boards were later published as a graphic novel that you can read against the film like a score. Akira Kurosawa spent the years he couldn't get Ran financed painting hundreds of full-color images of it - storyboards as oil paintings - so that when the money finally came, the film already existed in a museum's worth of canvases. And George Miller developed Mad Max: Fury Road as roughly 3,500 storyboard panels before a conventional script, reasoning that a two-hour chase should be written in pictures, in the medium it would live in.

But hold the counter-argument in your other hand, because this module insists on both: Spielberg abandoned his boards for the ocean scenes of Jaws because the sea refused to hold still; the Dardennes design almost nothing and discover the shot by following the actor's body through real rooms. Design versus discovery is not a war with a winner. It is a dial, and knowing where to set it - per film, per scene, per shot - is the actual skill.

By the end of this module you will be able to take a scene from page to floor plan to boards to shot list - and, just as important, know when to throw all four away.

Chapters

Visualization is not drawing pretty pictures; it is making decisions early, when they're cheap. The workflow this chapter drills, from Katz: read the scene and answer the interpretive questions first (whose scene is it? what changes? where's the emotional peak?) - then draw the floor plan (the space, the actors' movements, numbered camera positions) - then board the key frames - then compile the shot list. Each stage catches errors the previous one couldn't see: a floor plan reveals your two dream shots require the camera to be inside a wall; a board reveals your coverage never shows the object the scene turns on. The core design principle underneath: visual progression. A scene's shots should form a deliberate arc - classically wide-to-tight as intensity rises, but the arc can invert, plateau, or explode, as long as it's an arc and not an accident.

These three documents are how a vision leaves your skull and enters a hundred other people's workdays. The floor plan (overhead diagram) is the logician: actor paths as arrows, numbered camera setups, the 180-line drawn in. Crews love it because it answers their real questions - where do we rig, where does the dolly track go, what needs art dressing. The storyboard is the poet: key frames showing size, angle, and composition, with arrows for movement inside the frame and between panels; an animatic (boards cut together with temp sound) tests rhythm before you've shot anything - standard in animation, increasingly standard for action. The shot list is the accountant: scene, setup number, shot size, lens feel, movement, description, and - critically - priorities, because the day will shrink and you must already know which shots are the scene's spine and which are its jewelry.

Two philosophies of shooting a scene, and every director leans one way. Coverage: shoot the master, then work down - over-shoulders, singles, inserts - so the editor can build the scene twelve different ways. It is the industrial default (television runs on it) and its virtues are real: safety, performance options, schedule resilience. Its vice: scenes directed by nobody, assembled later from anonymous angles. The designed shot: this moment has one correct camera, and we will find it and commit. Its virtue is authorship - the shot means something because it excludes everything else. Its vice: no escape hatch. The old masters ran the second philosophy as economics: John Ford shot so little film, so precisely, that studio editors had nothing to recut - "cutting in the camera" as a power move. Gordon Willis lit The Godfather so dark and Coppola covered so lean that Paramount executives panicked ("you can't see Brando's eyes!") - which was, of course, the design.

The working synthesis most great directors actually use: design the spine, cover the flesh. D-3 · Year 1 · Module Three

Staging & Blocking - Mise-en-Scène

The Trailer

There is a story film people tell about Ernst Lubitsch: given a locked door, he could direct an entire love affair - who knocks, who hesitates, which side of the door the camera waits on. Billy Wilder kept a sign above his desk for his whole career: "How would Lubitsch do it?" The answer was almost always the same kind of answer: not a line of dialogue, not a camera trick - a piece of staging. Where the bodies are. Where they move. What the camera is allowed to see them do.

Blocking is the most invisible of the director's crafts and the one that most reliably separates directors from shot-collectors. Kurosawa staged crowds like weather systems - wind, rain, horses, and a hundred extras all moving in choreographed counterpoint, so that even his stillest frames feel like a held breath. Mizoguchi answered with the opposite religion: one scene, one shot, the camera drifting past sliding screens like a hand unrolling a scroll. Ozu barely moved the camera at all and staged everything into the frame instead. Three masters, three complete systems, one shared conviction: the arrangement of bodies in space is the meaning. Alexander Mackendrick - who left directing to become the greatest directing teacher of his age - put the working version of it to his students at CalArts: a director must be able to tell the story with the sound turned off. If the staging doesn't narrate, the words are doing illegal work.

And a warning from the trenches: blocking is the first thing that dies under schedule pressure. The panicked default - two actors planted on marks, trading close-ups - is how films start looking like depositions. This module exists so that under pressure, staging is your reflex, not your luxury.

Four chapters: moving actors and camera as one thought, staging in depth, the sequence shot, and the locked room. Sound off. Story on.

Chapters

Blocking is choreography with meaning attached: who approaches whom, who turns away on which line, who owns the doorway. The grammar is old and reliable - distance encodes intimacy or power; movement toward equals pressure; a character who crosses the room on a line gives that line physical force; the actor who stays still while others orbit reads as the scene's gravity. The professional workflow this chapter installs is the on-set liturgy: block, light, rehearse, shoot. The blocking rehearsal comes first because everything else - lighting, marks, lens, sound - is built on where bodies actually go. A director who arrives without a staging idea surrenders the scene to whatever geometry the furniture suggests.

D-2 debated when to use the long take; this chapter is how to stage one. A sequence shot is a scene whose editing happens inside the frame: the camera's path and the actors' choreography must create every "cut" - each new composition, each reveal, each size change - by movement alone. The design method: break the scene into its beats as usual, then assign each beat a composition the moving shot must arrive at, then invent the connective choreography (who crosses, what the camera follows, what pretext moves us) that walks the frame from station to station. The craft secrets: use actors as wipes to hide resets; let the camera "hand off" attention from one character to another; build in breathing beats where the frame settles, or the audience's eye exhausts. And rehearse in reverse-priority: the hardest station first, because if the climax composition can't be reached, the rest is wasted choreography.

D-4 · Year 1 · Module Four

Directing the Camera

The Trailer

A young critic named Luc Moullet wrote, in Cahiers du Cinéma, that "morality is a question of tracking shots." Jean-Luc Godard, being Godard, flipped it into the version everyone quotes: "tracking shots are a question of morality." Both men meant the same alarming thing - that where you put the camera, and how you move it, is an ethical act. Years later Jacques Rivette proved the point in fury, condemning a Holocaust film for a single "abject" tracking shot that beautified a death. The camera is never neutral. It is always someone's way of looking.

This module is the director's half of a conversation you'll continue with cinematographers for the rest of your life - and the good news is the great DPs want a director who speaks it. Roger Deakins says the job is simple to state and endless to do: the camera serves the story. Steven Spielberg - needing the audience to feel Chief Brody's world lurch on that beach in Jaws - reached for the dolly-zoom Hitchcock invented for Vertigo: track in while zooming out, and the background collapses toward a man whose face stays fixed. Vertigo made visible. Spike Lee bolts actor and camera to the same dolly so his people float toward destiny while the world slides past. Kubrick shoved wide lenses down one-point-perspective corridors until symmetry itself felt like malice. None of these are "camera tricks." Each is a sentence in a language - focal length, height, distance, movement - and each says something no other sentence could.

Ozu spent a career refusing almost all of it: one lens (a 50mm), one height (low, seated), no dollies to speak of - and built one of the largest bodies of feeling in cinema. That refusal is also a camera direction. That's the lesson hiding under this whole module: you cannot not choose.

Four chapters: what lenses say, what movement means, whose eyes we're inside, and what the frame argues. Learn to speak camera, and the DP becomes your co-author instead of your vendor.

Focal length is psychology. Wide lenses expand space and bind a subject to their world - rooms loom, backgrounds stay present, movement toward camera accelerates; push a wide close and faces distort toward the grotesque or the intimate. Long lenses compress and isolate - backgrounds stack into walls, crowds flatten into fate, a face floats free of context like a specimen or a saint; movement toward camera slows to futility (the sprinting man who never arrives). The "normal" range disappears - which is itself a statement: the unmediated look. Directors don't need to talk in millimeters to own this; they need to talk in effects - "I want the room pressing on him" is a lens direction. The great signatures are lens philosophies: Kurosawa's monstrous telephotos turning battle into pressed-flower chaos; Ozu's single 50mm as a vow of humility; Kubrick's wides as clinical dread.

Before moving the camera, answer Mackendrick's question: who is moving it? Movement always implies an intelligence - a character's attention, the story's pressure, fate's approach - and unmotivated movement reads as a nervous director's fidget. D-5 · Year 1 · Module Five

Directing Actors I

The Trailer

Alfred Hitchcock spent decades dogged by a quote: "actors are cattle." His correction was worse: "I never said actors are cattle. I said actors should be treated like cattle." He was joking - mostly - but the joke marks one pole of this module's territory: the director for whom performance is one more designed element. At the other pole stands Elia Kazan, who wrote that direction is "turning psychology into behavior" - the whole job, in four words. Kazan came out of the Group Theatre, co-founded the Actors Studio, and directed Brando in Streetcar and On the Waterfront; when he needed a moment, he didn't design it, he planted it in the actor and let it grow on camera.

Most directing disasters with actors are not failures of sensitivity. They are failures of language. Judith Weston built the modern curriculum on one distinction: result direction - "be angrier," "be sadder," "give me more" - describes the outcome and is unplayable; an actor can only fake it. Action direction - "make him back down," "get her to forgive you" - gives the actor something to do, and doing, photographed, is what acting is. Sidney Lumet ran two full weeks of rehearsal before every film and swore the movies were made there. Mike Leigh spends months building characters from birth with his cast before a "script" exists. Cassavetes - patron saint of "improvisation" - turns out to have shot meticulously written scenes; the looseness was performed, which is its own lesson about what rehearsal can hide.

And before any of that: casting. Ask directors off the record and most give you the same scandalous arithmetic - cast right and the directing is mostly done; cast wrong and no note will save you. Paramount fought Coppola for months over an unknown mumbling method actor and a short unknown theatre kid. Their names were Brando and Pacino. The studio wanted Redford.

Script analysis for performance, casting, the shared vocabulary, and rehearsal: the four rooms where films are actually won. Enter speaking action, not results.

Chapters

D-1 analyzed the script for meaning; this chapter re-analyzes it for behavior. The Stanislavskian toolkit, in its practical film form: every character in every scene wants something - the objective - and pursues it through actions (tactics), each expressible as a transitive verb you can actually play: to charm, to threaten, to test, to beg. When one tactic is abandoned for another, that's a beat change - and a scene's beat map is its true musical score, the thing you and the actor must agree on even when you agree on nothing else. Above the scene sits the super-objective (what the character wants across the whole film) and against everything push the obstacles - because drama is want meeting resistance, and an actor with no obstacle has nothing to do but emote, which is the death you can smell from the last row.

D-6 · Year 1 · Module Six

Scene Craft

The Trailer

Howard Hawks' entire theory of cinema fit in a shrug: "A good movie is three great scenes and no bad ones." He never explained how to make the three - that was the sly part - but he'd named the true unit of the art. Nobody remembers plots. Audiences walk out carrying scenes: the diner in Heat, the tavern in Inglourious Basterds, the restaurant in The Godfather, the speech about the USS Indianapolis delivered by a drunk shark hunter at night. A film is a necklace; scenes are the stones; and directing, at ground level, is the cutting of stones.

This module is where Year 1 converges. William Goldman's screenwriting commandment - get in late, get out early - becomes a directing principle here: the scene begins at the last possible moment and ends before its echo fades, and the director enforces that economy with the camera. Sergio Leone built his church on the opposite altar: stretching the seconds before violence into liturgy, three killers and a fly and a dripping hat brim while Bresson-length minutes pass - proof that a scene's clock belongs to the director, not the wall. Fincher shot the opening of The Social Network - nine pages of dialogue in a bar - ninety-nine times, not for perfection of line readings but for the scene's tempo: two minds fencing at different speeds. And on the far side of the world, Jackie Chan's team at Golden Harvest rehearsed a shopping-mall descent through live electrical decorations until the glass and the burns were real enough to put stuntmen in hospital - then printed the take where pain and grace fused. Every one of these is the same job: a director deciding, beat by beat, what a scene is.

Dialogue, action, the joints between scenes, and the pulse within them. Master the stone; the necklace follows.

Chapters

The beginner's error is believing a dialogue scene is about the dialogue. It is about what the dialogue is doing - the negotiation under the words - and the director's craft is staging that under-scene. The toolkit: geometry (across a table is confrontation; side by side is alliance or evasion; who stands while the other sits is a power diagram); movement as punctuation (the cross on a key line, the turn away that outs a lie); the reaction economy - dialogue scenes live on the listener's face at least as much as the speaker's, and deciding whose reaction owns each beat is half the direction; size discipline from D-2 (spend the close-up when the subtext surfaces, not before); and tempo - overlaps, pauses, interruptions are directed, not transcribed. Mamet's blunt test applies: if you can't say what each character is trying to get, the scene isn't ready to stage.

Action is not a break from storytelling; it is storytelling at maximum pressure, and it fails the moment either half of that sentence is forgotten. Films are written in scenes but experienced in joints - and the cut between two scenes is a sentence the director writes with juxtaposition. The vocabulary: the hard cut (neutral, or brutal when contents collide); the match cut (shape, movement, or idea rhyming across the joint - argument by visual echo); the sound bridge (audio from the next scene invading this one, or this scene's sound haunting the next - time made porous); the dialogue hook (a question answered by the next scene's image - the joint as wit); and the pillow shot, Ozu's signature - a few seconds of empty corridor, clothesline, smokestack between scenes, transition as breath and as the world's indifference. D-7 · Year 2 · Module Seven

Directing Actors II - Non-Professionals, Ensembles, Difficult Scenes

The Trailer

In 1948, David O. Selznick offered Vittorio De Sica full Hollywood financing for his little script about a stolen bicycle - on one condition: the unemployed father would be played by Cary Grant. De Sica declined, went home to Rome, and cast Lamberto Maggiorani, an actual factory worker with an actual worker's walk, opposite a boy found in a street crowd. Bicycle Thieves became one of the most influential films ever made - and Maggiorani, in the era's cruelest irony, lost his factory job after shooting and struggled to find work, having played unemployment too well to escape it. That is this module's territory: performance drawn from people who are not performing, and the responsibilities that come with borrowing a real life.

Half a century later, Abbas Kiarostami refined the tradition into a philosophy: children and villagers directed through tasks and conversations rather than scripts, lines fed moments before the take, the director sometimes sitting in the car seat opposite a non-actor, being the scene partner himself and cutting his own presence out later. Mike Leigh built the opposite cathedral in London - six months of improvisation constructing characters from birth-year forward before a single "scripted" scene exists - and John Cassavetes, patron saint of American looseness, guarded a trade secret his collaborators eventually confessed: most of that famous improvisation was written. The freedom was rehearsed. The spontaneity was staged. Which is the module's second lesson: ensemble electricity is engineered.

And the hardest chapter is the newest: how to direct what should never be casually directed - intimacy, violence, children, animals. W.C. Fields' old vaudeville warning ("never work with children or animals") is filed under apocrypha-adjacent showbiz wisdom; the modern industry's answer is better than avoidance: choreographers, coordinators, protocols, and the discovery that safety and heat are not opposites - consent is what makes danger playable at all.

Three chapters: the non-professional tradition from Rome to Tehran, the ensemble and its engineered spontaneity, and the scenes that demand protocols. Advanced actor-work is mostly advanced trust-work.

Why cast someone who cannot act? Because they cannot act - they can only be, and for certain films being is the whole prize: a face history wrote, a body a trade shaped, a regional truth no conservatory teaches. The craft is a different instrument entirely. Cast for essence, not skill: you are casting a person's existing relationship to the role's world (De Sica's factory worker, Bresson's students, the Dardennes' street casting), and the audition is observation - how they wait, not how they read. Restrict the script: many masters never hand non-actors the full screenplay - lines arrive scene by scene, sometimes moment by moment (Kiarostami fed dialogue and questions from off-camera; the responses are half-documentary), so the performance stays in discovery rather than recitation. Direct behavior, not emotion: give tasks, business, and real objects - carry this, count these, find your son in that crowd - and let feeling be a byproduct the camera catches; asking a non-actor to "be devastated" produces acting, and acting is precisely what you hired them to be incapable of. Protect them in the cut: performance is finished in editing (Bresson's whole system, waiting one module ahead), where selection turns behavior into inner life.

D-8 · Year 2 · Module Eight

Genre & Register - Comedy, Horror, the Musical, Action

The Trailer

In 1928, Buster Keaton stood on a mark while a two-ton house façade fell around him, his body passing through an open window with inches of clearance on either shoulder. One take. Crew members reportedly couldn't watch. The gag works because the frame is wide and unbroken - you can see it's real, and comedy's oldest law is proven in one crash: the frame is the joke-teller, and where you put the camera decides whether an audience laughs, gasps, or shrugs.

Every register in this module has such a physics. Hitchcock formalized suspense as an information equation - his famous bomb-under-the-table lecture: surprise gives the audience fifteen seconds of shock, suspense gives them fifteen MINUTES, and the difference is only who knows what. Stanley Donen and Gene Kelly shot cinema's most joyful sequence with its star running a reported 103-degree fever, milk mixed into the rain so it would read on film - the musical's secret being that ecstasy is the most rehearsed emotion on screen. And Jackie Chan ended his films with outtake reels of his own broken bones - a philosophy disguised as blooper credits: action's currency is visible truth, the audience's animal certainty that a body really did that, really there.

The theatre's oldest deathbed line - "dying is easy; comedy is hard" (attributed to the actor Edmund Gwenn, and to half a dozen others before him; apocrypha flag raised and saluted) - is this module's motto because it generalizes: every register is hard, each in its own dimension, and each has a craft literature the general directing books skip. A director who can modulate register - who knows why the horror frame withholds, the comedy frame reveals, the musical frame liberates, and the action frame orients - owns the whole keyboard.

Four chapters, four physics: the comedy of timing and frames, the Hitchcockian dread machine, the musical number, and action's grammar of clarity and impact. Genre is not a costume. It is a camera philosophy.

Chapters

Comedy is rhythm made visible, and the director conducts it with four instruments. The frame: physical comedy demands wides - Keaton and Tati compose in full figure because the joke IS the body's relationship to space, and cutting in destroys the proof (the modern sin Edgar Wright's essay indicts: comedy shot like drama coverage, jokes carried only by dialogue); the reveal-pan and the widening frame are jokes in themselves (the thing was there all along); background gags run a second comic channel (Tati's democratic frame, where the funniest event may be in deep focus behind the "scene"). The cut: timing lives at the splice - cut TO the reaction or hold OFF it, arrive late on the aftermath (cause offscreen, absurd effect revealed), or smash-cut as punchline (setup line → contradictory image); the discipline of the held take lets awkwardness ripen (the comedy of duration - the Office-era stare, Roy Andersson's fixed tableaux). The performance clock: beats before the punch (the amateur rushes; the professional waits one uncomfortable extra beat), deadpan as amplifier (the unmoved face beside chaos), and the ensemble's overlapping timing rehearsed like music. Escalation: comic sequences are structures - establish the rule, repeat with variation, break at the third (the rule of three's percussion), then top the topper.

The bomb theory, stated properly, is an information design principle: surprise is a bomb exploding unannounced (fifteen seconds of shock, then resentment); suspense is the audience SEEING the bomb placed under the table while the characters chat about baseball (fifteen minutes of agony, every banal line electrified) - dread is manufactured by giving the audience MORE knowledge than the characters, then rationing the relief. From this root, the construction kit: the withheld reveal (the unseen monster is the audience's own imagination on your payroll - Jaws' broken shark as the tradition's accidental scripture, Lewton's shadows as its deliberate one); offscreen space as the genre's true set (what the frame excludes presses on what it shows; sound from beyond the edge is the cheapest terror ever invented); the POV chain weaponized (look / seen / reaction - Year 1's grammar now running the identification machinery of stalk-and-watch); pattern and violation (establish a visual rule for safety, then break it: the background that should be empty, isn't); and the false-scare economy (cat-scares spend audience trust - budget them, and make the real scare arrive where the fake one taught them to relax).

D-9 · Year 2 · Module Nine

World Directing Traditions & Grammar

The Trailer

Yasujirō Ozu spent thirty years breaking Hollywood's most sacred rule - the 180-degree line - on purpose, in every film, from a camera three feet off the floor. His crews maintained the "wrong" eyelines with fanatical precision; a red kettle would be moved two inches for an afternoon; and out of this heresy came films so emotionally exact that Wenders, Schrader, and Kore-eda made pilgrimages to his grave, which bears no name - only the character mu: nothingness. Meanwhile in Paris, Robert Bresson was shooting the same action fifty times until his non-actors - he called them "models," never actors - stopped performing entirely, reciting flatly, moving automatically, until something he considered the soul leaked through the exhaustion. Two grammars, both complete, both unteachable by Hollywood's textbook - and both now taught in this module.

The tour continues: Eisenstein designing shots as colliding cells, convinced two images could detonate an IDEA that existed in neither; Vijay Anand staging songs inside Indian films with such single-take architectural wit that directors still storyboard from his picturizations; Yuen Woo-ping's Hong Kong action units choreographing on the day, wire teams hauling actors into ballet while the camera undercranks reality into ferocity - a system so complete Hollywood simply imported it whole (The Matrix's fights are, administratively speaking, a Hong Kong production). Kiarostami answered a century of spectacle with a car, two faces, and questions. And in 1995 Copenhagen, four directors signed a monastic vow - no props, no score, no tripods, no genre - and mailed cinema a certificate program in creative poverty.

Godard said cinema is truth at twenty-four frames a second; De Palma answered that the camera lies twenty-four times a second. This module's position: both are describing GRAMMARS - and a director fluent in only one writes with half an alphabet.

Seven grammars, seven chapters: Ozu's floor-level cosmos, Bresson's models, the Soviet collision, Bombay picturization, Hong Kong's action units, Iranian minimalism, and the Dogme vow. Learn them as living languages, not museum pieces.

Chapters

Ozu's system, catalogued: the low camera - roughly the eye-height of a person kneeling on tatami, applied everywhere (offices, bars, train platforms), flattening hierarchy: no one is looked down upon, the world addressed from the posture of domestic attention. 360-degree space - where Hollywood fences a scene behind the 180 line, Ozu shoots from anywhere in the circle, cutting across the axis freely; his conversation grammar is frontal: speakers framed alone, gazing almost into the lens, so dialogue becomes a series of direct addresses - the audience seated in the middle of the family, spoken to from both sides. Graphic matching holds the heresy together: compositions rhyme across cuts (a bottle here, a figure there, echoed in the reverse), continuity of DESIGN replacing continuity of geography. Pillow shots - those still lifes of kettles, corridors, clotheslines, trains - punctuate scenes like breath marks: transitions that carry no plot, only duration and place (the ma this curriculum keeps meeting). And ellipsis: the weddings, deaths, and confrontations a Hollywood film exists for happen OFFSCREEN, between scenes; Ozu films the before and the after, trusting the audience to have attended what mattered.

Bresson's system begins with a renunciation: cinema, he insisted, must owe nothing to theater - and acting is theater smuggled onto film. His replacement: the model - a non-professional, cast once and never reused, directed through repetition until automatism: the same line, the same gesture, twenty, forty, fifty takes, not to perfect expression but to EXHAUST it, until intention drains away and what remains is a human being's irreducible presence performing actions without commentary. Around the model, the grammar: fragmentation - bodies filmed in parts (hands above all: Bresson's cinema is the great cathedral of hands - passing money, picking locks, opening doors), the close-up of the part carrying what the face is forbidden to indicate; flat delivery - speech as recitation, emotion left for the AUDIENCE to supply (the Kuleshov principle adopted as ethics: the viewer's soul does the acting); sound over image - "when a sound can replace an image, cut the image": offscreen trains, doors, footsteps building worlds the frame refuses to show; and the ellipsis of climax - violence and grace alike arriving between shots. The wager under it all: strip performance, psychology, and spectacle away, and what leaks through the automatism is the thing he refused to name cheaply - call it soul.

On the Cinémathèque Française's hundredth-birthday stage in 1995, Lars von Trier read a manifesto, threw red pamphlets into the audience, and left. The Vow of Chastity, co-signed with Thomas Vinterberg (Kragh-Jacobsen and Levring joining as the founding four): shooting on location only, props found there; sound never produced apart from image (no score unless it plays in the scene); handheld camera; color, no special lighting; no optical work or filters; no superficial action (murders, weapons "must not occur"); temporal and geographical alienation forbidden (here and now only); no genre movies; Academy 35mm; and - the sting - the director must not be credited. The purpose, in the manifesto's own mock-theology: to strip cinema's "cosmetics," force truth from constraint, and counter both Hollywood gloss AND auteur vanity (the movement certified films by committee, confession culture included - directors publicly admitting their violations, von Trier confessing a draped window on The Idiots from certificate one). The honest record: the rules were broken from the start, the movement wound down within a decade - and it WORKED anyway: Festen's Jury Prize announced digital handheld as a legitimate aesthetic, a wave of certificates followed worldwide, and the Vow's DNA runs through mumblecore, found-footage, and every no-budget movement that discovered poverty could be a style if declared loudly enough.

D-10 · Year 2 · Module Ten

Directing Across Departments

The Trailer

When John Williams first played Steven Spielberg the theme for Jaws - two notes, alternating - Spielberg laughed and asked to hear the real one. Williams insisted. Those two notes became the shark the broken mechanical one couldn't be, half the film's terror delivered by a tuba. A decade of collaborations later, Spielberg's standing joke was that Williams keeps getting hired because he keeps being right. The counter-legend sits one shelf over: Alfred Hitchcock, mid-career pinnacle behind him, ordered Bernard Herrmann - the composer of Psycho's shrieking strings, Vertigo's spirals - to write a pop-friendly score for Torn Curtain. Herrmann wrote Herrmann instead. Hitchcock fired him at the recording session, and one of cinema's supreme partnerships ended in a scoring stage's stunned silence. Two stories, one lesson: the director's relationship with department heads is the film's actual nervous system - and it runs on trust that must be BUILT, and can be killed in a sentence.

The happy marriages are cinema's quiet superpower: Scorsese and Thelma Schoonmaker, cutting together across fifty years, three Oscars, and a friendship begun at a summer film program; the Coens and Roger Deakins storyboarding winters in advance; Wong Kar-wai and Christopher Doyle inventing a visual language across a decade of no-script nights. David Fincher, asked about imposing his will on collaborators, gave this module its motto: it's not about what I want - "it's what the film wants." The director's job across departments is to be the person who can hear that, and translate it into a hundred specialists' languages.

Three chapters: leading the DP, designer, and composer; the director–editor marriage; and previsualization - the shared drawing everyone builds from. You don't operate the departments. You conduct the argument they're all having about the same film.

Chapters

Department leadership is translation: one theme (Lumet's sentence from D-1) rendered into each craft's native language. The universal protocol: brief with REFERENCES, not adjectives - "moody" means nothing; three frames from other films, two paintings, and a photograph mean everything (the lookbook from D-1, now working for its living); state the dramaturgy (what must the audience FEEL here, what changes) and let the specialist choose the tools; hire taste, then direct outcomes, never methods (tell a DP the scene should feel like the marriage is already over - don't tell them the lens). The DP: the earliest and longest conversation - look development (tests, LUTs, references), the shot-list negotiation (D-2's documents as shared authorship), and on-set shorthand built in prep so shooting days spend no words on philosophy. The designer: the world's rule-maker - brief them on class, history, and psychology of spaces (what does this room believe?), respect the palette as dramaturgy (color arcs are theirs to propose, yours to aim), and walk the sets BEFORE the schedule makes changes cost fortunes. The composer: the spotting session is the craft - walking the cut together, deciding where music enters, exits, and (hardest) stays out; brief in emotion and function ("this cue must make us forgive him") and beware the temp-score trap - falling for the editorial's borrowed music until the real score can only imitate it (the industry's most confessed sin; Kubrick firing Alex North's finished 2001 score for the temp classics is its founding legend).

D-11 · Year 2 · Module Eleven

Authorship & Personal Style

The Trailer

In 1962, Federico Fellini stood at the peak of world cinema with no idea what his next film was. He had the crew, the sets rising at Cinecittà, the contracts signed - and no film. He sat down to write a letter of surrender to his producer, and while composing it was called away to a crew member's birthday toast; standing among the celebrating grips and carpenters, he understood: the film would be ABOUT a director who has everything except the film. He tore up the letter and made 8½ - the crisis converted, in one afternoon, into the most influential film about creation ever shot. That is this module's subject in a single anecdote: personal style is not decoration. It is what happens when a director stops impersonating cinema and starts confessing through it.

The confessions take every form. Satyajit Ray, a Calcutta advertising illustrator, met Jean Renoir location-scouting on the Hooghly, absorbed the encouragement, saw Bicycle Thieves in London - and spent years shooting Pather Panchali with borrowed money and an amateur crew, the production stalling until (as the cherished, partly-true legend runs) family jewelry and a government loan filed under "roads improvement" carried it home. Andrei Tarkovsky built Mirror from his father's poems and his mother's face, restructured it some twenty times in the edit, and answered baffled Soviet committees with the letters of ordinary viewers who wrote that the film was about THEIR lives. And Wes Anderson's symmetry became so legible a signature that three frames identify him from across a room - raising this module's warning label: a style strong enough to be recognized is strong enough to become a cage.

Four chapters: developing a signature, the feature versus the short, adaptation as a director's reading, and working from personal material. The other modules taught you cinema's languages. This one asks what you have to say in them.

D-12 · Year 2 · Module Twelve

The Director in the Industry

The Trailer

In 1985, Universal shelved Terry Gilliam's Brazil, demanding a shorter, happier cut. Gilliam's counter-attack has no equal in industry history: he screened the film secretly for critics (who promptly voted it Best Picture of the year, un-released) and took out a full-page ad in Variety consisting of one sentence in a black border: "Dear Sid Sheinberg - when are you going to release my film, 'BRAZIL'? - Terry Gilliam." The studio chief's own trade paper, weaponized. Universal blinked; the film came out nearly whole; and the whole war got its own book. That is this module's terrain: the director as negotiator, guerrilla, and occasionally publicist of last resort.

The cases stack up like legal precedents. Orson Welles, locked out of Touch of Evil's edit, wrote a 58-page memo of cutting notes so precise and so humbly argued that - forty years later, both Welles and the studio regime long dead - editors reconstructed the film FROM the memo, and the 1998 version is now the one the world watches: a director's cut executed posthumously, by document. Blade Runner exists in at least five versions, the "director's cut" label itself an industry invention its own director initially disowned. And the modern chapter wrote itself in fan-pressure and streaming money: an entire alternate four-hour cut of a studio tentpole, willed into existence by campaign. The director's cut began as a contract clause; it has become a genre, a business model, and a philosophical problem about which version of a film IS the film.

Underneath the war stories runs the career itself: pitching without begging, producers as partners and adversaries, television's guest-director discipline, and the long game - Agnès Varda made her first feature before the New Wave had a name and her last at ninety, having outlasted every industry that underfunded her. The final module's real subject is hers: the body of work as the project, with each film - and each fight - just one move in it.

Four chapters: pitching and the producer-studio dance, episodic directing, the director's cut, and career strategy. The industry is not the enemy of the work. It is the weather the work sails in - learn to read it.
