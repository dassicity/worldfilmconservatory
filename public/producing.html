P-1 · Year 1 · Module One

The Producer's Role & Types

The Trailer

David O. Selznick dictated memos. Thousands of them - to directors, writers, cameramen, his own publicity department - on Gone with the Wind alone, pages a day, so that a book of them runs to five hundred pages and reads like the autobiography of a film. He fired his first director, hired a second, rewrote the script nightly, and stood beside the burning of Atlanta with a stopwatch. When the Academy invented a title for William Cameron Menzies's drawings (PD-1), Selznick had already invented the modern producer: the person whose film it is, before and after everyone else has gone home.

The credit has since fractured into a family. The creative producer finds the material and carries it for years; the executive producer may have financed it, sold it, or lent it a name; the line producer and the unit production manager spend the money and run the machine; and the title's abuse - the dozen producers on a poster, most of whom never saw the set - is the trade's oldest joke and the reason the Producers Guild built a mark to certify who actually did the job. Behind every type sits the same first act: the judgment, made alone, that a story is worth your soul, your money and years of your time. This module is that judgment, and the jobs that follow from it. The producer is the person who says yes first and is still there at the end. Learn what the yes costs.

Two chapters: the producer's types - creative, executive, line, UPM; and the development judgment - is this worth your soul, your money and your time. Say yes carefully.

Chapters

One word, four jobs. The creative producer (the producer, the "produced by" credit): finds or originates the material (P-2), develops it with the writer, attaches the director and cast, raises the money (P-3), oversees the production (P-6, P-7), stays through post (P-8) and sells the film (P-9, P-10) - the person whose project it is from the first option to the last territory; the Producers Guild's mark certifies that the credited producer performed a majority of these functions, because the title is otherwise unregulated. The executive producer: a title with several meanings - the financier or the person who secured the financing; the studio or company executive who oversaw the project; the star's or director's own company's principal; the television showrunner (P-12); and the honorary EP - the name lent for its weight; the credit's ambiguity is the industry's own, and the chapter's discipline is to ask, of any EP, which of these they were. The line producer: the producer of the physical production - the budget's author and keeper (P-5), the schedule's, the crew's hiring, the vendors' deals, the daily cost - "line" from the line in the budget dividing the above (story, director, cast, producers) from the below (everyone and everything else); the line producer runs the below-the-line and reports to the producer on the whole. The unit production manager: the DGA-classified manager of the production's daily operations - the schedule executed, the call sheets approved, the locations and permits, the crew deals, the cost reports (P-7) - often the same person as the line producer on smaller films and a separate, subordinate role on larger ones. The associates and co-producers: the co-producer (a substantial partner or the line producer's credit), the associate producer (a contributor - or a favour), the production supervisor and coordinator (P-6's office). The regional variations: the British producer (often the creative and financial producer in one), the French producteur délégué (the legally responsible producer under the CNC's system - P-11), the Indian producer (historically the financier - the "banner" - with the creative role often the director's; the modern Indian studio's shift toward the creative producer), and the studio system's producer (Selznick's era - the executive producer as auteur, when directors were employees). The chapter's law: know which producer you are on this film, and which the others are - because the credit is one word and the work is four jobs.

Before the option, the question. The three costs: SOUL (the story you will defend in every room for years - the producer who does not love it will not survive the third rejection; and the story you should not make - the one whose values you'd have to fake), MONEY (your own, at first: the option fee, the development spend, the travel to the market - and the money you will ask others for, which you must believe you can repay), and TIME (the honest arithmetic: development is years; a film is two to five of your life; a slate is a decade - and the project that takes your years must be worth them). The judgment's questions: WHAT IS IT (the one-line - the logline that a financier, a sales agent and an audience can repeat), WHO IS IT FOR (the audience, named - not "everyone"; the festival, the platform, the territory), WHAT WILL IT COST and WHAT COULD IT EARN (the first budget guess against the first comparables - P-3's arithmetic, done in a day), WHY YOU (what you bring that another producer wouldn't - the access, the relationship, the knowledge), WHY NOW (the cultural moment, the star's availability, the rights window), and CAN IT BE MADE (the script's feasibility - a hundred locations and a child lead and winter exteriors are a budget before they're a story). The traps: the passion project with no audience, the commercial project with no soul, the project you take because you're afraid of having none, the writer's favourite scene that costs a million, the star who "might" attach, the sunk cost (the years already spent as the reason to spend more - the producer's most common ruin). The slate: the judgment made across projects - a company survives on a portfolio (the safe one, the reach, the one that could break out), and the producer's discipline is to say no to nine to say yes to one. The exemplars: Charlie Kaufman's Adaptation as the judgment dramatised (a project that could not be adapted, adapted); The Disaster Artist's The Room as the passion project with no judgment at all - and its accidental audience; Ed Wood as the producer-director whose soul said yes to everything and whose money said nothing. The chapter's law: the judgment is made alone, before the option, with three questions - soul, money, time - and the honest answer to any one of them can save you the other two.

P-2 · Year 1 · Module Two

Development

The Trailer

Robert Evans bought Chinatown before it was written. Robert Towne had an idea about Los Angeles and water; Evans, running Paramount, paid for the script on the strength of a conversation, then spent the development attaching Polanski, casting Nicholson, and arguing the ending - Towne wanted Evelyn to live; Polanski insisted she die, and the producer let the director win. The film is the version the development made. Every produced film is: the material found, the script rewritten under a producer's questions, the director and cast attached in an order that determines the money, and a pitch that turned a room's polite interest into a commitment.

Development is the producer's longest job and the least visible. It is the years before the first day: the search for material (the novel, the article, the life story, the original idea from a writer the producer trusts), the option that secures it, the drafts that make it a film, and the PACKAGE - the director and cast whose names turn a script into something a financier can price. This module is that craft: finding and optioning, working with writers, and pitching and packaging - the three labours that produce the thing the rest of the track will spend money on. A film is made three times, and the first time is in a producer's head, over years, with a writer.

Three chapters: finding and optioning material - the search and the deal; script development and working with writers - the drafts and the notes; and the pitch and packaging - attaching the director and cast. Find it. Fix it. Sell it.

Chapters

The search, then the deal. Where material comes from: BOOKS (the novel and the non-fiction - the book scouts, the publishers' rights departments, the agents' submissions; the bestseller everyone chases and the backlist no one has read), ARTICLES and journalism (the magazine piece optioned the week it runs - the true-story market), LIFE RIGHTS (the person's own story, optioned from them - P-4's clearance), REMAKES and formats (the foreign film's rights; the format's), the PUBLIC DOMAIN (the classic no one owns - P-4), PLAYS, GAMES and IP (the franchise's holders), and the ORIGINAL - the writer's spec script or pitch (the market's most common path for independents, and the relationship-based one: the writer the producer knows). The reader's discipline: the producer reads - everything, fast, with the judgment (P-1 CH 2) applied on every page - and keeps the coverage (the reader's report: logline, synopsis, comments, recommendation - the trade's triage form) as the slate's memory. The option: the deal that secures the right to buy - the OPTION FEE (small: a percentage of the purchase price, or a token; the independent's "free option" negotiated on a relationship), the TERM (a year or eighteen months, with EXTENSIONS for further fees), the PURCHASE PRICE (paid on the first day of principal photography, often a percentage of the budget with a floor and ceiling), the RIGHTS optioned (film, television, sequels, remakes, merchandising - each a negotiation; the author's reserved rights - publishing, stage, radio - kept), the CREDIT and the BACK END (the author's profit participation - P-3's waterfall), and the CHAIN OF TITLE (P-4 - the proof that the person optioning owns what they're selling). The negotiation: the agent across the table, the producer's leverage (the relationship, the vision, the track record) against the seller's (the competition, the book's heat), and the honest limit - the option you can't afford to exercise is a hobby. The exemplars: Sullivan's Travels' producer chasing a film about suffering and finding a comedy; Get Shorty's gangster discovering that the movie business runs on options and promises; Boyhood's twelve-year commitment - the option as a life. The chapter's law: read everything, option what passes the judgment, and secure the rights you'll actually need for the money you can actually pay.

P-3 · Year 1 · Module Three

Film Finance

The Trailer

Art Buchwald sued Paramount over Coming to America and won - and then discovered that the film, which had grossed nearly three hundred million dollars, had officially made no profit. The studio's accounting deducted distribution fees, overhead, interest and marketing until the "net profits" Buchwald's contract promised him were a negative number; the judge called the formula unconscionable. Every producer since has known the phrase "Hollywood accounting," and every deal since has been about which line of the waterfall your money sits on. Mel Brooks made a comedy about financing fraud - The Producers - in 1967; the joke was that you could sell more than a hundred percent of a film that was certain to flop. The trade's darker joke is that you can sell a hundred percent of a film that succeeds and still owe everyone.

Film finance is the module producers fear and the one the track teaches plainly: where the money comes from (the studio, the equity investor, the bank's loan against pre-sales, the gap lender who covers what's left), what the public offers (the tax incentive, the rebate, the grant, the crowd), and how the money comes back - the recoupment waterfall that decides who is paid first, last, and never. Jodorowsky raised and lost a fortune on a Dune that never shot; the Blair Witch investors turned tens of thousands into hundreds of millions; Forrest Gump's novelist was told his film had lost money. The producer's second job is money. This module is the arithmetic.

Three chapters: studio, equity, debt, pre-sales and gap - the sources; tax incentives, rebates, grants and crowdfunding - the public's money; and the recoupment waterfall - who gets paid, in what order, and why "net profits" is a joke. Bring a calculator.

The money comes back through a series of buckets, and the order is the deal. The waterfall: GROSS RECEIPTS (the money the distributors collect - the box office's share after the exhibitor's cut, the streamer's licence, the television sale) → the DISTRIBUTION FEE (the distributor's percentage off the top, per medium - the studio's thirty percent, the sales agent's ten to twenty) → the DISTRIBUTION EXPENSES (prints and advertising, the marketing spend recouped before anyone else - P-9's P&A as the waterfall's largest early bucket) → the SALES AGENT's fee and expenses → the LENDERS (the bank's pre-sale loan, the gap lender, with interest and fees - CH 1's debt repaid first among the financiers) → the COMPLETION BOND's costs → the EQUITY (the investors' recoupment - their money back, often with a premium: the "120" of a hundred-and-twenty-percent return before profit) → DEFERMENTS (the crew's and producer's deferred fees) → and only then NET PROFITS, split between the financiers' pool and the participants' (the producer's, director's, writer's, cast's back ends - usually a share of "producer's net"). The studio's waterfall: the same buckets with the studio's definitions - the distribution fee, the overhead charge on the budget, the interest on the production cost, the marketing charged at full rate - so that Coming to America grossed hundreds of millions and showed no net profit (the Buchwald ruling: the formula "unconscionable," the case settled, the practice unchanged); Forrest Gump's novelist told the film had lost money; the Batman deal where Jack Nicholson's GROSS participation (a share of gross receipts, before the buckets) made him richer than the film's official profit - the lesson that gross points are worth a fortune and net points are a courtesy. The independent's waterfall: negotiated per deal - the equity's position, the premium, the producer's corridor (a share of the equity's recoupment stream - the producer paid as the investors are), the talent's participations defined against a real number (the "adjusted gross" or a defined "net" with the deductions listed), and the COLLECTION ACCOUNT MANAGER (the neutral third party that collects the receipts and pays the waterfall - the independent's protection against everyone's accounting). The reading: every producer reads their own back-end definition - what is deducted, in what order, by whom - before signing, and models the waterfall on a sample gross (the arithmetic exercise below), because the difference between "net profits" and "adjusted gross" is the difference between a courtesy and a house. The exemplars: Coming to America (the case), Forrest Gump (the novelist's letter), Batman (Nicholson's gross). The chapter's law and Year 1's finance close: the money comes back in an order, the order is negotiated, and the producer who doesn't model the waterfall before the deal has agreed to be paid last, or never.

P-4 · Year 1 · Module Four

Legal & Rights

The Trailer

Nosferatu was stolen. F.W. Murnau's 1922 film changed Dracula's name to Orlok, London to Wisborg and the Count's death to a sunrise, and Bram Stoker's widow sued anyway; a German court ordered every print destroyed, and the film survives only because copies had already left the country. Forty-six years later, George Romero's distributor left the copyright notice off the prints of Night of the Living Dead, and under the law of the time the film fell into the public domain on release - the most profitable horror film of its decade earned its makers almost nothing, because a line of text was missing from the title card. Chain of title is the module's first subject because films have been lost, destroyed and given away for want of it.

This module is orientation, not legal advice - the track's standing note: entertainment law is a profession, and the producer's job is to know enough to hire the lawyer early and understand what they're doing. It teaches the rights a film is built on (copyright, the chain that proves you own what you're making, the option's law), the contracts that bind everyone who works on it (talent, crew, the clearances for every face, brand and song), and the insurance and counsel that let a distributor accept delivery. A film is a bundle of rights. The producer's job is to hold all of them, on paper, before the first day.

Three chapters: copyright and chain of title, and the option's law; talent and crew contracts, and clearances; and errors-and-omissions insurance and entertainment law. Hire the lawyer. Then read this.

P-5 · Year 1 · Module Five

Breakdown, Budgeting & Scheduling

The Trailer

Jaws was scheduled for fifty-five days and shot for a hundred and fifty-nine. The shark didn't work, the ocean didn't cooperate, and a young director learned on Martha's Vineyard that a schedule is a promise the weather has not signed. The film's production manager's stripboard - the coloured strips on a board, one per scene, arranged into shooting days - was rearranged so often it became a legend, and the overage nearly ended a studio's patience. The stripboard is the producer's oldest instrument: a script broken into its elements, each scene a strip, each strip a cost, the strips ordered into the cheapest days the story allows.

This module is that arithmetic. The breakdown that turns a script into lists (cast, locations, props, costumes, effects - every department's needs per scene, the eighth-of-a-page as the unit of labour); the budget that prices the lists above the line and below it; the schedule that orders the scenes into days and the days into weeks; and the software that has replaced the coloured strips with a database that costs a day before it's shot. Jaws went a hundred days over; Waterworld's ocean set sank; Paranormal Activity was made for fifteen thousand dollars because its breakdown fit its money. The budget is the script, priced. The schedule is the script, in days. Learn both before you promise either.

Three chapters: the script breakdown, and above- and below-the-line - the lists and the line; the budget, the shooting schedule and the stripboard - the arithmetic and the board; and scheduling and budgeting software - the tools. Count the eighths.

The Trailer

Year 2 opens on daylight stock at 2:20 in the morning on July 23, 1982. A helicopter flying a night scene for Twilight Zone: The Movie was caught in a pyrotechnic blast, lost control, and killed the actor Vic Morrow and two children who should not, under the law, have been working at that hour. The set is run by two people who must agree: the first assistant director, who runs the day, and the producer or UPM, who runs the money and the plan. This module is their relationship - the schedule executed shot by shot, the call times kept, the company moved - and the producer's other daily duties: the contingency (the actor who's sick, the location that's flooded, the shark that won't work - and the plan that already exists for each), the COST REPORT that tells the financiers where the money stands every week, and the safety and insurance regime that the Twilight Zone night, and every accident since, wrote into the day. The set is a factory that makes one product a day. The producer's job is that it makes it safely, on schedule, and on the money.

P-9 · Year 2 · Module Nine

Marketing & Distribution

The Trailer

Hitchcock refused to let anyone into Psycho after it had started. The policy - no late admissions, enforced by cinema managers under contract, announced by the director himself in the trailer and the lobby cards - turned a habit of the era (people wandered in mid-film) into an event, and the film into the highest-grossing of his career. Fifteen years later Universal spent an unheard-of sum on television advertising for Jaws and opened it on hundreds of screens at once, and the summer blockbuster was born from a marketing plan. In 1999 two first-time directors built a website claiming their film's footage was real, and The Blair Witch Project's campaign became the film's largest asset.

Distribution is how the film reaches an audience, and marketing is how the audience learns to want it; the producer who thinks the job ended at delivery has handed the film's fate to strangers. This module is the map - theatrical, streaming, television, VOD, the international sales that finance most independent films before they're shot - and the tools: the sales agent who sells the territories, the positioning that decides what the film is FOR, the trailer that is the film's most-watched version, the publicity, and the audience found and built. The film is not finished when it's delivered. It is finished when someone has seen it on purpose.

Three chapters: theatrical, streaming, TV, VOD and international sales - the windows and the territories; the sales agent, positioning and the trailer - the film sold; and publicity, audience and outreach - the film found. Open the campaign.

Festivals & the Circuit

The Trailer

In January 1989 a twenty-six-year-old's first film played the Sundance Film Festival, then called the U.S. Film Festival, in Park City. Sex, Lies, and Videotape won the audience award, went to Cannes in May, won the Palme d'Or, and was released by Miramax into a gross that taught an industry a new arithmetic: a film costing a million dollars could return twenty-five if the right festival said so first. The independent film movement of the next decade - and the acquisitions market that funded it - dates from that winter. Festivals are not exhibitions of films; they are markets where the film's future is decided in a week.

This module is the circuit as a producer works it: the strategy that chooses which festival premieres the film (a world premiere can be spent once, and the wrong festival wastes it), the map of the majors and their markets - Cannes and its Marché, Berlin and the EFM, Sundance, Toronto, Venice, Busan, and the specialised festivals where a documentary or a genre film finds its buyers - and the endgame: the acquisition (the distributor buying the film at the festival, the bidding war the trades love) or its absence, and the self-distribution that has become a real path rather than a consolation. The festival is the film's first audience and its first market. Choose it as carefully as the lead actor.

Three chapters: festival strategy and selection - the premiere spent once; premieres and markets - Cannes, Berlin, Sundance, Toronto, Busan; and acquisitions and self-distribution - the endgame. Submit.

Chapters

The studio is a system for financing, making and distributing films at volume, and the world has built several. Hollywood's classical system: the vertically integrated studios of the 1920s to the 1940s - production, distribution and exhibition owned together, the contract talent (stars, directors, writers on salary), the producer-unit and the executive producer (P-1's Selznick and Thalberg), the genre factory, the B-picture, the annual slate of dozens - and its dismantling (the Paramount decree of 1948 separating exhibition; the contract system's end; the independents' rise); the Singin' in the Rain and Bad and the Beautiful era as its own subject. The modern studio: the conglomerate's film division - financing the tentpole and the franchise from the parent's balance sheet, distributing worldwide through its own arms, owning the IP across media (the sequel, the series, the park), with the mid-budget film ceded to the specialty labels and the streamers; the greenlight committee; the marketing spend as the film's largest line; the studio's arithmetic (the franchise's global gross against the original's risk) as the reason the module's independents exist. Producing for Television & New Media

The Trailer

Seinfeld has earned more than three billion dollars in syndication. A show that a network nearly cancelled after its first episodes became, once it had enough episodes to sell into the local stations' afternoons, the most profitable licensing asset in television - and when the streamers arrived, Netflix paid a reported half-billion for it, and Friends and The Office changed hands for sums that made the theatrical waterfall (P-3) look quaint. Television's economics are not film's: the series is a factory that produces a product weekly or by season, its authorship belongs to a writer-producer the trade calls the showrunner, and its money comes back not from a box office but from the licence - first to a network or platform, then to everyone else, for decades.

This final module is the medium most producers actually work in: the showrunner system that organises a series' authorship (the writers' room, the episodic directors, the producer titles that mean something different on a call sheet for a season), the streaming commission that has rewritten the deal (the platform ordering, financing and owning the show - House of Cards's first season ordered whole as the model's founding gesture; Sacred Games as India's; Squid Game as the model at global scale), and the licensing and syndication that are television's waterfall - the show's long life as an asset. Twelve modules ago you learned what a producer is. This module ends with the producer running a room, a season and a licence. The series is the film's economy, multiplied by episodes and decades.
