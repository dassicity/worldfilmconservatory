S-1 · Year 1 · Module One

The Physics & Perception of Sound

The Trailer

Inside your skull is a spiral the size of a pea that performs, continuously and without instruction, a Fourier analysis. The cochlea unrolls every arriving pressure wave into its component frequencies along a tapering membrane - low tones resonating at the far end, high ones near the entrance - and reports the spectrum to the brain as a living graph. Before there was any theory of sound, before there were microphones, mixing desks or Dolby, there was this organ, doing the mathematics at the speed of hearing. Every craft in this track exists to serve it - and, occasionally, to fool it.

The fooling is old. In 1877 Edison shouted a nursery rhyme into a horn and a tinfoil cylinder said it back - the first time a sound had ever existed twice - and audiences reportedly wept, or laughed, or accused him of ventriloquism. Fifty years later two Bell Labs engineers, Fletcher and Munson, sat listeners in booths and discovered that the ear is a liar with a schedule: it hears bass and treble more faintly at low volumes, which is why a mix that sounds balanced at cinema level sounds thin on a laptop, and why every loudness war since has been fought on the ear's own curved terrain. George Lucas is often quoted saying sound is half the movie experience; the line's exact wording drifts, and the track flags it as a working legend rather than a citation - but the proportion is the truth this module opens on. You do not hear the world. You hear a model of it, built by a spiral, and film sound is the art of building that model on purpose.

Three chapters: acoustics - waves, frequency, amplitude, and the rooms that shape them; psychoacoustics - how the ear and brain construct hearing, and where they can be led; and active versus passive listening - the discipline that turns a person with ears into a sound professional. First, learn the instrument you were born with.

Chapters

Sound is pressure travelling: a source vibrates, air compresses and rarefies in waves, and the wave's properties are the whole vocabulary. Frequency - cycles per second, in hertz - is heard as pitch; hearing spans roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz (the top shrinking with age and abuse), and the bands have jobs: sub-bass felt more than heard, the low-mids where mud and warmth both live, the presence band where consonants and intelligibility sit, the airy top that reads as detail. Amplitude is heard as loudness, measured in decibels - a LOGARITHMIC scale, because the ear is: doubling power adds three dB, doubling perceived loudness needs roughly ten, and the trade's meters (dBFS in the digital domain, dB SPL in the air) describe different things that beginners conflate. Timbre - why a cello and a voice at the same pitch differ - is the harmonic recipe: the fundamental plus overtones in shifting proportion, the spectrum the cochlea unrolls; and envelope (attack, decay, sustain, release) is timbre's shape in time - a plucked string and a bowed one differ mostly at the start. Phase is where waves stand relative to each other, and two microphones on one source at different distances teach you why: partial cancellation, comb filtering, the hollow sound of a scene mic'd carelessly. Then the ROOM: every space is an instrument - reflections arrive after the direct sound (early reflections defining a room's size to the ear, reverberant tail its material), RT60 measuring how long the tail takes to fall sixty dB, standing waves booming at frequencies set by the room's dimensions, and hard parallel walls producing the flutter echo that ruins location dialogue. The chapter's cinematography cross: sound obeys the same INVERSE-SQUARE law CL-2 taught for light - halve the distance, quadruple the intensity - and the "microphone close, then everything else" discipline of location recording is that law lived. And a working fact to keep forever: sound travels about 343 metres a second, slow enough that a cannon seen across a valley arrives late - which is why sync in the real world is negotiable, and why the cut can cheat it.

The ear reports; the brain composes - and the gap between them is the film sound designer's entire playground. Equal loudness: the Fletcher-Munson curves (refined since into the ISO standards) show the ear hearing bass and treble more faintly at low volumes, so balance depends on playback level - the reason mixes are made at calibrated reference loudness and why the home version needs its own consideration (S-8). Masking: a loud sound hides quieter ones nearby in frequency - the physics behind why dialogue drowns under a score in the same band, and why a designer carves a spectral SLOT for each element rather than piling everything up (S-6's soundscape law, born here). Localization: the brain triangulates a source from tiny timing and level differences between the ears (and the pinna's filtering - the HRTF that binaural recording exploits), and the PRECEDENCE effect - the first arrival wins, later reflections fused into it - is why a single delayed copy of a sound reads as room rather than as two sounds, and why surround formats can steer attention without the listener noticing. The cocktail-party effect: attention selects a voice from a crowd - which filmmakers can exploit (the one line rising out of a party mix) or defeat (Altman's overlapping chaos, coming in S-3). The missing fundamental: the brain infers a bass note from its overtones alone - how a phone speaker "plays" a bass line it cannot physically produce, and a trick mixers use for small-speaker translation. Audio-visual integration: the McGurk effect (seeing lips shape one syllable changes the syllable you hear) proves hearing is partly seeing - the physiological ground under Chion's synchresis (S-2). Auditory illusions: the Shepard tone climbing forever, the tritone paradox, phantom words in loops - all exploitable, all evidence that perception is inference. The chapter's working law: never design for the meter; design for the inference machine - what will THE LISTENER'S BRAIN conclude from this signal in this context? Everything in the track is that question.

S-2 · Year 1 · Module Two

Sound Theory

The Trailer

The story goes that Pythagoras taught from behind a curtain, so his students would attend to the words and not the man - and the word for those listeners, akousmatikoi, survived two and a half millennia to be picked up by Pierre Schaeffer in a Paris radio studio in the 1940s, cutting and looping recorded sounds until they detached from their sources and became, in his term, sound objects. The Pythagorean origin is a tradition, not a document, and the track flags it as such; the concept it carries is real and central: the ACOUSMATIC - sound heard without its cause seen - which turns out to be half of what film sound does, and most of what it does to frighten us.

Schaeffer's student Michel Chion took the idea into cinema and wrote the theory this track is built on. In Audio-Vision he ran the experiment every film student should repeat: the same footage with different soundtracks, and the image changed - a face grew sad, a room grew large, a cut grew violent - while the audience swore they were simply seeing. He called it added value, and named the weld that makes it possible synchresis: the brain's helpless fusion of a sound and a sight that happen together, regardless of whether they belong. Walter Murch, cutting The Conversation, had already discovered the same laws with his hands. Robert Bresson wrote them as aphorisms: the ear goes more toward the within, the eye toward the outer. Theory is not decoration here. It is the set of laws every craft chapter in this track obeys - and the reason a sound designer can make you see what was never filmed.

Three chapters: Chion's core - added value, synchresis, and the acousmatic; on-screen and off-screen sound - the frame's acoustic geography; and the three listening modes - causal, semantic, reduced. The laws first. The tools follow.

Chapters

Three laws, and the experiment that proves them. Added value: sound enriches an image with information or feeling the audience then attributes to the image itself - they believe they SAW the sadness, the size, the violence, when they heard it. The proof is Chion's masking method: watch a scene with sound off and describe it; then with picture off; then together - the third experience is not the sum of the first two, and the difference is added value. Every designer's power lives in that gap; every director who says "we'll fix it in the mix" is unknowingly citing it. Synchresis (synchronism plus synthesis): the involuntary weld between a sound and a visual event occurring at the same moment - the reason a punch dubbed with a melon strike reads as bone, why Foley works at all (S-7), why any sound can be married to any image if the timing is right. The law has an accomplice in S-1's McGurk effect: the brain WANTS to fuse. And it has limits: the weld holds best on discrete events; continuous sounds drift free. The acousmatic: sound whose source is not seen - the offscreen voice, the noise behind the door, the score itself (acousmatic by nature) - and its special case, the ACOUSMÊTRE: a voice heard but not embodied on screen, which Chion shows acquires powers (omniscience, ubiquity, authority) that evaporate the moment the body appears; de-acousmatization is the moment the Wizard is revealed behind the curtain, and horror runs on delaying it. Beneath the three: rendering versus reproduction (film sound gives the SENSATION of an event, not its recording - a real punch sounds like nothing; the rendered punch sounds like a punch), and the audiovisual contract - the audience's agreement to treat the two channels as one perception. The chapter's working law: you are never adding sound to a finished image; you are completing an image that does not yet exist.

The frame has edges; sound doesn't - and the difference is a design space. Chion's zones: ONSCREEN sound (source visible), OFFSCREEN sound (source in the story's space but outside the frame - the acousmatic, spatially placed), and NONDIEGETIC sound (outside the story's world: score, narration) - with the borders deliberately porous (a character's radio drifting between diegetic and score is a classic transition; "internal" sound - thoughts, remembered voices - a fourth zone with its own rules). Extension: how far the sound world reaches beyond the frame - narrow (only what's in shot) or vast (the city beyond the window, the war beyond the wall) - a dial the designer sets per scene, and one of the strongest tools for scale and claustrophobia alike. Point of audition: the ear's equivalent of point of view - whose hearing is the mix modelling? (a character's muffled panic, the distant party heard from the bedroom) - with the honest complication that audition is harder to pin to a body than vision, and film exploits the vagueness. The superfield: in multichannel cinema, the ambient sound world surrounds the audience and no longer needs the image to establish space - the frame becomes a window onto a world the ears already own, which changed editing itself (cuts no longer have to re-establish where we are). And the craft consequence this chapter exists for: OFFSCREEN SPACE IS NARRATIVE - the unseen is where horror, dread, scale and moral weight live; what you hear but cannot see is what you cannot stop imagining (The Zone of Interest builds a whole film on it), and the designer's decision about what stays acousmatic is as consequential as any cut. Bresson's dictum applies: when a sound can replace an image, cut the image.

S-1 trained the ear; this chapter names what it does. Causal listening: hearing a sound for its SOURCE - what made it, where, how big, how far; the everyday mode, and the one the audience mostly lives in - which is why designers can lie to it so productively (the causal ear identifies a tiger; it was a lion slowed down, and no one will ever know). Reduced listening: Schaeffer's mode - hearing the sound ITSELF, its pitch, grain, envelope, texture, independent of cause or meaning; the professional's working mode, and the hardest to sustain, because the brain keeps sliding back to "what was that?" The three run simultaneously and the craft is knowing which one the audience is in at every moment: the designer works in reduced mode (choosing textures) to serve the audience's causal mode (believing the world), while the dialogue editor guards semantic mode (understanding), and music moves between all three. Chion's later additions refine the map - sounds can be listened to for their INDEX (what they reveal about a person's state: the tremor in a voice) and for pure FIGURE (musical listening). The chapter's practical law: identify a scene's primary listening mode BEFORE designing it - a scene about understanding words is mixed differently from a scene about locating a threat, and a scene about texture (rain, machines, a body) differently again. The Conversation is the track's founding case: Harry Caul listening causally, then semantically, then - as the line "he'd kill us if he got the chance" is remixed in his ear - discovering that the same signal means something else depending on the mode, and losing his mind in the gap.

Production Sound & Location Recording

The Trailer

In 1958 a Polish-born engineer in Switzerland named Stefan Kudelski put a professional tape recorder in a box you could carry, and the Nagra went out into the world and changed what films could be. Before it, sync sound meant trucks, cables and studios; after it, a two-person crew could follow a senator through a hotel corridor or a rock band into a dressing room with the sound running - Direct Cinema, the New Wave's street dialogue, and every handheld revolution since owe their existence to a machine whose name meant "will record" in Kudelski's Polish. The recordist became mobile, and the whole idea of where a film could happen moved with them.

The department's folklore is thick, and the module flags it as it goes. MOS - the slate mark for a shot rolled without sound - is explained on every set as a German émigré director's "mit out sound," a story so beloved and so undocumented that the track files it under apocrypha with affection. What is documented is the craft: the boom operator's ballet, holding a pole overhead for twelve hours while dancing out of the light and the frame (CL-7's flags and S-3's booms negotiate the same six inches of ceiling), the lavalier hidden in a collar, the room tone recorded in a silence the whole crew must hold, the sound report handed to post like a chain-of-custody document. Location sound is the track's most physical craft and its least forgiving: a beautiful image can be re-lit in the grade, but a line of dialogue lost to a generator is gone, and everything Year 2 builds stands on what this module's crew brought home. Record it right. Everything else is optional.

Chapters

A microphone is a transducer with opinions. The types: DYNAMIC (a coil in a magnet - rugged, insensitive, needing no power; the stage vocal mic, rarely the film one), CONDENSER (a charged capsule needing phantom power - sensitive, detailed, the location and studio standard, from large-diaphragm studio mics to the small-diaphragm pencils and shotguns of the set), and RIBBON (a metal strip in a field - warm, figure-eight, delicate; a studio colour more than a set tool). The patterns - the shape of a mic's hearing - decide everything on location: OMNIDIRECTIONAL hears equally all round (natural, immune to proximity boom, the lavalier's usual pattern, and the honest choice for ambiences); CARDIOID rejects the rear (the general-purpose interior boom pattern); HYPERCARDIOID and SUPERCARDIOID narrow the front and grow a small rear lobe (the indoor dialogue workhorses - tight enough to reject room, forgiving enough to survive a moving actor); the SHOTGUN adds an interference tube for still narrower reach - the exterior standard, and the famous indoor trap: the tube's off-axis rejection colours reflected sound, so a shotgun in a reverberant room hears the room's reflections as a hollow, phasey smear - which is why the hypercardioid rules interiors; and FIGURE-EIGHT hears front and back, rejecting the sides (the ribbon's nature, and M-S stereo's partner). The specifications that matter: self-noise (how much hiss the mic contributes - critical for quiet scenes), sensitivity, maximum SPL, and the PROXIMITY effect (directional mics boost bass as they close in - the radio voice, and a plot point when a boom drifts). Wind and handling: foam for breath, the blimp-and-furry for exteriors, shock mounts for pole rumble - the wind that ruins a take is a physics problem with a fur solution. The chapter's law: choose the pattern for the ROOM and the distance you can actually achieve, not for the spec sheet - S-1's inverse-square law means the best mic three feet away loses to a modest one at eighteen inches.

Every line of dialogue arrives by one of two roads, and the craft is knowing which, and usually taking both. The boom is the department's first instrument: a directional mic on a pole, held above the frame line (or below it, when the shot allows), aimed at the mouth from the closest legal distance - and the BOOM OPERATOR is its artist: cueing every line from a memorized script, swinging between speakers a beat BEFORE they speak, holding perspective (a wide shot's voice should sound a little wider than a close-up's - the boom's distance IS the perspective), and dancing continuously out of the frame, out of the lights (the shadow on the wall is the boom's cardinal sin, and CL-7's flags are its negotiating partners), and out of the actors' eyelines. The boom's sound is the sound the trade prefers - full, natural, perspective-true, a single mic capturing the room's reality - and "boom first" is the doctrine: the lav is insurance. The lavalier - the small omni clipped or hidden on the body - is the road the boom can't take: wide shots with no overhead room, walking-and-talking exteriors, whispering, dancing, overlapping ensembles (Nashville's lesson), any shot where the pole would show. Its craft is HIDING (under a collar, in a tie knot, taped to the chest with the mounting tricks - the moleskin, the vampire clip - that defeat clothing rustle) and its curse is that hiding colours it (chest resonance, fabric muffling, the rustle of a costume the designer refused to change); WIRELESS is its transport, and RF coordination - frequency planning, antenna placement, the interference from LED panels and every phone on set - is a sub-craft with its own war stories. The chapter's law: the lav saves the take; the boom makes the film - and the operator who can hold both truths in one setup is the department's centre of gravity.

S-4 · Year 1 · Module Four

The Digital Audio Workstation

The Trailer

For sixty years the sound editor's instrument was a razor blade. Dialogue, effects and music lived on 35mm magnetic stock - film-shaped tape with sprocket holes - and an editor built a reel by physically cutting and splicing it, track by track, on a synchronizer whose sprockets kept everything in step with the picture. A crossfade meant a diagonal cut. A mistake meant tape. When Walter Murch layered Apocalypse Now's helicopters into the first quintaphonic mix, he did it with rooms full of mag and a schedule measured in seasons; the credit he invented for himself on that film - sound designer - described an architect of layers built by hand.

In 1989 two former drummers, Peter Gotcher and Evan Brooks, released a Macintosh program called Sound Tools, having earlier sold digital drum-sample chips to musicians under the company name Digidesign. Two years later it became Pro Tools, and within a decade it had done to the mag bench what the Avid did to the Moviola: made the cut reversible, the layers infinite, and the session portable between rooms. The trade standardised on it less for its features than for INTERCHANGE - a session that opens identically on a dialogue editor's laptop and a dub stage's console is worth more than any plug-in - and that standardisation is why this module teaches one platform's conventions while insisting the principles belong to all of them. The DAW is the razor blade with an undo key. Learn it the way the old editors learned the blade: as an instrument, not an appliance.

Three chapters: Pro Tools and the workflow - sessions, tracks, conventions, and why the standard is a standard; editing fundamentals - the cuts, fades and disciplines of non-destructive work; and sync - frame rates, pull-downs, the 2-pop, and staying married to a picture that keeps changing. Chapters

The session is the room you work in; learn its architecture before its buttons. The anatomy: TRACKS (audio, aux, master, VCA, instrument) holding CLIPS (references to files on disk - non-destructive, so a cut never damages the recording); PLAYLISTS (alternate versions of a track's edit, the DAW's parallel universes); BUSES routing signal between tracks; SENDS feeding a copy to a reverb or a submix; and the mixer's hierarchy - individual tracks summed into stems, stems into the master - which is also S-8's whole structure in miniature. The standards: film post runs at 48 kHz and 24-bit (the sample rate the picture world speaks; the bit depth that gives headroom without heroics), and sessions are laid out by CONVENTION so any editor can open any other's work - dialogue tracks first (DX), then Foley (FOL), effects (FX), backgrounds (BG), music (MX), each group colour-coded, each track named by content and character, with the picture track and guide audio on top. The convention is not fussiness: the dub stage will load your session and needs to know at a glance where everything lives. The turnover: picture editorial hands over an AAF (or the older OMF) - the edit's sound as clips with handles - plus a reference movie with burnt-in timecode, an EDL, and change lists as the cut evolves; the sound team CONFORMS the AAF into the template, links to the original field recordings (the mixer's ISO tracks from S-3, retrieved by timecode and file metadata - the reason S-3's reports mattered), and the session is born. The alternatives: Nuendo (deep in game and immersive work), Reaper, Logic, Fairlight inside Resolve - all capable, all speaking these same principles; the standard is a standard because a session must travel, and this chapter's lesson is to learn the CONVENTIONS as portable knowledge and the platform as this year's vehicle. File hygiene closes: naming, folder structure, saving copies with media, backing up before every conform - the DAW's razor has undo, but the disk does not forgive.

The blade's grammar, rebuilt in software. The cut: edit at ZERO CROSSINGS (where the waveform passes through silence) or across a tiny fade, or the cut clicks - the DAW's most basic courtesy; cut on TRANSIENTS when rhythm matters (a footstep's attack, a door's impact) because the ear locates events by their starts. The fade: every clip boundary should carry one - a few milliseconds hides the seam; CROSSFADES join two clips across an overlap, and their shape matters: equal-power curves keep the loudness steady across the join (the default for most joins), equal-gain suits highly correlated material, and long asymmetric fades are the dialogue editor's smoothing tool (S-5). Handles: the extra material beyond a clip's edges, preserved by the turnover - the reason you can extend a fade; a turnover without handles is a session without options. Modes and gestures: slip (move a clip freely), shuffle (clips snap end-to-end), spot (place by timecode - the sound editor's mode), grid; nudging by frames or samples; trimming; separating; the keyboard as the instrument, learned until the mouse is an insult. Levels: CLIP GAIN adjusts a clip's level before the fader - the editor's tool for evening a performance so the mixer inherits consistency; the FADER is the mixer's tool; confusing the two is the classic beginner's mess (S-8 explains why mixers curse edited-in fader automation). Organisation: markers and memory locations as the session's index, clip groups for repeated elements, spotting notes as the session's to-do list. And the principle beneath all of it: NON-DESTRUCTIVE means every choice is reversible, which means the discipline shifts from caution to DECISION - cut boldly, keep playlists of alternatives, and commit when the cut serves the scene; the editors who cut mag learned to decide because they had to; you must learn it because you don't.

Sync is the department's marriage vow, and the arithmetic is where it's kept. Frame rates: film's 24, cinema's digital 24 or 23.976 (the fraction inherited from television's colour-era compromise), broadcast's 25 (PAL regions) and 29.97 (NTSC regions - with DROP-FRAME timecode's periodic skipped numbers keeping the clock honest against the fraction, and NON-DROP's steady count drifting from wall time), and the high-frame-rate experiments; a session must be set to the picture's rate before anything is placed, or every event lands progressively wrong. Pull-up and pull-down: converting between 24 and 23.976 (or film's 24 and NTSC's 29.97 through telecine) changes duration by 0.1 percent - sound must be stretched or squeezed to match (the pull-down), an invisible fraction that, unaccounted, unsyncs a reel by frames by its end; the post supervisor's checklist item that has ruined more dailies than any creative choice. The references: the 2-POP - a single frame of 1 kHz tone exactly two seconds before first frame of action, matched to a visible flash frame in the leader - the universal sync handshake between rooms; head and tail leaders; burnt-in timecode on the reference movie; and the AAF's own timestamps - checked against the 2-pop every time a new picture arrives. Conforming: picture changes (the edit continues while sound builds), delivered as CHANGE LISTS or new AAFs; the sound team re-conforms - sliding, trimming and inserting its work to the new cut with conform tools that compare old and new EDLs - and the discipline is version hygiene: every session named to its picture version, every change list logged, no work done against a stale cut. Perception: the audience tolerates sound slightly LATE (a frame or two - consistent with the physics of S-1, where sound arrives after light) far better than early; lip-sync drift of a few frames is noticed by everyone and named by no one - "something felt off" is a sync problem's usual review. And the historical frame the screening room supplies: whole cinemas were built on loose sync - Italy's post-dubbed decades, the Indian studio era - where sync was an aesthetic tolerance rather than a technical vow; know that tradition, then keep the vow anyway.

Dialogue

The Trailer

When Darth Vader walks onto the Tantive IV, the man inside the suit is David Prowse, a Bristol weightlifter, and the voice that arrives with him belongs to James Earl Jones, recorded later in a studio in a few hours, uncredited at his own request on the first film. The most recognisable villain in cinema is an ADR session. Audiences have never minded, because they never knew - which is the entire ethic of this module: dialogue work is measured by its invisibility, and the dialogue editor is the only artist in the building whose masterpiece is the absence of any evidence that they exist.

The history is full of borrowed voices. Marni Nixon sang for Audrey Hepburn, Natalie Wood and Deborah Kerr, uncredited, while the studios denied it. Italian cinema dubbed everything for decades - the story that Fellini's actors sometimes recited numbers on set, since the words would be supplied later, is reported so widely and documented so thinly that the track files it as legend with a grain of practice inside. The Hindi studio era dubbed its dialogue in post as standard; Sholay's most quoted lines were performed twice, once for the camera and once for the microphone. And on the quiet end of the craft, every dialogue scene you have ever believed was smoothed by someone splitting lines between microphones, filling the gaps with the room tone S-3 begged the crew to hold still for, and removing a hundred lip-smacks you would have heard as a faint wrongness without ever knowing why. Year 1 ends on the voice - the element the audience listens to semantically, forgives least, and notices only when the invisible art fails.

Chapters

The dialogue editor receives a conformed session (S-4) and delivers a track that sounds as if the whole scene was recorded in one uninterrupted take by one perfect microphone - and it never was. The splits: the guide track is a Frankenstein of takes, angles and mics, and the first labour is dividing it - by CHARACTER (each voice on its own track or pair, so the mixer can treat them separately), by MICROPHONE (boom and lav on different tracks - they have different colours and will need different EQ), and by CHECKERBOARDING (alternating clips between A and B tracks so every join can crossfade across an overlap instead of butting). The fill: every gap, every cut between angles where the background jumps (a wide's room tone differs from a close-up's - the boom moved), is bridged with FILL - room tone (S-3's gift) laid beneath and between lines so the scene's noise floor becomes one continuous presence; when no room tone was recorded, fill is stolen from the pauses within takes, looped and disguised - a craft of hours the crew could have saved in sixty seconds of standing still. Perspective: the boom's distance (S-3) gave each angle a spatial truth; the editor preserves it where the cut supports it and evens it where it distracts, choosing between the wide's air and the close-up's intimacy line by line. Take selection and matching: a line may come from a different take than its picture if the performance is cleaner and the sync survives (a few frames of tolerance, S-4); alternate takes and the lav's copy are the editor's rescue kit before ADR is called. The removals: lip smacks, clicks, chair creaks, the boom's brush against a doorframe, the director's whispered cue - surgically excised with fill beneath. PFX: production effects (a real door, a set glass) split to their own tracks so the mixer can keep or replace them, and so the M&E (S-8) can drop the dialogue without losing the world. The delivery: tracks laid out by the S-4 convention, clip gain evened, fades everywhere, a notes document listing every line that needs ADR - the premix's foundation. The chapter's law: if the mixer can hear where you worked, you haven't finished.

Automated Dialogue Replacement - the "automated" a relic of the machines that once cued the loops - is the art of recording a line again, months later, in a room that sounds like nothing, and making it belong. The history in the name: LOOPING - a few seconds of picture spliced into a physical loop, run round and round while the actor matched it; now a cue on a screen with BEEPS (three tones counting in to a silent fourth where the line begins) and STREAMERS (a line wiping across the picture to the sync point), but the word persists. Why it's called: noise the editor couldn't rescue (S-3's generator, the plane, the silk blouse), a performance the director wants changed, a line rewritten after the shoot, censorship versions, foreign versions, a whisper that never reached the mic, and - the oldest reason - an actor's voice replaced entirely (Vader; the uncredited singers; the dubbing industries below). The stage: the ADR MIXER matching the production microphone (same model, same distance, same pattern - the closer the match, the less CH 3 must fake), the actor watching picture with the guide in headphones, the SUPERVISOR running the cue sheet (every line numbered, timecoded, annotated with the reason), and the director's job - the chapter's most important teaching - directing PERFORMANCE, not lips: an actor asked only to match sync delivers a corpse; an actor re-inhabiting the scene (the energy, the breath, the physical state - running in place for a running line) delivers something the editor can fit; sync is the editor's problem, life is the actor's, and great ADR directors protect that division. The fit: ADR is edited to picture (phrase by phrase, sometimes syllable by syllable, with elastic-time tools as last resort), then matched to the production track in CH 3. The traditions the worldwide mandate demands: Italy's post-dubbed golden age (Fellini, Leone - S-4's screening), where dubbing was the national method and voices were a separate craft with their own stars; India's studio era, dialogue routinely re-recorded in post, and the modern dubbing industry translating southern blockbusters into Hindi and Hindi into everything, with dubbing artists whose voices are famous in their own right; the anime and European dubbing cultures where the dub is an artform not an apology. And walla and loop groups: the crowd's voice, recorded by specialist actors improvising period-appropriate chatter that never lands a real word (English "rhubarb" legend included, flagged as folklore), layered by distance and language into every public space in cinema. The chapter's law: ADR is not a repair; it is a second performance - cast it, direct it, and honour it as such.

The tools have become miraculous; the craft is knowing when to stop. The repair suite: SPECTRAL EDITING - the recording displayed as a spectrogram (S-1's picture of sound, now a canvas) where a cough, a car horn or a phone chirp is a visible shape to be painted out and replaced with interpolated surroundings; DE-NOISE (broadband reduction learned from a noise profile - the hiss, the hum, the air conditioner subtracted); DE-CLICK and DE-CRACKLE; DE-RUSTLE for the lav's costume complaints; DE-REVERB for the room the boom couldn't escape; DIALOGUE ISOLATION (machine-learning separation of voice from everything) - each a tool that would have been science fiction to the mag generation, each with a cost: over-processed dialogue acquires the PLASTIC voice - swirly artifacts, a hollow, underwater sheen, consonants smeared - and the trade's wisdom is that a little honest noise beats a lot of artificial silence; clean to the point the mix can hide the rest, then stop. Matching: the boom and the lav (S-3's two roads) EQ'd toward each other so a cut between them is inaudible (the lav's chest boom cut, its muffled top restored; the boom's air respected); ADR matched to production - EQ to the production mic's colour, then the ROOM added: reverb matched to the location, or the ADR WORLDIZED (played back through a speaker in a similar space and re-recorded - the analogue trick S-6 will use on effects), or convolved with an impulse response captured on set; the goal is a line that lives in the same air as its neighbours. Futz: dialogue deliberately degraded - the phone voice, the radio, the intercom - with band-limiting, distortion and the specific artifacts of each device (the craft here is accuracy: a 1940s telephone and a modern mobile fail in different ways). De-essing and dynamics: sibilance tamed, levels evened by clip gain and gentle compression so the mixer inherits a consistent voice. The premix: the dialogue's own pass on the stage (S-8) where the editor's smoothing is auditioned in the room it must survive - the last place a seam can be found. And the ethical frame: the voice is the element the audience forgives least and the machine can now most easily counterfeit; the craft's boundaries - repair versus fabrication, matching versus replacing - are yours to hold, and the module ends by naming that responsibility as part of the job.

S-6 · Year 2 · Module Six

Sound Design

The Trailer

Year 2 opens on daylight stock with a young man holding a microphone up to a bear. Ben Burtt, hired in 1975 to find "the sounds" for a space movie nobody at the studio understood, spent a year not synthesising but COLLECTING: a brown bear named Pooh at a California animal park became Chewbacca's grief and rage; the hum of an idling film projector's motor, mixed with the buzz a television picked up from a bare microphone cable, became the lightsaber; an elephant's cry stretched over a car on wet pavement became a TIE fighter's scream. The doctrine that emerged - that invented worlds should be built from ORGANIC recordings so they carry the world's own physics - is the module's first law, and Burtt's library, still growing, is its founding archive.

The credit "sound designer" itself was invented a few years earlier, by Walter Murch on Apocalypse Now, to describe someone who thought about a film's sound as a whole rather than as a pile of departments; Randy Thom, who came up under both men, wrote the essay every director should be handed on day one - Designing a Movie for Sound - arguing that sound is decided at the script stage, not rescued at the mix. And the sound most people can name from the era is a joke: the Wilhelm scream, a 1951 library cry that Burtt found, named after a character who uttered it, and planted in Star Wars as a private signature - now in hundreds of films, a running gag that proves the library is a living tradition with a sense of humour. Sound design is worldbuilding by ear. Record the world; then teach it to lie beautifully.

Three chapters: building soundscapes and the library - layers, beds, and the archive's discipline; designed versus recorded effects, and worldizing - how sounds are invented, and how they're taught to live in rooms; and the Murch–Burtt–Thom lineage - the founders, the school they built, and the global generation after them. Chapters

A soundscape is layers with jobs. The strata: BACKGROUNDS (BG - the beds: room tone's designed cousin, the traffic, the wind, the hum of a world - continuous, low in the mix, and the element that most defines a space's reality and mood; S-1's Schafer vocabulary of keynotes returns here as design), HARD EFFECTS (the sync sounds of visible events - doors, cars, gunshots - cut to picture), DESIGNED ELEMENTS (the sounds that don't exist until you make them - the creature, the machine, the dread tone), and the two strata other modules own (dialogue from S-5, Foley from S-7, music from S-9) with which everything here must share the spectrum. The slot discipline: S-1's masking law becomes the designer's first rule - every element needs its own frequency and time slot, so a soundscape is carved as much as built: the bed thinned where dialogue sits, the effect pitched away from the score, the dread tone placed where nothing else lives; Murch's "two-and-a-half" (S-2's essay) sets the ceiling on how many distinct things the mind will track at once, and the art is choosing which. The sound map: Sonnenschein's script-stage method - reading for every scene's sonic world, listing its beds and events and emotional sounds BEFORE picture exists - is where design starts (Thom's essay's whole point), and the map becomes the brief for recording and library work. The library: commercial collections (the great catalogues, from the studio archives to the boutique field recordists), the designer's own recordings (S-3's skills turned outward - Burtt's year with a microphone), and the organisation that makes any of it usable: METADATA (descriptions, sources, microphones, dates), the UNIVERSAL CATEGORY SYSTEM's naming convention that the trade has adopted so libraries speak one language, and the search-and-audition habits that let a designer find the right door in seconds among ten thousand. The chapter's law: the bed is the world; the hard effects are its events; the designed elements are its meaning - and the library is the memory that lets you build all three tomorrow faster than today.

The designed sound is a recorded sound that has been taught to lie. The organic-source doctrine: Burtt's law that invented things should be built from real recordings - because a real source carries irregularity, physics and life that synthesis must laboriously fake; the designer's first instinct is therefore to RECORD (the field trip, the prop session, the animal park) and the second to TRANSFORM. S-7 · Year 2 · Module Seven

Foley

The Trailer

In 1927 Universal Pictures had a silent film called Show Boat and a problem: the studio next door had released The Jazz Singer, and audiences suddenly wanted to hear things. A former stuntman and prop-shop hand named Jack Foley was sent to a stage with a screen and a microphone to add sound to a finished picture - and rather than hunt for recordings, he PERFORMED it: walking in sync with the actors, rustling cloth, setting down cups, live to picture, for the rest of a career that gave the craft his name. The story of his first session has been polished by decades of retelling and the track flags its details as trade legend; the method it describes is the whole of this module, unchanged in principle for a century.

The stage looks like a junk shop designed by a choreographer: pits in the floor filled with gravel, sand, marble, wood and water; racks of shoes; a wall of doors that open onto nothing; drawers of chains, cutlery, leather, celery for bones and coconuts for hooves - and in the middle of it, an artist watching a screen, walking. Foley is the department that gives every film its body: the footsteps and cloth that S-3's microphones never captured, the props that must exist separately for the foreign versions S-8 will deliver, and the intimate, human-scale sounds that make a character present in a room. It is also - the module's second chapter insists - an ACTING craft: a walk has weight, mood, gender, age and intent, and the artist who performs it is playing the character as surely as the actor on screen. The library gives you the world. Foley gives it a body, and the body a performer.

Two chapters: the craft after Jack Foley - footsteps, moves and specifics on the stage; and performing sound - Foley as acting, and the artists who made it one. Cue the walk.

Chapters

Three categories, one stage, two people. Footsteps: the foundation - every step on screen re-performed on a matching surface (the pits: concrete, wood, gravel, grass mat, marble, carpet, water; the portable surfaces; the boards that stand in for stairs), in matching shoes (the rack: heels, boots, sneakers, bare feet - the artist's collection is a career's accumulation), in sync with the actor's weight and rhythm; steps are cut to picture afterward only for correction, because the point of Foley is the LIVE sync that no library step can match. Moves: the cloth track - every gesture's rustle, the sleeve on a table, the coat as a body turns, the sheets in a bed - performed with fabrics held and worked in the artist's hands while watching the scene; the least noticed and most essential layer, the sound that tells the ear a body is present, and the layer that most exposes a soundtrack when it's missing (the M&E without moves sounds like ghosts). Specifics: the props - the cup on the saucer, the key in the lock, the punch (leather, celery, a phone book), the sword (a metal rod and a hand-swipe), the horse (the coconut, still), the gunshot's handling (never the shot itself - that's design) - each a small performance with a physical object chosen for how it sounds, not what it is. Why Foley when a library exists: sync (the performance locks to picture in one pass where library cutting takes hours), intimacy (the close-mic'd stage captures human scale the location boom couldn't), consistency (one artist's walk for one character across a film), and the M&E - foreign versions (S-8) strip the production track and its PFX with the dialogue, so every sound the world makes must exist separately, and Foley is how. S-8 · Year 2 · Module Eight

The Mix

The Trailer

The dub stage is a cinema with a desk in the middle of it. For most of the sound era the desk had three chairs - one mixer riding dialogue, one effects, one music - and the film's final soundtrack was a live performance: reel by reel, faders under fingers, the director in the row behind saying "again" until the balance felt like the film. The word "dub" survives from dubbing, the old practice of re-recording a mix onto a new track, and the trade's other name for the job - RE-RECORDING mixer - says exactly what happens: everything Year 1 and Year 2 built is played back through the room the audience will sit in, and recorded one last time, together.

The room's history is a history of channels. Mono for four decades; Dolby Stereo's matrix in 1975 putting Star Wars' starships into the walls of ordinary cinemas; digital 5.1 arriving with Batman Returns in 1992; 7.1; and in 2012 a Pixar film about a Scottish princess became the first released in Dolby Atmos - a format that stopped thinking in channels and started thinking in OBJECTS, sounds with coordinates that the cinema's processor renders to however many speakers the room has, including the ones in the ceiling. And under all of it, the discipline that never changed: the mix is made at a calibrated reference level so that what the mixer hears is what the audience hears, and the moment that discipline slips, the loudness wars begin. The mix is where the film finally becomes one sound. Sit down. Ride it.

Four chapters: pre-dubs, temps, stems and the final dub - the stage's stages; re-recording mixing - the craft of balance and the chairs that perform it; Atmos and immersive formats - from mono to objects; and M&E and deliverables - the versions that let a film speak every language. The room is calibrated. Roll reel one.

Chapters

The mix is not an event but a sequence. The temp mix: long before the final, picture editorial needs a soundtrack for screenings, previews and the director's own judgment - the TEMP, built from the guide track, library effects and temp music (S-9's famous danger), rough but persuasive; its job is to let the film be evaluated, its risk is that everyone falls in love with it. The pre-dubs (premixes): the departments' tracks are too many to balance at once (a feature can run to hundreds), so each is mixed DOWN first - the dialogue premix (S-5's edit auditioned in the room, smoothed, levelled, placed), the effects premix (S-6's beds and hard effects balanced among themselves), the Foley premix (S-7's feet and cloth placed in the picture's rooms), the music premix (S-9's cues levelled and positioned) - each delivered as a manageable set of tracks, each mixer or the single mixer working department by department before the departments meet. The final dub: the premixes combined on the stage, reel by reel, with the director present - the balance between departments decided, the film's dynamic shape performed, notes taken and played back, the mix REVISED in the room until the last reel is approved; the final's tempo is set by the notes culture: fast directors, slow directors, directors who hear a line at 2 dB and ones who feel a scene. Stems: the final's outputs kept SEPARATE - dialogue, music, effects (and often Foley and backgrounds as their own) - so that every deliverable in CH 4 can be built without remixing, and so a change to one department in a later version needn't touch the others; stems are the mix's DNA, and a film delivered without them is a film that can never be re-versioned. The printmaster: the combined stems rendered to each format - the theatrical 5.1, 7.1, Atmos - the thing the cinema actually plays. The rooms: the theatrical stage (a cinema, calibrated), the near-field room (the home-scale monitors where the streaming version is checked), and the discipline of hearing the film in both before calling it done. The chapter's law: the mix is built in stages so it can be changed in stages - premix to isolate, final to unify, stems to preserve - and the mixer who skips a stage pays for it in the next version.

The craft in the chair. Hierarchy: dialogue first - the semantic layer (S-2) protected absolutely, every other element balanced around intelligibility; then whichever of music or effects the scene is ABOUT (the mixer's first question per scene: what is the audience listening for?); and the ruthless subtraction that follows - the mix is made by what's turned DOWN, and the beginner's mix is loud everywhere because nothing was chosen. Balance in context: level is meaningless alone - a footstep at the right level under dialogue is wrong under silence; the mixer works the relationships, not the meters, and the film's DYNAMIC SHAPE (where it whispers, where it roars, how it arrives at each) is the mix's largest structure, planned reel by reel. The tools, as used on the stage: EQ to fit elements together (carving S-1's slots in real time), dynamics for control rather than colour, PANNING to place and to move (the frame's geography from S-2 honoured - dialogue centre, effects placed, music wide; the hard rule that a voice leaving the screen's centre should mean something), REVERB as the room every element must share (S-5's matching finished here - the Foley given the location's air, the ADR settled into the set), and AUTOMATION - every fader move recorded, refined, performed again - which is why S-4 forbade editors from baking levels into fader data: the mixer's automation is the mix. Reference and fatigue: the stage is calibrated to the theatrical reference (the standard 85 dB SPL at the mix position for a specified signal) so decisions translate to cinemas; the ears tire within hours, judgment drifting quieter and brighter, and professional mixers schedule against fatigue and check the morning's work in the afternoon. The chairs: the three-chair tradition (dialogue/effects/music, each a specialist - the Hollywood model) versus the single or dual re-recording mixer (the European and independent norm, and increasingly everywhere) - with the same craft either way, and the same relationship at its centre: the DIRECTOR's notes, translated from feeling to fader ("it feels heavy" may mean the low bed, the music's cellos, or the reverb's tail - the mixer's genius is knowing which). Mixing to picture: cuts and camera moves are mix cues - a perspective change on a cut, a swell on a push-in, a hard silence on a shock cut; the mixer reads picture like the Foley artist reads the actor. The chapter's law: mixing is listening on the audience's behalf, at their level, in their room, with the director's feeling as the score - and the fader is the instrument you perform it on.

The history, as a set of decisions about where sound may live. Mono: one channel behind the screen, the sound era's first forty years - and still the discipline underneath everything (a mix that works in mono has its hierarchy right). Dolby Stereo (1975): four channels - left, centre, right, and a mono surround - matrixed into two optical tracks, so ordinary cinemas could carry Star Wars' starships into the walls; the LCRS layout that put dialogue in the centre speaker for good. Digital 5.1 (1992): five discrete full-range channels plus a low-frequency effects channel - left and right surrounds finally separate, the LFE giving the trade its felt bass - the format the industry mixed in for two decades and still delivers everywhere; then 7.1's split rears. Atmos (2012): the conceptual break - a BED (a 7.1.2 channel layout, the ".2" its first overhead pair) plus OBJECTS (individual sounds with position metadata, up to 118 of them, placed in three-dimensional space in the session) which the cinema's RENDERER maps to whatever speakers that room actually has, including the ceiling arrays; the mix is no longer a set of channels but a description of a space, rendered per venue - and, crucially, rendered DOWN to 7.1, 5.1, stereo and headphones from one master (the binaural render that has made Atmos a music-streaming format too). The craft in objects: what belongs in the bed (ambiences, the world's presence) versus an object (the helicopter passing overhead, the voice that circles), the restraint the format demands (Chion's superfield from S-2 realised - space established by sound alone, so the discipline is not to fill it), the EXIT-SIGN EFFECT (a sound placed hard in the surrounds pulls the audience's attention to the wall and out of the film - surrounds for envelopment, screen for story, and objects moved only with narrative reason), and the near-field problem (the home mix hears the same objects through a soundbar - the mixer checks both). The other formats: DTS:X, Auro-3D, the IMAX twelve-channel rooms, and the headphone renders that now reach more listeners than any cinema. The chapter's law: every format is a set of permissions, and the mixer's art is the same in each - put sound where the STORY is, use the rest for air, and let the void be the void.

Music for Film I - The Composer's Craft

The Trailer

When John Williams first played Steven Spielberg the theme for Jaws - two notes, alternating, on the low end of a piano - the director laughed, assuming it was a joke. Williams played it again. The notes, he explained, were the shark: simple, relentless, and audible long before anything surfaced - and the film's most effective scenes would work because the music arrived when the animal did not. Spielberg has told the story often enough that its exact wording has drifted; the track flags the dialogue as anecdote and the lesson as fact. Two notes, placed by a composer who understood what the picture needed, did what a mechanical shark could not.

The module's other founding stories are about the relationship failing. Bernard Herrmann and Alfred Hitchcock, after Psycho, Vertigo and North by Northwest, broke over Torn Curtain: the studio wanted a pop score, Herrmann delivered brass and terror, Hitchcock rejected it, and the two never worked together again. Stanley Kubrick commissioned Alex North for 2001, cut the picture to a temp track of Strauss and Ligeti, fell in love with it, and never told North the score he'd written and recorded was gone - the composer learned at the premiere. Every composer since has known the phrase "temp love" as a diagnosis. This module is about the collaboration that produces a score: the spotting session where director and composer decide where music lives and what it does; the line between the music the characters hear and the music only we hear; and the two great tools - the leitmotif and the temp track - one a gift from Wagner, the other a trap the trade keeps walking into on purpose. The composer's craft is half music and half conversation. Learn both, or the score dies at the premiere.

Three chapters: spotting sessions and the director - where music goes, and how to talk about it; diegetic and non-diegetic - screen music, source music, and the border between; and leitmotif and the temp score - the theme as memory, and the temporary track that keeps replacing the real one. Play it again.

Chapters

The score begins as a conversation in a dark room. The spotting session: director, composer, picture editor and MUSIC EDITOR screen the (near-)locked cut together and decide, scene by scene, where music STARTS, where it STOPS, and what it does - the three questions that define a score more than any melody; each decision becomes a CUE, numbered by reel and position (1M1, 1M2, 2M1 - the trade's notation), with in-and-out timecodes and a description of function ("underscore the approach; hit the reveal; out on the door"). The music editor: the department's unsung specialist - keeps the cue sheet, builds and maintains the temp track (CH 3), prepares timing notes and click tracks, conforms cues when the picture changes (S-4's discipline applied to music), and edits the final recordings to picture on the stage; the composer's most important ally after the director. Talking about music: directors rarely speak in musical terms, and the composer's craft is TRANSLATION - adjectives ("lonely," "expensive," "don't tell us how to feel") into instrumentation and harmony; reference films and temp tracks into intent rather than imitation; the trick many composers describe of asking what the music should NOT do. The sketch and the mockup: themes presented at the piano or as synthesized mockups (the modern norm - a director hears a full orchestral approximation before a note is recorded), revised in rounds, approved before the expensive session. The timeline: composers are hired late and paid for speed - weeks for a feature, the cut still changing, cues rewritten to each conform - and the professional's survival skills are the music editor's timing notes, a template that renders fast, and a director relationship built on early, honest sketches rather than late surprises. The session: the scoring stage, players reading to a click and streamers, the composer conducting or supervising, the director present for the first time hearing the orchestra - and the mix (S-8) receiving the cues as stems. The chapter's law: music is decided at the spotting session, not the piano - a perfect cue in the wrong place is worse than silence, and silence is a decision the session must be brave enough to make.

Talking about music, without music.

Two kinds of music, and the most interesting things happen at the border. Non-diegetic score: music the characters cannot hear - the classical film score, Gorbman's "unheard melody," conventionally inaudible in the sense that audiences feel it without noticing it, cueing emotion, continuity, character and time; its rules (the classical Hollywood model) - invisibility, emotional signification, narrative cueing, unity through themes - are the assumptions every score either obeys or argues with. Diegetic (source) music: music that exists in the story's world - the radio, the band, the jukebox, the character singing - subject to S-2's geography (it has a place, a distance, a room; it can be off-screen; it obeys perspective) and to the mix's rules for placing it (S-8: worldized, futzed, panned to its source). The crossings: source music swelling into score as a scene's emotion outgrows its radio (the "As Time Goes By" move - a piano in a bar becoming an orchestra in memory), score revealed as source (the music we took for underscore turning out to be a record playing), and the "meta-diegetic" - music heard in a character's mind, or imagined, or remembered (a musical's numbers, a dream); each crossing is a narrative event, and Gorbman's framework lets you name it. The needle drop: licensed songs as score - Scorsese's jukebox method (the pop record as period, character and irony at once), the New Hollywood's song-scores, the music supervisor's craft of clearance and placement (a role S-12 formalises), and the honest debate about songs that comment versus songs that decorate. Source scoring: the middle path - a cue written to sound like source (a period dance band, a street musician) so the score can hide inside the world. The musical: the genre where the border is the whole game - characters who hear the orchestra, numbers that step out of the diegesis and return, the playback craft (S-3) that records it. The chapter's law: decide, for every cue, whether the characters can hear it - and if you cross the line, cross it on purpose, because the audience will feel the border even when they can't name it.

Two tools, one inheritance and one hazard. The leitmotif: Wagner's technique - a musical idea attached to a character, object, place or concept, recurring and TRANSFORMING as the drama develops - imported into Hollywood by the émigré generation (Max Steiner's Tara, Erich Korngold's Robin Hood) and made the classical score's structural spine: the theme announces, the variation comments, the transformation tells you what has changed (the love theme in a minor key at the funeral; the villain's motif hidden inside the hero's at the moment of temptation). The craft of themes: a motif must be short enough to survive fragmentation and distinctive enough to survive orchestration - Williams' five-note contact signal, the Imperial March's dotted rhythm - and a score's theme-map (which idea belongs to what, how they combine) is designed at the spotting stage alongside the cues; the largest modern system, Howard Shore's for The Lord of the Rings, runs to dozens of interrelated motifs, a leitmotivic web documented at Wagnerian scale. Transformation as narrative: the theme's development IS the character's - tempo, mode, instrumentation, harmony changing with the story, so a melody heard in reel one carries reel one's memory into reel eleven; this is the score's unique power (S-2's added value across time), and the reason theme-less scores must find other unities (texture, a chord, a sound - S-10's electronic and minimalist traditions). The temp track: the music editor's assembly of existing music under the cut so it can be screened before the score exists (CH 1) - an essential tool, and the trade's most reliable trap: TEMP LOVE, the director's (and studio's) attachment to music heard a hundred times in the cutting room, so that the composer's new score is judged against it and pushed toward imitation; the case law - Kubrick keeping his 2001 temp and discarding North's finished score; Gladiator's Holst resemblances argued in public; a thousand unlitigated cues that sound like their temps - is the composer's cautionary syllabus. Handling it: composers who ask to temp with their OWN earlier work, who deliver sketches before the temp calcifies, who translate the temp's function rather than its notes ("it's here for momentum, not for cellos"), and directors educated (by this chapter) to hear the temp as a placeholder for a decision. The chapter's law, and the module's: a theme is a memory the audience will carry, so design it as one; a temp is a memory the director already carries, so replace it before it becomes the film.

S-10 · Year 2 · Module Ten

Music for Film II - Traditions & Techniques

The Trailer

In December 1957, Miles Davis walked into a Paris studio, watched a rough cut of Louis Malle's Ascenseur pour l'échafaud projected on a screen, and improvised. The band played to the picture in a single night - no themes written, no cues spotted in the Hollywood manner, the trumpet following Jeanne Moreau down the Champs-Élysées as if it were walking beside her. The recording became a landmark of both jazz and film music, and it proved a principle this module is built on: the classical Hollywood method is one tradition among many, and the world's cinemas have scored themselves in their own languages all along.

The largest of those languages is India's. For most of the twentieth century a Hindi or Tamil film's MUSIC DIRECTOR was as famous as its stars - Naushad, S.D. and R.D. Burman, Ilaiyaraaja, A.R. Rahman - and the singers whose voices the stars mimed to were more famous still: Lata Mangeshkar's recorded output is often cited in the tens of thousands, a figure the track flags as contested while noting that no other recording career on earth is even in the argument. Songs were composed and recorded FIRST, then picturized (D-9's and CL-15's chapters, seen from the composer's chair), and the raga a music director chose carried a rasa - an emotional flavour codified two millennia earlier - that the audience could feel before a word was sung. Add the synthesizer's arrival (a Greek keyboardist's Chariots of Fire beating the orchestras to an Oscar in 1981), the jazz and world-music scores from Ellington to Sakamoto, and the perpetual argument between the song and the score, and the module has its map. Music for film speaks every language cinema does. This is where the composer learns the other ones.

Four chapters: orchestral and electronic scoring - the two great engines and their hybrid; Indian film music - the music director, raga to rasa, playback singing and the pre-recorded song; jazz and world-music scoring - improvisation and the world's idioms on picture; and the song versus the score - the oldest argument in the business. Roll the playback.

Chapters

The orchestra: the classical score's engine, inherited from the late-Romantic concert hall via the émigrés (S-9) - its craft is ORCHESTRATION for picture (the ORCHESTRATOR as a distinct trade, turning a composer's sketch into full parts under deadline; the doubling and voicing that keep a theme audible under dialogue; the sections' emotional registers - strings for feeling, brass for power, woodwinds for character, percussion for time), the scoring stage's economics (players by the hour, a click track's tyranny and freedom, streamers and punches for hits), and the modern reality that most "orchestral" scores are first heard as sample-library MOCKUPS and often partly delivered that way (the hybrid below). The electronic engine: born in 1956 when Louis and Bebe Barron's "electronic tonalities" scored Forbidden Planet (the first all-electronic feature score, credited carefully to avoid the musicians' union), grown through the synthesizer decades - Carpenter's DIY minimalism (a director scoring his own films on cheap keyboards because it was faster and cheaper), Vangelis's Chariots and Blade Runner, Tangerine Dream's sequenced Americas - into the present's dominant mode: Reznor and Ross's textural scores, Jóhannsson's and Guðnadóttir's cello-and-electronics dread, Mica Levi's alien strings, scores built from PROCESSED SOUND where the line between S-6's design and music dissolves. The hybrid: the norm since the 1990s - orchestra recorded and layered with synthesis, sampled and processed, the Remote Control school's mass-produced power and its critics, the composer as producer with a template as much as a pencil; the craft question is which engine carries what (the orchestra for line and warmth, electronics for pulse, texture and the uncanny). The techniques both share: hit points and their avoidance (mickey-mousing - synchronising music to action beat by beat - as animation's joy and drama's cliché), tempo mapped to picture, harmonic language as period and genre code, and the theme-versus-texture decision S-9 raised: whether a score is remembered as a melody or a feeling. The chapter's law: choose the engine for the story's temperature - and know that the orchestra and the synthesizer are both, in the end, instruments played by someone who watched the picture.

The world's largest scoring tradition, taught as its own system. The music director: not a composer hired late (S-9's Hollywood timeline) but a central creative authority engaged early, composing the SONGS that will structure the film - the melodies first, the lyricist (Sahir Ludhianvi, Gulzar, Kannadasan, Vairamuthu) writing to the tune or the tune to the words, the arrangers and session orchestras (the Bombay and Madras studio cultures, with their own legendary players) realising them - and the background score (the BGM, S-11's subject) often composed by the same figure; the names are a canon: Naushad's classical grandeur, S.D. Burman's folk-rooted lyricism, R.D. Burman's cosmopolitan invention, Ilaiyaraaja's symphonic fusion of Carnatic idiom and Western counterpoint across a thousand films, A.R. Rahman's studio revolution from Roja onward. Raga to rasa: the classical system supplies the emotional grammar - a raga is a melodic framework with an associated time of day, season and mood, and the Natyashastra's RASA theory (the eight or nine aesthetic flavours - love, sorrow, heroism, wonder, terror, and the rest) gives the music director a map from melody to feeling: Bhairavi for devotion and dawn, Darbari for gravity, Yaman for the evening's longing - used, bent and blended into film's own hybrid idiom so that an audience raised in the tradition feels the flavour before the lyric lands; the composer's craft is knowing the map and knowing when to leave it. Playback singing: the star mimes; the SINGER records - a separate profession whose voices (Lata Mangeshkar's, Mohammed Rafi's, Kishore Kumar's, Asha Bhosle's, S.P. Balasubrahmanyam's, K.J. Yesudas's, and today's generations) became a nation's inner soundtrack; the craft is singing FOR a face - the singer studying the actor's manner, the lip-sync's demands, the emotional register of a scene they will never appear in; the tradition's early years included stars who sang themselves (K.L. Saigal) and classical masters recorded for period grandeur (Bade Ghulam Ali Khan in Mughal-e-Azam). The pre-recorded song and its picturization: the song is finished BEFORE shooting, and the choreographer, director and cinematographer build its picture (D-9 and CL-15 from the other chair) - so the music director composes knowing the number must carry dance, spectacle, location changes and narrative, and the playback craft on set (S-3) is the bridge. The modern turn: Rahman's digital studio, the sync-sound era's effect on song placement, the song's migration to the soundtrack album and streaming, and the regional industries' distinct musical cultures. The chapter's law: in this tradition the song is not decoration but ARCHITECTURE - and a composer who cannot write one cannot score the film.

S-11 · Year 2 · Module Eleven

Sound Aesthetics Across Cinema

The Trailer

In 1948, cutting Drunken Angel, Akira Kurosawa found that the scene where his dying gangster wanders a black-market street needed music - and chose "The Cuckoo Waltz," a cheerful tune he'd heard a loudspeaker playing on a real street, laid against the character's despair. He called the effect counterpoint: sound that refuses to agree with the image, so that both become more themselves. The story is Kurosawa's own, told in his autobiography, and it announces a tradition this module treats as a full aesthetic rather than a style: Japanese cinema's use of silence, interval and disagreement - the concept of ma, the meaningful gap - as the soundtrack's first material.

The world's other traditions stake out their own positions. In 1995 Lars von Trier and Thomas Vinterberg wrote a ten-point Vow of Chastity - reportedly in under an hour, a claim the track flags as the manifesto's own legend - whose second rule forbade any sound not produced with the image: no score, no post-synchronised anything, the diegetic-only cinema of Dogme 95. At the opposite pole, Hollywood's maximalist mixes fill every channel until directors are accused of drowning their own dialogue - Christopher Nolan defended Tenet's mix in public as an aesthetic choice. And the Indian mainstream built the densest soundtrack of all: continuous background score, thick reverb inherited from the dubbing era, songs as architecture, and a theatre culture that expects the interval to arrive at full volume. Every cinema has decided what silence is for. This module visits four of those decisions - and asks you to make your own.

Four chapters: silence and ma in Japanese sound design; wall-of-sound versus minimalist approaches; Dogme 95's diegetic-only rule - and what it proved; and Indian cinema's dense mix - the full soundtrack as a tradition. Listen to the gaps.

Chapters

Ma is the interval - the gap between things where meaning gathers - and Japanese aesthetics treats it as a material: the empty space in a room, the pause in Noh, the rest in gagaku, the moment in a tea ceremony when nothing is done. In cinema it became a sound philosophy. Ozu: the pillow shots (D-9's stillness from the ear's side) - a vase, a corridor, a chimney - held with only the world's quiet ambience; dialogue delivered at conversational pace with pauses left intact; scores (Saburo Ito's, Kojun Saito's) sparse and unemphatic, the dramatic cue refused so grief arrives without music announcing it; a soundtrack that never tells the audience when to feel. Takemitsu: the composer's version - the single sound weighted, silence composed as duration (his own description of writing for film was of subtracting music), Woman in the Dunes' sand-and-string textures and Ran's requiem (S-10) as scores that exist mostly in their gaps. Kurosawa's counterpoint: the trailer's principle - sound that disagrees with image (the cheerful waltz against despair; the wedding music through the wall of a death scene) - a Japanese answer to added value (S-2) where the value comes from contradiction rather than reinforcement. The modern inheritors: Kore-eda's domestic quiet, the anime sound directors' precise silences, and the tradition's export - Bresson's ascetic French cousin, the Iranian minimalists, every "quiet film" in the festival circuit. The craft translations: silence is not the absence of sound but a DESIGNED bed (the room tone S-3 recorded, the ambience S-6 built - silence in cinema is always something), the pause is a cue (spotted at the session with as much care as music), and contradiction is a tool (the source that refuses the scene's mood). The chapter's law, from Bresson by way of Ozu: when the sound film learned to be silent, it learned its greatest power - and the mixer who fears the gap has not yet learned the tradition.

The theatre and the audience: a culture of single-screen cinemas with large, vocal crowds, the interval as structure, the "mass" sequence designed for cheers - a soundtrack made to be heard over an audience, and to give it something to answer; the mix's density is a CONTRACT with a room, as Hollywood's reference level is a contract with silence. The sync-sound turn and the hybrid: Lagaan's production sound (S-3), Pookutty's advocacy (S-6), and a generation of films that recorded the world honestly and then mixed it with the tradition's BGM - Gangs of Wasseypur's Wasseypur recorded live and scored with folk-and-hip-hop songs; the regional cinemas' distinct positions - Tamil's BGM culture (Ilaiyaraaja's continuous scores; today's composers whose background themes become chart hits), Malayalam's naturalism (the sync-sound, score-light realism of Kumbalangi Nights and its generation), Telugu's spectacle (Baahubali's mix as an event the theatre can barely contain). The craft judgments: designing a BGM that carries a film without smothering it (motif discipline, dynamic shape - CH 2's law inside the tradition), dialogue intelligibility under continuous score (S-8's hierarchy renegotiated for a culture that expects both), the song's placement as the mix's peak, and the loudness debates the tradition has begun to have with itself. The module's close and the worldwide mandate's sound clause: every tradition in this module is a decision about what the audience is for - silence for attention, the wall for immersion, the vow for honesty, the dense mix for participation - and your own films will be heard by rooms that expect one of them. Know all four; choose on purpose.

The Sound Department & Industry

The Trailer

Watch the end credits of any modern feature and count the sound department: a supervising sound editor, a sound designer, dialogue and ADR editors, effects editors, a Foley team, a music editor, the re-recording mixers, their assistants, the production mixer and boom and utility from a year earlier - thirty names for a mid-sized film, more than the camera department, and almost none of them known outside the building. In 2020 the Academy merged its two sound Oscars - Editing and Mixing - into one, prompting a public argument about whether the world could tell the crafts apart; the trade's own societies, the Motion Picture Sound Editors and the Cinema Audio Society, keep their separate Golden Reels and CAS Awards precisely because they can.

The department in two halves, joined at the turnover.
