DOC-1 · Year 1 · Module One

History & Traditions

The Trailer

In 1922 Robert Flaherty released a film about an Inuk hunter named Allakariallak, whom he renamed Nanook, whom he asked to hunt a walrus with a harpoon his community no longer used, and whose igloo was built oversized and cut in half so a camera could see inside it. Nanook of the North is the founding feature of documentary, and every one of those facts is a problem the form has been arguing about for a century. The word itself came four years later, when John Grierson reviewed Flaherty's Moana and called it "documentary" - then spent the rest of his life defining the term as "the creative treatment of actuality," a phrase that quietly concedes the problem is permanent.

This module is where non-fiction came from, told as a set of traditions still in use rather than as a museum. Flaherty's romantic reconstruction and Grierson's civic movement, which built the British documentary tradition on public money and a social mission. The city symphony and Dziga Vertov's kino-pravda - the camera as a machine for seeing what the eye cannot, the film as an argument about seeing. And the wartime and propaganda documentary, where the form's persuasive power was weaponised by every side at once and where its ethics were tested most severely. Documentary has never been the record of reality. It has always been a treatment of it - and the treatment is the history.

Three chapters: Flaherty, Grierson and the British movement; the city symphony and Vertov's kino-pravda; and propaganda and the wartime documentary. Tungsten loaded. The walrus is a reconstruction.

Chapters

Two founders who disagreed about what the form was for. Flaherty: the explorer-filmmaker - a year in the Arctic with the Inuit, the first footage lost to a cigarette fire (a story Flaherty told; the track files it as his own account), the return and the film built through RECONSTRUCTION (the hunt restaged, the traditional methods revived for the camera, the half-igloo, the family assembled from people who were not a family), the romantic vision (the noble subject against nature; the disappearing way of life preserved), and the method's descendants - Moana in Samoa (the tattooing ritual revived because the film needed a climax), Man of Aran (the shark hunt the islanders had to relearn), Louisiana Story (funded by Standard Oil); the critique (the colonial gaze, the participation of subjects in a fiction of their own past, the ethnographic problem DOC-10 CH 2 pursues) and the defence (the subjects' consent and collaboration were real; the films are the record of a relationship, not a lie about a people); the lasting legacy - the documentary as a CRAFTED story with a protagonist, a structure and a climax, which is the mainstream feature documentary's form to this day (DOC-6). Grierson: the Scottish social scientist who coined the word, borrowed Flaherty's poetry and rejected his romance - the documentary as PUBLIC EDUCATION, the "creative treatment of actuality" in the service of citizenship, the state as patron (the Empire Marketing Board, then the GPO Film Unit, then the National Film Board of Canada he founded in 1939); the BRITISH MOVEMENT he ran: Drifters (his own film about herring fishing), Basil Wright's Song of Ceylon, Harry Watt and Wright's Night Mail (with Auden's verse and Britten's score - the documentary as poem and public-information film at once), Edgar Anstey's Housing Problems (the first film to let working-class subjects speak directly to camera about their own lives - a democratising gesture with its own paternalism), Humphrey Jennings's wartime work (CH 3); the ideology (the film as a tool of social reform and democratic persuasion, made by an educated class about a working class it observed), the aesthetic (the poetic montage, the heroic worker, the voice-over's authority - DOC-2's expository mode was born here), and the critique (whose voice, whose gaze, whose money - the questions DOC-7 formalises). The two legacies: Flaherty's narrative documentary and Grierson's civic one are the twin roots of the mainstream form, and most of what follows in this track - the observational revolt (DOC-3), the essay film (DOC-8), the militant cinema (DOC-10) - is a reaction against one or both. The chapter's law: know that the founding films were staged, sponsored and shaped, that their makers knew it and argued about it, and that "creative treatment of actuality" has been the form's honest description from the start.

The city as subject and the camera as an argument about seeing. The city symphony: the 1920s form that took a metropolis as protagonist and a day as structure - Paul Strand and Charles Sheeler's Manhatta (1921), Alberto Cavalcanti's Rien que les heures (1926, Paris), Walter Ruttmann's Berlin: Symphony of a Great City (1927 - the dawn-to-night structure, the machine rhythms, the montage of classes and speeds), Joris Ivens's Rain (1929, Amsterdam - the poetic documentary as pure form), and the mode's descendants (Koyaanisqatsi, the drone symphony); the formal principles - RHYTHM as structure (the edit set to tempo before sense - E-1's Soviet theory applied to actuality), the DAY as arc, the ANONYMOUS crowd rather than the individual subject, the machine and the body compared, and the absence of voice-over (the form is pre-sound and mostly stayed silent by choice); the critique (Ruttmann's film as "aestheticised" - the political content dissolved into pattern) and the reason it matters: the city symphony is the POETIC mode's origin (DOC-2), and every non-narrative, sensory, montage-led documentary since - the Baraka lineage, the sensory ethnography of Leviathan (DOC-10) - descends from it. Vertov: Denis Kaufman, renamed Dziga Vertov ("spinning top"), the Soviet newsreel-maker turned theorist - the KINO-PRAVDA newsreel series (1922–25: "film-truth," the name cinéma vérité would translate forty years later, DOC-3), the KINO-EYE (the camera as an organ superior to the human eye - it can slow, speed, reverse, magnify, be anywhere; the "cinema of facts" against the "cinema of theatre"; the war declared on fiction film and on the scripted, acted, staged), the method (the camera catching "life unawares" - the hidden camera, the long lens, the subject unaware; and the montage that reassembles the fragments into a Communist argument about a new society), and MAN WITH A MOVIE CAMERA (1929): a day in a Soviet city, a cameraman filming it, an editor cutting it, an audience watching it - the film that shows its own making, whose every trick (freeze frames, split screens, superimpositions, the camera on a tripod walking by itself) is a demonstration of the kino-eye's powers, and which is the REFLEXIVE mode's founding text (DOC-2, DOC-8 CH 3) fifty years before the word was used. Vertov's fall: the Stalinist turn, the denunciation of "formalism," the decades of newsreel obscurity, the rediscovery by the 1960s French left (Godard's Dziga Vertov Group) and by Rouch and Morin, who took his name for their method. The two poles: the city symphony's aestheticism and Vertov's militancy are the poetic and reflexive modes in embryo, and both are alternatives to the Flaherty–Grierson narrative-civic mainstream - the form's radical wing, established in the same decade as its establishment. The chapter's law: the camera does not record what the eye sees; it sees differently, and the honest documentary maker knows what their machine adds - Vertov made a whole film to say so.

The form's persuasive power, weaponised by every side. The problem stated: documentary's claim on the real makes it the most effective propaganda medium ever built, and the 1930s and '40s proved it in every direction at once; the module's discipline is to study the technique without flinching from the content, because the techniques are still in use. Riefenstahl: Triumph of the Will (1935) - the Nuremberg rally staged partly FOR the film (the architecture, the choreography, the camera positions built into the event), the aesthetic (the low angles, the mass geometry, the leader descending from the clouds), the mythic structure, and Olympia (1938) - the athletic body as ideology, the innovations (the underwater and tracking shots) that sports coverage still uses; the argument about her (the "I was only an artist" defence, the evidence against it, and the question the track will not let go of: does technical brilliance in the service of atrocity teach anything a decent filmmaker should learn? - the answer this track gives is yes, and that learning it is not the same as admiring it). The Soviet and the Allied answer: the Soviet wartime newsreel; Frank Capra's WHY WE FIGHT series (1942–45) - the US Army's orientation films, built by a Hollywood director from the enemy's own footage recut against them, animated maps from Disney, a narration of moral certainty (the EXPOSITORY mode at its most powerful, DOC-2 - the "voice of God" narration's home); John Huston's The Battle of San Pietro (1945 - combat footage partly reconstructed; the Army suppressed his Let There Be Light, on shell-shocked veterans, for thirty-five years) and Ford's The Battle of Midway; the British - Humphrey Jennings's Listen to Britain (1942, sound montage of a nation at war, no narration - the poetic mode as propaganda) and Fires Were Started (firemen re-enacting their own Blitz, the Flaherty method for the war effort), and the Crown Film Unit's Target for Tonight; the Nazi answer to the answer (Der Ewige Jude's monstrous "documentary" antisemitism - studied, if at all, only through the scholarship on it, never as a source). The techniques: the ENEMY'S FOOTAGE recut (the found-footage argument - DOC-8's compilation film has this origin), the NARRATOR as authority, the MUSIC as instruction, the STAGED event presented as caught, the MAP as proof, and the appeal to the collective - every one of which appears in the modern advocacy documentary and the political campaign film, and every one of which DOC-7's ethics must reckon with. After the war: Night and Fog (1956 - Resnais's thirty-two minutes on the camps, the archive against the present-day tracking shot, the narration's refusal of consolation; the film against which the atrocity documentary is still measured), the Cold War's documentary fronts, and the newsreel's decline into television. The chapter's law: learn the propagandist's techniques so that you recognise them in your own cutting room - and hold to the line the form has drawn since 1945, that the record of atrocity is owed a treatment that does not console.

DOC-2 · Year 1 · Module Two

The Modes - Nichols's Taxonomy

The Trailer

Bill Nichols proposed six modes of documentary - poetic, expository, observational, participatory, reflexive, performative - and the taxonomy has become the form's most-used teaching framework, its most-argued-with, and, its author has repeatedly insisted, its most misunderstood. The modes are not genres, not periods and not a ranking. They are answers to a question every non-fiction film must answer somehow: what is the relationship between the filmmaker, the subject and the audience, and how does the film make that relationship visible?

This module is the taxonomy taught properly - each mode as a set of conventions, an implicit contract with the viewer, a history, a characteristic ethical problem and a set of exemplary films - followed by the chapter that matters most in practice: how real films mix them. Almost no documentary is one mode. A film has a DOMINANT mode that governs its contract, and it borrows freely from the others for particular sequences, and the maker's job is to know which contract they have made with the audience and not to break it by accident. The modes are not boxes. They are the six ways a film can be honest about what it is doing.

Two chapters: the six modes - poetic, expository, observational, participatory, reflexive, performative - each with its contract; and how films mix modes - the dominant mode and the borrowed sequence. Name what you are doing.

Chapters

Six contracts, each with a history, a grammar and a characteristic sin. Poetic: the film that organises reality by rhythm, association, tone and pattern rather than by argument or story - the city symphony (DOC-1 CH 2), Ivens's Rain, the Koyaanisqatsi lineage, Sans Soleil's associative drift (DOC-8), sensory ethnography; the CONTRACT: the viewer is offered an experience and an implied view, not a case; the SIN: the subject dissolved into aesthetics - the human being who becomes a shape. Expository: the film that addresses the viewer directly with an argument - the "voice of God" narration (Grierson, Why We Fight, the television documentary, Ken Burns), the images as EVIDENCE for the commentary (the "illustrative" cut - the picture serves the word), the interview as testimony marshalled, the archive as proof; the CONTRACT: we will tell you what this means; the SIN: the false authority - the narration that asserts what the footage does not show, and the viewer's inability to check. Observational: the film that watches and does not intervene - direct cinema (DOC-3), the long take, no narration, no interviews, no music, the crew as invisible as it can make itself, the sync-sound revolution's child; the CONTRACT: you are seeing what happened, as it happened, and the film's meaning is in what you notice; the SIN: the pretence of absence - the camera changes the room and the edit makes an argument, and the mode's purest claim (Wiseman's "reality fiction" is his own honest term) is never literally true. Participatory: the film in which the maker is visibly present and engaged - the interview as encounter rather than testimony, the filmmaker's question in the film, the provocation (Rouch's "cinema of provocation"), Broomfield and Moore in the frame, Kopple's years with her subjects, Herzog's voice; the CONTRACT: this is what happened when I, the filmmaker, met these people; the SIN: the film that becomes about the filmmaker while claiming to be about the subject - and its cousin, the staged encounter presented as spontaneous. Reflexive: the film that shows its own making and questions its own claims - Man with a Movie Camera, Chronicle of a Summer's final debate (DOC-3 CH 2), Ross McElwee's asides, The Act of Killing's method exposed, Stories We Tell's reveal (DOC-8 CH 3); the CONTRACT: this is a film, and here is how films make truth; the SIN: reflexivity as alibi - the disclosure that excuses rather than examines. Performative: the film that foregrounds the maker's subjective, embodied, emotional experience as its route to a truth that objective address cannot reach - Marlon Riggs's Tongues Untied, Waltz with Bashir's animated memory, the first-person testimony of trauma, much of the queer and diasporic documentary of the 1980s and '90s; the CONTRACT: this is true because it is how it was lived, and my body and voice are the evidence; the SIN: the confessional that mistakes intensity for insight. The order: Nichols's sequence is roughly historical - each mode emerging partly as a critique of the last's limitations (expository's authority answered by observational's restraint, answered by participatory's honesty about presence, answered by reflexive's honesty about form) - but every mode is alive and in use, and the modern feature documentary picks from all six. The chapter's law: for any film you make or watch, name the contract it has made with its viewer - and name the sin that contract is most tempted by.

Almost no film is one mode; every film has one contract. The dominant mode: the address that governs the film's relationship with its viewer - established in the first minutes (a narrator speaks; or nobody does; or the filmmaker is on screen asking a question) and understood by the audience as the film's promise; a film can borrow any mode for a sequence, but its dominant mode is the one whose sin the audience will hold it to. The common mixtures: the observational feature with an expository frame (the title cards that tell you where you are - Wiseman uses none; most of his descendants use some), the expository film with observational sequences (the Ken Burns episode that lets a scene breathe; the television documentary's "actuality" between the interviews), the participatory film with a poetic sequence (Herzog's landscapes between his encounters), the performative first-person film with archive evidence (Waltz with Bashir's final minute; the personal essay that ends on the document - DOC-8), the observational film with a reflexive moment (the subject who looks at the camera and speaks; the crew that becomes visible because it must). The interview's place: the interview is a TECHNIQUE, not a mode - expository films use it as testimony, participatory ones as encounter, reflexive ones as a thing to examine; the same footage of a person answering a question can serve three contracts depending on how the film frames the asking (DOC-5 CH 2's craft). Breaking the contract: the failures - the observational film that suddenly narrates (the audience feels the maker's hand where it was promised absence), the expository film whose narrator is caught asserting what the footage contradicts, the participatory film that hides an intervention it made (the staged encounter is not a participatory choice, it is a lie inside one), the performative film that borrows the expository mode's authority to make its subjective claims look like facts; and the DELIBERATE break - the Act of Killing's shift from participatory to reflexive as the film's own ethics become its subject (DOC-7 CH 3), Stories We Tell's late reveal that reframes everything before it (DOC-8 CH 3), Chronicle of a Summer's final reel in which the subjects watch the film and argue with it. The maker's discipline: decide the dominant mode in development (DOC-4 - the treatment states it), shoot for it (DOC-5 - the observational film needs the long take; the expository one needs the b-roll the narration will need), edit to it (DOC-6 - the mode determines the structure), and when you borrow, know you are borrowing. Reading a film for its modes: the practical exercise of marking a documentary sequence by sequence - dominant, borrowed, borrowed, dominant - which reveals the maker's decisions faster than any interview. The chapter's law and the module's: name your dominant mode before you shoot a frame, borrow the others knowingly for what they do best, and never break the contract by accident - because the audience will feel the break before they can name it.

DOC-3 · Year 1 · Module Three

Direct Cinema vs. Cinéma Vérité

The Trailer

In 1960 two things happened that made modern documentary possible, and they happened on opposite sides of the Atlantic with opposite conclusions. In the United States, Robert Drew's team - Richard Leacock, D. A. Pennebaker, Albert Maysles - took a newly portable camera and a newly synchronised tape recorder into the Wisconsin primary and made Primary, a film that followed Kennedy and Humphrey through hotel corridors and rallies without narration, without interviews, without the crew ever seeming to be there. In Paris, Jean Rouch and Edgar Morin took the same technology onto the streets and asked strangers "Are you happy?", put themselves in the film, provoked their subjects into confrontations, and ended Chronicle of a Summer by screening the footage to the people in it and filming their argument about whether any of it was true.

Direct cinema and cinéma vérité are routinely confused, and the confusion is instructive: they share a technology and an enemy (the staged, narrated, scripted documentary of the previous era) and disagree completely about what the camera does to a room. The Americans believed that if the crew were quiet enough, reality would forget them. The French believed the camera was a catalyst and the honest thing was to admit it. This module is that argument - and the machines that made it possible. The fly on the wall, and the fly in the soup.

Three chapters: observational fly-on-the-wall - Wiseman, Pennebaker, the Maysles; participatory and interactive - Rouch, Morin and Chronicle of a Summer; and the portable sync-sound history that made both possible. Roll sound.

Both schools were born the year the equipment let them be. DOC-4 · Year 1 · Module Four

Research & Development

The Trailer

Steve James and his collaborators set out in 1987 to make a half-hour film about playground basketball in Chicago. They followed two fourteen-year-olds into high school, kept following them through five years, shot two hundred and fifty hours, and released Hoop Dreams as a three-hour feature in 1994. Nothing about that was in the proposal. Documentary development is the discipline of writing a plan for a film whose subject will not follow it - a treatment for events that have not happened, a budget for a shooting ratio you cannot predict, a proposal to funders for a story you are promising to find rather than tell.

This module is that discipline. Finding the story: the difference between a subject and a story, the character who carries a film, the access without which there is no film at all, and the months of research that precede the first day of shooting. And the paper: the treatment that describes a film not yet made, the funding landscape - grants, broadcasters, foundations, the pitching forums (DOC-11), the private equity of the streaming era - and the proposal, which is the document by which a documentary maker persuades a stranger to pay for a year of their attention. You cannot write a documentary. You can only write the conditions under which one might be found.

Two chapters: finding the story and getting access - subject, character, the door that opens; and the treatment, funding and the proposal - the paper that buys the year. Find the person first.

Chapters

A subject is not a story, and a story is not a film until someone lets you in. Subject versus story: the SUBJECT is the topic (the coal strike, the dementia ward, the migration route); the STORY is what happens to someone inside it - a person with a want, an obstacle and a possible change (the Screenwriting track's engine, found rather than written); the documentary maker's first discipline is to refuse the subject until the story has a CHARACTER, because the audience follows people, not issues (Kopple did not make a film about labour; she made one about Harlan County's strikers, and the labour film followed). The character: who carries the film - the criteria (someone with a stake in what happens next; someone in a situation that will change during the shoot; someone who is interesting on camera - articulate or not, but present; someone who wants to be filmed, or can come to; someone whose story does not require the film to harm them - DOC-7), the RANGE (the single protagonist, the ensemble, the institution as protagonist - Wiseman's answer, DOC-3 CH 1), and the honest arithmetic that the person you start with is often not the person the film ends up about (Hoop Dreams' third boy; the subject who drops out; the minor figure who becomes the film). The research: the READING (everything published about the subject; the maker as the world's temporary expert), the PRE-INTERVIEWS (off-camera conversations that find the characters and the story - recorded with permission or noted, never wasted on tape you will want to have shot properly), the ARCHIVE search (DOC-5 CH 3 - what exists, who owns it, what it costs), the SITE visits, the TIMELINE (what is scheduled to happen - the election, the trial, the season, the closure - the crisis structure's deadline, DOC-3 CH 1), and the WORKING HYPOTHESIS (Rabiger's term: a one-sentence statement of what the film is about and what you expect to find, written to be proved wrong). Access: the single most important asset in documentary and the one no craft substitutes for - the INSTITUTION's permission (the hospital, the school, the prison, the company: legal, layered, revocable), the INDIVIDUAL's trust (built over months, by presence without a camera, by honesty about what the film is and who will see it), the GATEKEEPER (the union rep, the family elder, the fixer - DOC-10 - whose blessing opens doors and whose alienation closes them), the ACCESS AGREEMENT (what you may film, when, what they may see, what they may veto - the negotiated terms that DOC-7 CH 2 examines ethically; write them down), the EXCLUSIVITY question (does another crew have the same door?), and the maintenance of access (it is granted daily, not once; the subject who stops answering the phone has withdrawn it, and the film is over unless the relationship is repaired). The story that isn't there: the development decision to stop - when the character will not carry it, the access will not open, or the story has already happened; the proposal that was never written is the cheapest failed film. The chapter's law: find the person, not the topic; research until you have a hypothesis worth disproving; and get access in writing, then keep earning it every day - because everything else in this track presumes a door that is open.

DOC-5 · Year 1 · Module Five

Documentary Production

The Trailer

Errol Morris built a machine so that his subjects would look him in the eye. The Interrotron puts the interviewer's face on a teleprompter mirror in front of the lens, so the subject looks directly at Morris and therefore directly at the audience - a device that turns the interview from a conversation overheard into a confession addressed, and which Morris has used on a Holocaust denier, a former Secretary of Defense and a pet cemetery's mourners with equal effect. The interview is documentary's most-used and least-examined technique, and the machine is a reminder that every choice in it - the eyeline, the framing, the question, the silence - is an authorial decision the audience feels without seeing.

This module is the shoot. Filming real events with a small crew - the two-person team, the one-person rig, the days that cannot be re-shot, the ratio that cannot be predicted. The interview and vérité shooting as crafts with their own grammars, from the question that gets an answer to the operator's instinct for where the moment will be. And archive and re-enactment: the found footage that must be searched, cleared and paid for, and the staged sequence that the form has used since Nanook and still argues about. Year 1 closes with a camera rolling on something that only happens once. Fiction shoots what the script says. Documentary shoots what is there - and the craft is being ready when it happens.

Documentary Editing

The Trailer

Year 2 opens on daylight stock in a cutting room with two hundred hours of footage and no script. Frederick Wiseman spends a year on each film at a flatbed - now an Avid - watching everything he shot, twice, before he cuts a frame, and describes the process as the moment the film is actually written. Every documentary editor says a version of the same thing: the shoot gathers material; the edit finds the film. In fiction the editor serves a script. In documentary the editor is, with the director, the film's co-author, and the credit "a film by" on a non-fiction feature is at least half theirs.

This module is that authorship. Finding structure in footage - the logging and the paper edit, the assembly that is four hours long, the search for the spine, the act structure that reality did not provide and the film must. Building character and arc from what people actually did - the protagonist made legible, the change found or shaped, and the line between shaping and lying. And the ethics of the edit - the juxtaposition that implies, the reaction moved, the answer cut to a different question, the silence that condemns. The editor has more power over a documentary's truth than anyone who was on the shoot, and this module is about using it with both craft and conscience. The camera recorded what happened. The edit decides what it meant.

Inside is CH 3's evidence. You gain the profession's own account of its editing conscience. Read Honest Truths in full before CH 3.

Chapters

Two hundred hours in; ninety minutes out; the film somewhere between. Logging: the discipline begun on the shoot (DOC-5 CH 1) and completed in the cutting room - every clip watched and described (the content, the quality, the emotional temperature, the usable line, the moment), the TRANSCRIPTS of every interview (time-coded; the film's most-consulted document), the KEYWORDS and bins (by character, by theme, by event, by location - the system chosen for how the film will be found, and kept ruthlessly), the SELECTS (the best of each scene pulled into its own sequence - the first reduction, from two hundred hours to perhaps forty), and the WATCHING (Wiseman's twice-through; the editor who knows the material better than the director who shot it - the co-authorship begins here). The paper edit: the film built on cards, on a wall, in a document, before it is built in the timeline - the interview transcripts cut into statements and arranged, the observational scenes summarised on cards and ordered, the STRUCTURE tried in an afternoon that would take a week in the timeline; the paper edit as the documentary's script, written after the shoot, and revised weekly. The assembly: the first long cut - everything that might belong, in a plausible order, at three or four hours - watched once through without stopping (the most important screening in the process: what is the film about, and who is it about?), and the honest assessment that follows (the character who isn't carrying it; the theme that isn't there; the material that is thin exactly where the story needs it - the note to shoot more, DOC-4's contingency spent). The spine: the structural principle that organises the film - the options: the CHRONOLOGY (the crisis structure's default, DOC-3 CH 1; the shoot's own timeline), the CHARACTER'S ARC (CH 2 - the protagonist's change as the through-line), the ARGUMENT (the expository film's case, built in steps - DOC-2), the MOSAIC (Wiseman's institution - scenes arranged for cumulative meaning, not causation; the city symphony's day), the QUESTION (the investigative film's inquiry - The Thin Blue Line; the true-crime series - unfolded as the audience's discovery), the ESSAY's associative drift (DOC-8), the PARALLEL lines (two or more characters intercut - Hoop Dreams' two boys; the multi-strand feature), and the FRAME (the present-day device - the screening to the subjects, the reunion, the return to the site - that contains the past); and the act structure imposed on reality (the Screenwriting track's three acts as a diagnostic, not a template: the setup that establishes the world and the want, the complication, the crisis and the resolution - many documentaries have the first two and must find or manufacture the third, which is CH 3's problem). The opening: the first three minutes that make the contract (DOC-2 - the mode declared, the character met, the question posed, the world established), rebuilt more times than any other sequence. The cuts: the assembly to the rough cut (two hours) to the fine cut (the length) to picture lock, with SCREENINGS to outsiders at each stage (the only reliable test of whether the film is understood - the notes taken, the defensiveness suppressed), and the calendar (the documentary edit runs six months to a year; the funders' schedule says three; the truth is in the middle and the fight is P-8's). The chapter's law: watch everything, log everything, build it on paper before the timeline, assemble long and screen it cold, find the spine that the material actually supports - and rebuild the opening until the contract is clear.

A person who did things, made into a character who wanted something. The character in the cutting room: the subject of DOC-4 CH 1 becomes a character only in the edit - the editor finds the WANT (what this person was pursuing across the footage, which they may never have stated), the OBSTACLE (what stood in the way - visible in the scenes, or implied), the STAKES (what it cost them; made legible by selection), the CONTRADICTION (the trait that complicates - the generous man's cruelty, the victim's agency - which the edit must keep, because a character without contradiction is a poster), and the INTRODUCTION (the first scene that shows who this is - chosen from everything, and usually not the first thing shot). The arc: the CHANGE - did this person end differently from how they began, and can the footage show it? - and the honest cases: the ARC THAT HAPPENED (the crisis structure's gift: the election lost, the trial ended, the child grown - the change is in the material and the edit reveals its shape), the ARC THAT IS PARTIAL (the change begun but not completed when shooting stopped - the edit ends on the question, or the frame device (CH 1) supplies a later moment), the ARC THAT IS INTERNAL (the change visible only in what the person says at the end versus the beginning - the interview's placement does the work), and the ARC THAT ISN'T THERE (the person who did not change - the edit that pretends otherwise is CH 3's lie; the honest options are the mosaic (the film is not about change), the reframing (the change is the audience's, not the subject's), or the admission that this is a portrait and not a story). The tools: SELECTION (what is shown of a person is who they are to the audience - the editor's first and largest power), ORDER (the scene placed before or after another changes what it means - the kind act shown after the cruel one is redemption; before it, hypocrisy), the INTERVIEW as spine (the subject's own reflection placed to interpret the scenes - the participatory film's structure; and the risk that the interview explains what the scene should have shown), the REACTION SHOT (E-6 - the listener's face that tells the audience what to feel about the speaker; in documentary, often shot at a different time, and CH 3's central problem), MUSIC (E-9 - the score that tells the audience who to love; the documentary's most abused tool and DOC-7's), the NARRATION (written last, when the film knows what it needs said - the expository spine, or the personal voice (DOC-8), or none), and the ENSEMBLE (the multi-character film's intercutting - E-8's parallel action; the balance of screen time as a statement of importance). The line: shaping is the job - the audience needs a character and an arc to follow, and reality does not arrive with either; the line is crossed when the shaping asserts what did not happen (the change invented, the want imposed, the contradiction cut so the character is simpler than the person) - and the working test is whether the subject, watching the film, would recognise themselves as the person they are, even if they dislike the portrait (DOC-7 CH 2). The chapter's law: find the want and the obstacle in what the person actually did, keep the contradiction, show the change the footage supports and admit the one it doesn't - and remember that selection and order are the two most powerful lies available to a truthful editor.

The manipulations the cutting room makes available, and the lines the profession draws. The inventory: the JUXTAPOSITION that implies (the politician's statement cut to the riot; the Kuleshov effect - E-1 - as accusation; the audience infers a causation the film never asserts and could not prove), the REACTION SHOT moved (the listener's disbelief shot in a different interview, or a different hour, placed after a statement it was not a reaction to - the television default and the practice most makers admit to), the ANSWER TO A DIFFERENT QUESTION (the interview statement placed after a question it did not answer - the mechanical lie the transcript exposes), the CONTEXT removed (the qualification cut; the "but" that followed; the joke made earnest), the CHRONOLOGY rearranged (the event moved to where the story needs it - legitimate as structure, a lie when it changes causation), the MUSIC that instructs (E-9 - the minor key under the subject the film has decided against), the SILENCE that condemns (the pause held after a statement until the subject looks guilty; the cutaway to the empty chair), the NARRATION that asserts beyond the footage (DOC-2's expository sin - "he never returned," when the film does not know), the COMPOSITE (two scenes from different days presented as one - DOC-7's consent and DOC-5's re-enactment rules apply), the TRANSLATION shaded (DOC-10 - the subtitle that softens or sharpens), and the ENDING chosen (the film stopped where the story looked resolved rather than where it was). Honest Truths: the 2009 study in which documentary makers described their own practices - the composites, the moved reactions, the staged moments - and the lines they said they would not cross; the profession's own ethic as revealed: makers believe they owe truth to the audience and care to the subject, and negotiate between them daily without any code. The lines the track draws: the edit may SELECT, ORDER, COMPRESS and JUXTAPOSE - that is the job, and the audience knows it is watching a construction; the edit may not ASSERT what did not happen (the invented causation, the answer to a different question, the reaction to a statement never heard, the narration beyond the evidence), may not CHANGE what a person meant (the context cut that reverses a statement's sense), and may not present the STAGED as caught (DOC-5 CH 3); the tests - the TRANSCRIPT test (would the subject, shown the transcript beside the cut, agree their meaning survived?), the AUDIENCE test (if the audience knew how this sequence was made, would they feel deceived or informed?), and the CORRECTION test (if a fact-checker found the sequence's implication false, could you defend it as an implication you meant?). The pressures: the funder who wants a villain, the platform whose format wants a cliffhanger every eight minutes (DOC-9 CH 1), the schedule that has no time for the fair version, the maker's own investment in the story they set out to tell - and the editor's position as the person in the room whose job is to say the footage doesn't support it. The cases: the true-crime series whose editing decided guilt; the political documentary whose juxtapositions were litigated; the broadcaster's codes written after scandals; and the films that were fair to people they clearly disliked, which is the standard. The chapter's law and the module's: select, order and shape as much as the story needs - and never assert, by cut, music, silence or narration, what the footage does not show, because the audience's trust in the whole form depends on the frames they cannot check.

DOC-7 · Year 2 · Module Seven

Values & Ethics

The Trailer

Claude Lanzmann told the former SS men he interviewed for Shoah that he was a historian, paid them, and filmed them with a hidden camera through a van's window - then published nine and a half hours of testimony that no honest approach could have obtained, and that the world is measurably better for having. Barbara Kopple stayed in Harlan County for a year and was fired upon beside the strikers she filmed. The Maysles brothers gave the Beales of Grey Gardens a film they loved and a public that did not always love them back. Every one of these is a defensible ethical position, and no code covers all three.

Documentary has no Hippocratic oath. The ethics are negotiated per film between three obligations that pull in different directions - the truth owed to the audience, the care owed to the subject, and the maker's own vision and livelihood - and this module is the negotiation made explicit. Truthfulness, consent and representation: what the audience is promised, what the subject agreed to, and who gets to depict whom. Responsibility to subjects: the release form and the relationship it does not cover, the harm the film can do, the money question, the subject who changes their mind. And the filmmaker's presence and intervention: when the camera should stop, when the maker should step in, and the case the whole form has argued about since a rooftop in Medan. There are no rules. There are only the questions, asked before it is too late to answer them.

Three chapters: truthfulness, consent and representation; responsibility to subjects; and the filmmaker's presence and intervention. Ask before you roll.

Chapters

Three promises, made to three different parties. Truthfulness to the audience: the contract (DOC-2) is that this is a treatment of actuality and not an invention - the audience accepts selection, order and shaping (DOC-6 CH 3) and does not accept the asserted falsehood; the specific obligations: FACTUAL ACCURACY (the dates, the names, the archive's provenance - DOC-5 CH 3 - checked; the documentary fact-checker as a real position on serious films), the STAGED declared (DOC-5 CH 3), the COMPOSITE and the COMPRESSION disclosed where they change meaning, the NARRATION within the evidence, and the distinction the track holds to - between the maker's honest interpretation (permitted, and unavoidable) and the misrepresentation of what happened (not); the ARGUMENT documentary (the advocacy film, the political film - Moore, the issue campaign) is truthful when its facts are right and its case is openly a case, and dishonest when it dresses selection as neutrality; and the HYBRID (DOC-9 CH 3) is honest when the audience knows the game. Consent: the RELEASE (the appearance release form - what it legally grants: the right to use the person's image and words in the film and its exploitation, in perpetuity, in all media; what it does not: any obligation to be fair, kind or accurate; and its limits: minors, the incapacitated, the coerced, the drunk), INFORMED consent (the ethical standard above the legal one - the subject understanding what a film is, who will see it, how they may be portrayed, that the maker controls the edit, and that release is forever; the honest explanation given before, in the subject's language, without the pressure of the rolling camera), the subject who CANNOT consent (the crowd, the passer-by, the child, the person in crisis - the journalistic and public-interest exceptions, the blurred face, the filmed-in-public doctrine that varies by country, and the ethical question the law does not answer: should this person be in the film at all?), CONSENT WITHDRAWN (the subject who changes their mind after signing - legally bound, ethically a real claim; the maker's options from re-negotiation to removal, and the track's position that a signature obtained years ago does not settle a request made in tears), and the ONGOING consent of the long-term subject (the access renewed daily - DOC-4 - as a form of consent renewed daily). Representation: WHO depicts WHOM - the history (DOC-1's Flaherty, DOC-10 CH 2's ethnographic gaze - the educated maker and the observed poor, foreign or vulnerable subject; the "parachute" documentary; the poverty film that wins prizes for a maker whose subjects gain nothing), the CRITIQUES (the community's right to tell its own story; the extractive film; the maker's proximity as a qualification or a limitation), the RESPONSES (the collaborative and participatory models - DOC-10 CH 1 - where subjects co-author; the maker from within the community; the consultant and the community screening; the pay, credit and profit-share arrangements some films now make), the STEREOTYPE and the SINGLE STORY (the film that confirms what its audience expected to see about a place or a people), and the TRACK's position: proximity is not a requirement and distance is not a disqualification, but the maker who is a stranger to their subject's world owes it more research, more time, more consultation and more humility than one who is not - and should be able to answer, on any film, why this story is theirs to tell. The chapter's law: be accurate to the audience and honest about your argument, obtain consent that is informed and treat it as renewable, and ask whose story this is before you ask how to tell it.

The person in the film has a life after the credits. The relationship: the documentary subject is not a source, an actor or a contractor - they are a person who has let a stranger into their life, and the relationship (the months of access, the trust, the meals, the daily presence - DOC-4) creates obligations the release does not mention: HONESTY about the film's direction as it changes (the subject who thought it was about the strike and finds it is about them), PRESENCE after the shoot (the maker who disappears at wrap has taken something and left), and the DISTINCTION between friendship and use that the maker must hold even when the subject does not (Kirsten Johnson's Cameraperson - DOC-5 - as the operator's confession of this). Harm: the film can damage its subject - LEGALLY (the admission on camera; the immigration status revealed; the crime confessed - the maker's obligation to warn, and the cases where the footage was subpoenaed), SOCIALLY (the community that turns on the person who talked; the sexuality or belief made public; the family exposed), ECONOMICALLY (the employer who saw it), PSYCHOLOGICALLY (the trauma re-lived on camera and then re-lived at every screening; the subject's mental health as the maker's concern, and the support some productions now fund), and REPUTATIONALLY (the portrait the subject did not expect); the DUTY OF CARE that the profession is slowly formalising - the risk assessment for the subject as well as the crew (DOC-5 CH 1), the psychological support, the safeguarding for vulnerable subjects, the aftercare, and the PLAN for the film's release (the subject warned, prepared and, where possible, present). The vulnerable subject: the child (DOC-3's Titicut inmates; A-10 CH 4's protections from the acting side), the person in crisis, the person with impaired capacity, the person in an institution (Wiseman's whole subject, and the ban's argument - DOC-3 CH 1), the person in danger (the dissident, the witness, the abused - the face blurred, the voice altered, the location hidden, and the security protocols of the human-rights documentary), and the person who wants to be in the film for reasons the maker can see are not in their interest - the hardest refusal in the form. Money: the question the profession pretends is settled - the journalistic tradition that subjects are not paid (to preserve independence and the audience's trust), the documentary tradition that mostly follows it, and the growing argument that a subject whose life is the film's entire value should share its proceeds (the profit-share deals some makers now sign; the payment for time and expenses that most now make; the celebrity subject who is paid and the poor one who is not, as an indictment); the track's position is that the maker must decide and disclose - the audience is owed the knowledge if a subject was paid, and the subject is owed a clear answer before they agree. The preview: the film shown to the subject before lock (DOC-6 CH 3's practicum) - the profession is divided (the journalistic refusal of veto; the collaborative model's requirement), and the track's position is that the subject should see it and be heard, that veto is a separate negotiation made at access (DOC-4 CH 1), and that a film its subject recognises as fair is stronger, not weaker, for the screening. The long term: the subject's life after the film - the decade-later reunion films (Hoop Dreams' sequels; the Up series' fifty-year relationship, in which several subjects came to resent the franchise and stayed anyway), the maker's ongoing obligation, and the honest admission that a film can change a life for the worse and the maker will not be there when it does. The chapter's law: treat the subject as a person with a life after the film; assess and mitigate the harm the film can do them; decide and disclose the money question; show them the film; and stay in touch - because the release form is where your obligations begin, not where they end.

The presence: the camera is in the room and changes it (DOC-3 - Rouch's fly in the soup), and the maker's decision about how to handle that is an ethical one before it is aesthetic - the OBSERVATIONAL choice (the crew as quiet as possible, the presence hidden from the audience - honest as long as the film does not claim the room was unchanged), the PARTICIPATORY choice (the presence in the film - honest by construction, and open to the sin of the film that becomes about the maker), and the REFLEXIVE choice (the presence examined - DOC-8 CH 3). When to step in: the maker as a person before a filmmaker - the subject about to be harmed, the child in danger, the crime in progress; the journalistic tradition of non-intervention (the observer who does not become the story) against the human obligation, and the track's position that the human obligation wins every time, and that the film that costs a subject their safety to protect its own purity is not worth making. The maker's power: the asymmetry - the maker controls the edit, the release, the money and the frame, and the subject controls none of it; every decision in this module is made from that position, and the ethics of the form are the ethics of power used on people who agreed to less than they got. The chapter's law and the module's: know that your presence changes the room and decide honestly what to do about it; stop rolling when the moment is not yours; step in when a person needs you more than the film does - and remember that you hold all the power, and they trusted you anyway.

DOC-8 · Year 2 · Module Eight

Authorship & Voice

The Trailer

Chris Marker's Sans Soleil is narrated by a woman reading letters from a cameraman who may not exist, about footage shot in Japan, Guinea-Bissau, Iceland and San Francisco, on memory, time, cats, Hitchcock and the impossibility of representing anything at all. It is the essay film's masterpiece and it obeys none of the previous seven modules' rules: no character, no arc, no access negotiated, no interview, no contract with the audience except the promise of a mind at work. Marker called it a film about "the thing that makes a person say 'I'." That is this module's subject.

Authorship and voice: the documentary as a thinking, first-person, self-examining form. The essay film - the Montaigne tradition on celluloid, from Marker and Varda to the found-footage essayists and the desktop documentary. The personal and first-person documentary - the maker's own life as subject, the family film, the diary, the memoir with a camera, and the particular ethics of filming the people who raised you. And reflexivity - the film that shows its own making, from Vertov's cameraman to Stories We Tell's late reveal, as the form's most honest answer to the question every documentary is asked: how do you know? Every documentary has a voice. This module is about the ones that admit it.

Three chapters: the essay film; the personal and first-person documentary; and reflexivity. Say "I."

DOC-9 · Year 2 · Module Nine

Forms & Platforms

The Trailer

In December 2015 a streaming service released ten hours about a murder in Wisconsin, and the documentary's economics changed. The true-crime series had existed - The Staircase, The Jinx - but Making a Murderer's global binge established the multi-part non-fiction series as the streamers' most reliable original content, and within five years the feature documentary that once found its audience through a festival and a broadcaster was competing with an eight-episode format that had a cliffhanger every fifty minutes and a commissioning brief written by an algorithm. The form has always been shaped by its platforms - the newsreel, the television strand, the festival feature - and this module is the current map.

Forms and platforms: the feature documentary and what it needs; the series, with its episodic structure and its industrial pressures; the short and the ultra-short, from the festival short to the platform vertical. The interactive documentary and the webdoc - the branching, database and VR non-fiction that the 2010s promised and mostly abandoned, and what survived. And the hybrid - the doc-fiction that stages, casts and scripts while remaining documentary in its contract, which is the form's most creative current edge and the place where DOC-5's and DOC-7's questions are asked hardest. The form follows the platform. The maker's job is to know which platform they are making for, and what it will do to the film.

Three chapters: the feature, the series and short-form; the interactive documentary and the webdoc; and the hybrid doc-fiction. Choose the container.

DOC-10 · Year 2 · Module Ten

World Documentary

The Trailer

In 1968 Fernando Solanas and Octavio Getino released La hora de los hornos - The Hour of the Furnaces - a four-hour Argentine film in three parts, screened clandestinely under a dictatorship in union halls and churches, with the projector stopped at intervals so the audience could argue about what they had seen. The following year they published "Towards a Third Cinema," which declared that First Cinema was Hollywood, Second Cinema was the European auteur film, and the Third was a cinema of decolonisation made by and for the people it depicted, in which the film was an act rather than a spectacle. Most documentary curricula mention it in a paragraph. This track gives it a chapter, because it is the most consequential theory of non-fiction produced outside Europe and North America, and because the questions it asked about who films whom are the ones DOC-7 spent a module on.

This module is the world's non-fiction. Third Cinema and the militant documentary - Latin America's revolutionary film, Africa's post-colonial cinema, the activist and collective traditions that treated the documentary as a weapon and a tool. The ethnographic tradition and its critique - the anthropologist's camera from Flaherty and Rouch to sensory ethnography, and the people who turned it around. And the regional non-fiction movements - India's Films Division and its independents, Japan's Ogawa and Hara, China's New Documentary Movement, Iran, the Arab world, Eastern Europe, and the traditions this track cannot fit but can name. The documentary was not invented once. It was invented everywhere, for different reasons, and the reasons are the module.

Minh-ha's REASSEMBLAGE (1982 - Senegal filmed by a Vietnamese-American maker who refuses the ethnographic conventions: "I do not intend to speak about, just speak nearby" - the essay film (DOC-8) as the ethnographic film's refutation; and her essays on the anthropologist's "totalizing quest"). The response: the INDIGENOUS media movement (the Kayapo Video Project - Terence Turner's collaboration and the Kayapo's own use of video for political documentation; the Inuit Broadcasting Corporation and Zacharias Kunuk's Isuma (Atanarjuat and the documentary work); the Aboriginal media units in Australia; the Sámi, Māori and Native American media that turned the camera around - the subjects as makers, which is the only complete answer to the gaze), the COLLABORATIVE ethnography (the film made with, co-authored, community-screened - Rouch's shared anthropology fulfilled; the participatory video of CH 1), the REFLEXIVE ethnography (Asch's Ax Fight; the maker's position examined in the film - DOC-8 CH 3), the SUBTITLED SUBJECT (MacDougall's insistence that the person speak at length in their own language, with the maker's translation visible - DOC-5 CH 2's translated interview, as ethics), and the DECOLONISING scholarship (Fatimah Tobing Rony's The Third Eye; the Indigenous-led critiques; the museum's reckoning with its film archives and the repatriation of footage to the communities filmed). The maker's position: any documentary about a community not the maker's own is an ethnographic film whether it admits it or not, and the tradition's history is the list of ways to get it wrong - and its critique is the list of ways to get it less wrong: the collaboration, the consultation, the community screening, the subject's own voice at length, the maker's position disclosed, the profit shared, and the honest question of whether the film should be made by someone else (DOC-7 CH 1). The chapter's law: film across a difference only with the people on the other side of it - as collaborators, consultants, co-authors or makers - and speak nearby rather than about, because the tradition that spoke about has a century of damage to show for it.

DOC-11 · Year 2 · Module Eleven · Final

Docs in the Industry

The Trailer

Every November, several hundred documentary makers fly to Amsterdam for IDFA, and for a few days the world's largest documentary festival runs a Forum in which makers pitch unfinished films to a table of commissioning editors from broadcasters and funds on three continents, in seven minutes each, with a trailer, in front of an audience. The pitching forum is the documentary industry's most distinctive institution - no fiction market works this way - and it exists because the documentary is financed differently from everything else this Conservatory teaches: in pieces, from public money and mission money and, lately, platform money, by people who must be persuaded to fund a film that does not yet exist.

This final module is the documentary's industry. The festival and streaming market - how a non-fiction film finds its audience and its money, the premiere circuit, the acquisitions, the platform economics and their recent contraction, the broadcaster's surviving role. The pitching forums - IDFA, Hot Docs, Sheffield, Sunny Side, and the seven minutes that decide a film's existence. And distribution and impact campaigns - the release, the educational and institutional markets that sustain the issue film, and the impact campaign, which is the Third Cinema's film-act (DOC-10) reborn as a funded strategy for changing a law, a policy or a mind. The track has taught you to find a story, film it honestly and edit it with conscience. This module is how it reaches the people it was made for. The film is not finished when it is locked. It is finished when it has been seen - and, if it was that kind of film, when something changed.

The impact campaign: the strategy - funded, staffed and planned - for a film to produce CHANGE beyond its viewing: the IMPACT PRODUCER (the role, since the 2000s, of the person who designs and runs the campaign - distinct from the director and the marketing team), the GOALS (a law changed - The Invisible War and military sexual-assault policy; a person freed - The Thin Blue Line (DOC-2), the true-crime series' exonerations; a policy shifted - An Inconvenient Truth's climate discourse, Blackfish and SeaWorld, The Cove; a community mobilised; a conversation started - the smaller goals that most campaigns actually achieve), the METHODS (the strategic screenings - Congress, parliaments, the UN, the boardroom, the union hall; the partnerships with NGOs and advocacy organisations who carry the film to their constituencies; the discussion guides and curricula for the educational market; the petitions and calls to action attached to the film; the media strategy timed to the policy calendar; the subjects as spokespeople, with DOC-7's care), the MEASUREMENT (the theory of change; the metrics - screenings, signatures, coverage, and the actual policy outcomes; the honest evaluation, which most campaigns skip), and the FUNDING (the impact grants - the foundations that fund campaigns separately from films; the film's budget line, DOC-4 CH 2). The critique: the impact campaign as the Third Cinema's film-act with the politics removed (DOC-10 CH 1 - the revolutionary screening became the foundation-funded strategy), the FUNDER's agenda (the mission-driven money that shapes what the film argues - DOC-7 CH 1's truthfulness and the advocacy film's honesty about being one), the SUBJECT's burden (the person whose life was the film now asked to tour it), the OVERCLAIM (the campaign that credits the film with a change that had other causes), and the maker's question - whether the film's purpose was to change the world or to see it, and whether the campaign serves the first at the cost of the second. The release as ethics: DOC-7 CH 2's aftercare at scale - the subjects warned, prepared and, where they choose, present; the community screened to first; the harm that release can do, planned for; the film that reaches its audience without betraying the people in it. The graduation charge: you hold the history and its founding compromises, the modes and their contracts, the argument between the wall and the soup, the research and the access, the shoot that happens once, the edit that decides what it meant, the ethics that no code covers, the voice that says "I," the containers and their pressures, the world's traditions and their reasons, and the industry that funds films that do not yet exist. Find the person, not the topic; get access and keep earning it; be there when it happens; select and order but never assert what the footage does not show; ask before you roll and stay after you wrap; and know how you know. Eleven modules, thirty-one chapters, one contract with the audience, renewed every film. Ask the question. Then make the film that answers it.
