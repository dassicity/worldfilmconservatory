W-1 · Year 1 · Module One

The Elements of Story

The Trailer

Around 335 BC, a Macedonian tutor sat down and reverse-engineered why Greek audiences wept at plays about people who never existed. His lecture notes survive - battered, incomplete, missing the entire volume on comedy - and twenty-three centuries later they are still the most photocopied document in every screenwriting program on earth. Aristotle never saw a film. He would recognize every one you've loved: a person of consequence, wanting something fiercely, meeting reversal and recognition, arriving at an ending that is surprising yet inevitable. The elements haven't changed. Only the projector has.

William Goldman - two Oscars, author of the most quoted sentence in the industry ("Nobody knows anything") - spent his career insisting that screenwriting's craft is real even if its outcomes are unknowable: structure can be learned, character can be built, and still nobody can tell you in advance what will live. Billy Wilder kept the diagnostic version taped to the wall: "If you have a problem with the third act, the real problem is in the first act." Meaning: endings fail because elements failed - a want that was never sharp, stakes that were never real, a premise the story never actually argued. And in a Manhattan drama classroom in the 1940s, a Hungarian émigré named Lajos Egri taught the idea that would tunnel from Broadway into Bombay: every good drama is secretly a proof - a premise like "ruthless ambition destroys itself," demonstrated by characters built precisely to collide. Egri's book became, improbably, a bible of Indian screenwriting; ask the writers who built masala's moral engines.

One warning before the tools come out. Vonnegut liked to say the shapes of stories could be drawn on graph paper - and he also flunked his own thesis proposing it. The elements are not a formula. They are an anatomy. Anatomy doesn't tell you who to love; it tells you why the heart can break.

Two chapters: the working parts - character, desire, conflict, stakes, theme, premise - and the oldest blueprint we have. Learn the anatomy before you attempt surgery.

Chapters

Six words, one machine. A character becomes dramatic the moment they want - desire is story's ignition, and it must be specific, visible, and expensive (Kurosawa's rule for protagonists: "something must be wanting"). Between want and character stands conflict - the obstacle with force of its own; no obstacle, no story, only errand. Stakes answer the audience's silent question - what happens if they fail? - and must escalate; stakes that stay flat are a leak that sinks act two. Theme is what the events add up to mean; and premise, in Egri's strict sense, is theme sharpened into an arguable proposition the whole plot exists to prove: not "greed" but "greed destroys the ones who feed it." The premise is your compass in every draft decision - scenes that don't test it are trespassers, however charming.

"A whole is that which has a beginning, a middle, and an end." It sounds like nothing - until you read Aristotle's definitions. A beginning is that which does not necessarily follow from anything else, but from which something naturally follows; an end follows necessarily from what preceded and requires nothing after; a middle does both. Translation: he's not describing sequence, he's describing causality - the difference between "and then" and "therefore / but." A story is a chain where each link earns the next; "episodic" plots, where events merely follow one another, he names the worst kind. Add his machinery: peripeteia (reversal - the action producing the opposite of its intent), anagnorisis (recognition - ignorance turning to knowledge, devastating when it arrives WITH the reversal), hamartia (the protagonist's fatal miscalculation, not "flaw"), and catharsis - the purge of pity and fear that is tragedy's actual product. His standard for endings - surprising yet inevitable - remains the only note every development executive on earth still gives correctly.

W-2 · Year 1 · Module Two

Structure I

The Trailer

In 1979 a UCLA extension teacher named Syd Field published a slim paperback claiming that successful screenplays share a hidden skeleton: three acts, with "plot points" swinging the story around pages 25–27 and 85–90. Hollywood - an industry starving for anything teachable - swallowed it whole. Studio readers started rejecting scripts whose inciting incidents arrived "late." Writers started engineering to the page number. A craft became, for better and worse, a syllabus.

Then came the escalation. Robert McKee turned structure into theatre - a three-day seminar delivered like Moses with a whiteboard, so famous that Charlie Kaufman wrote it INTO a movie: in Adaptation, a despairing screenwriter attends the real McKee (played by Brian Cox, script approved by McKee himself) and gets bellowed at about wanting to write a film "where nothing happens." Blake Snyder's Save the Cat! went further than Field ever dared - fifteen beats, each with a page number - and after his death a thousand identical second acts prompted a famous Slate autopsy titled "Save the Movie!", accusing his sheet of breaking Hollywood. The backlash produced its own classics: Craig Mazin's "How to Write a Movie" - structure is not a template you fill but the residue of a dramatic argument - and John Yorke's Into the Woods, arguing five acts and change itself, not page counts, are the deep pattern.

Here is the position this module takes, and it is not neutral: paradigms are descriptive, distilled from thousands of stories that worked - invaluable as diagnosis, poisonous as prescription. Billy Wilder structured like a watchmaker and never counted a page in his life. You will learn every map in this room. You will also learn that the map is not the territory - the territory is a character who wants, and a world that says no.

Three chapters: the three-act paradigm, McKee's five-part engine, and the beat sheet - taught as instruments, played as music, never as arithmetic.

Chapters

The paradigm, properly understood: Act One (setup) establishes the world, the want, and the rules, then detonates the inciting incident - the event that knocks life out of balance and makes the story's question unavoidable. Plot Point One locks the protagonist in: a door closes behind them, and Act Two (confrontation) begins - the long war of escalating obstacles, hinged at the midpoint, where something fundamental shifts (a false victory or false defeat; reaction hardening into action; the want beginning its secret conversion into the need). Plot Point Two is the lowest ebb - the all-is-lost - that forces the final commitment, and Act Three (resolution) drives to the climax: the scene where want, need, and theme are settled in one irreversible action. The percentages (25/50/25) are averages, not laws; the causal functions are the actual paradigm. A structure works when each landmark is an earned consequence, not an appointment kept.

McKee rebuilds structure from a smaller particle: the gap. A character takes an action expecting the world's cooperation; the world responds otherwise; the gap between expectation and result forces a riskier action - and story is the chain of widening gaps. From that engine, five parts: the inciting incident (upsetting the protagonist's life-balance and igniting desire), progressive complications (the long deepening - each action closing off easier options, "the principle of the irreversible"), crisis (the ultimate decision: a dilemma between irreconcilable goods or lesser evils - the story's true question asked point-blank), climax (the decision enacted; the deepest character revelation because it's the costliest choice), and resolution (the settled world). Underneath, his accounting system: every scene turns on a value - alive/dead, love/hate, truth/lie - swinging from positive to negative charge; scenes that don't turn a value are, in his court, not scenes.

A beat sheet is structure at working altitude: the story reduced to its 12–20 functional events, one line each, before scenes exist. Its uses are real and plural: as a planning instrument (find the broken spine before writing 110 pages on it), as a diagnostic (beat out your stalled draft and the missing organ shows), and as an industry lingua franca - when a producer says "your All Is Lost is soft," they are speaking Snyder, and you must be fluent. The Save the Cat! fifteen (Opening Image through Final Image, with Catalyst, Debate, Fun and Games - his name for the premise's promised pleasures - Midpoint, Bad Guys Close In, All Is Lost with its "whiff of death," Dark Night of the Soul) is the dominant dialect; the physical version is the board: forty index cards, four rows, rearrangeable - outlining as a contact sport.

Fluent writers beat out finished masterpieces regularly as training: reverse-engineering is the one use of the sheet with no side effects.

W-3 · Year 1 · Module Three

Character

The Trailer

When Vince Gilligan pitched Breaking Bad, he needed one sentence to sell television's most radical character experiment: "We're going to take Mr. Chips and turn him into Scarface." The entire series - five seasons, a cultural earthquake - is that sentence executed. That is what a character arc is when a writer actually means it: not "growth," not a lesson learned in act three, but a designed transformation the whole story exists to make believable, one compromise at a time.

The craft has old bones. E.M. Forster split characters into flat and round in 1927 - and then, characteristically, defended flat ones (you need them; they're the fixed stars round characters navigate by). Jean Renoir handed screenwriting its most dangerous sentence in The Rules of the Game: "The awful thing about life is this: everyone has their reasons." Build antagonists on that line and villainy dies; opposition is born. Lajos Egri demanded three-dimensional "bone structure" - physiology, sociology, psychology - before a character speaks a word. And Hollywood's development culture eventually distilled the modern toolkit into a two-word incision: want versus need - the goal a character chases versus the truth they're avoiding - the gap between them being, in most films you love, the actual subject.

And keep one heresy in your pocket: some of cinema's greatest characters barely change at all. Indiana Jones ends as he began, minus one snake phobia's worth of dignity. Chihiro changes; Totoro doesn't. The masala hero often stands fixed as a moral pole while the world arcs around him. "Character must change" is a paradigm, not a law - what must change is our understanding, or the world, or the mask. This module teaches all three levers.

Protagonist and antagonist, want and need, the mask and the face beneath it. Build people strong enough that the plot becomes merely what they would do.

A protagonist is not "the main character"; it is the character whose desire drives the action and whose capacity for decision the story spends - willful enough to pursue, vulnerable enough to cost. An antagonist, in the professional sense, is not evil; it is the force best designed to attack exactly where the protagonist is weakest (Truby's rule: the necessary opponent), and at the highest grade, the antagonist embodies the story's counter-argument - a living rebuttal of the premise. The Joker isn't fighting Batman; he's prosecuting Batman's thesis that order and decency survive pressure - which is why their interrogation scene plays as philosophy with fists. Design your opposition as the strongest possible case against your theme, and act three writes its own stakes. The arc is the transformation this collision forces: positive (the lie surrendered), negative (the lie embraced - There Will Be Blood's long fall), or flat (the truth held while the world arcs - the tester, not the tested).

W-4 · Year 1 · Module Four

Scene Craft

The Trailer

Sometime in the 2000s, a memo David Mamet wrote to the staff of his TV show The Unit escaped into the wild - typed in furious capitals, and instantly one of the most circulated craft documents alive: "EVERY SCENE MUST BE DRAMATIC. THAT MEANS: THE MAIN CHARACTER MUST HAVE A SIMPLE, STRAIGHTFORWARD, PRESSING NEED... IF THE SCENE IS NOT DRAMATICALLY WRITTEN, IT WILL NOT BE DRAMATICALLY ACTED." And the test that has terrified writers' rooms since: any scene that merely conveys information is - his caps - "SOME BULLSHIT", and the audience will smell it.

The scene is the atom of screenwriting: the unit that gets written, pitched, rehearsed, shot, and cut. William Goldman's law governs its borders - get in late, get out early: enter at the last moment the drama permits, leave before the echo dies; the audience's imagination is your co-writer and it works for free. Robert Towne rewrote Chinatown's scenes past the point of reason - the orange-grove pages alone through draft after draft - because he understood what this module teaches: films are not written; scenes are written, and films are what scenes add up to. Ingmar Bergman put the writer's half of it in one sentence: a scene ends when its question changes.

The Coen brothers offer the module's sly benediction: asked about structure, they've claimed they just write scenes they'd want to watch, one after another. Don't believe them - no one plants and pays off like the Coens - but hear the truth smuggled inside: a script whose every scene turns, surprises, and costs will forgive many structural sins. A perfect outline of dead scenes forgives nothing.

The scene as a unit, the turn and the subtext under it, and the doors - how characters and scenes enter and leave. Master the atom; the molecule is chemistry, not luck.

A scene is a story in miniature: a character enters with an objective, meets an obstacle, deploys tactics, and the collision turns the situation - the scene ends with the world in a different state than it began. That state-change is the unit's definition: McKee's version is the value swing (a charge flips: safe→exposed, together→alone, hidden→known); if no value turns, what you've written is an event, a location, or - Mamet's caps - some bullshit. The professional pre-write, per scene: whose scene is it (the character whose objective drives, not necessarily who talks most)? why now (what makes this moment unpostponable)? what's the in-state and out-state? and what does it do for the spine - because every scene serves two masters: it must be a complete meal AND a course in the larger menu, advancing want, need, or theme. Scenes serving only the spine are exposition; scenes serving only themselves are darlings. Both get cut.

The canon proves the range: Harry Lime enters The Third Man sixty-six minutes late, in a doorway, lit by a window flash, wearing the smile of a man delighted to be caught - a full moral biography in four seconds; Gabbar Singh enters Sholay boots-first, pacing a rock ledge, voice arriving before face ("Kitne aadmi the?") - menace as catechism; Rick enters Casablanca as a HAND signing a check before we ever see his face: authority precedes identity. Late entrances borrow this power at scale - the film teaching us a character through others' fear or longing before delivery. Exits obey the mirror law: how a character leaves a scene - and how the film releases them finally - is the last brushstroke on the portrait; the great deaths and departures are exits designed as hard as entrances.

W-5 · Year 1 · Module Five

Dialogue

The Trailer

On the set of Taxi Driver, the script's page for the mirror scene read, more or less: Travis speaks to himself in the mirror. De Niro improvised into that white space, and "You talkin' to me?" entered the language. File that anecdote correctly: it is not proof that dialogue doesn't matter. It is proof that Paul Schrader had built a character so completely that the actor could speak AS him - which is the whole secret. Great dialogue is character audible; everything else is typing.

Every tradition has its gods here, and they disagree beautifully. Harold Pinter - Nobel lecture and all - built menace from pauses, insisting the speech we hear is "an indication of that which we don't hear... a violent, sly, anguished or mocking smoke screen." Aaron Sorkin writes dialogue as music, confessing the sound matters to him before the sense. Elmore Leonard's rules ordered writers to leave out the parts readers skip and never open with weather. Howard Hawks made actors talk over each other's cues until conversation became jazz. And in Bombay, two writers named Salim Khan and Javed Akhtar - the first Indian screenwriters powerful enough to get their names on the posters - perfected dialogue-baazi: lines built like weapons, designed for a thousand-seat hall to detonate as one body. When Deewaar's Vijay itemizes his empire to his policeman brother - buildings, property, bank balance - the brother answers with seven words that India has never stopped repeating: "Mere paas Maa hai." I have Mother. A moral economy settled in one line.

And hold the counterweight from the start: cinema's deepest tradition distrusts speech entirely. Leone opened a film with ten nearly wordless minutes of flies and dripping water. Ozu pared talk to ritual. The final chapter of this module is silence - because knowing when the page should shut up is dialogue craft too.

Voice, subtext, and exposition; the line as an act of war; and the eloquence of saying nothing. Write speech worth interrupting the silence for.

Chapters

Voice is the fingerprint in the speech: vocabulary, rhythm, syntax, obsession - the qualities that make a line attributable. The working test is brutal and simple: cover the character names on a dialogue page; if you can't re-attach them, the cast is one person wearing hats. Voice is built from inputs you control - education, region, era, self-image, what the character wants language to DO for them (impress, hide, wound, sell) - and its master class is ensemble comedy, where six voices must stay separable at speed. Exposition is dialogue's occupational disease: information the audience needs, delivered as speech nobody would say ("As you know, brother..."). The cures are ancient and reliable: ammunition (McKee's term - let facts emerge as weapons in a fight, because combat launders information), the pope in the pool (Snyder - deliver the data while something watchable happens), dramatize don't summarize, and trust the audience's inference engine: viewers assemble backstory from shrapnel gladly, and resent receiving it as freight.

The page-level tell of failure: characters taking turns delivering positions at each other. That's a panel discussion, not a scene.

W-6 · Year 1 · Module Six

The Short Film Script

The Trailer

In 1962, Chris Marker composed a twenty-eight-minute film almost entirely from still photographs - a man haunted by a childhood image, sent through time toward it. La Jetée contains a full apocalypse, a complete love story, and one of cinema's most devastating endings, in less time than a sitcom episode and with exactly one second of moving footage. It has since seeded features (Twelve Monkeys licensed it outright), and it remains the short form's constitution: a short film is not a small feature. It is a different instrument - closer to the short story or the ghazal than to the novel - and it rewards writers who compose FOR it.

The form is also, practically, where you will live for the next several years. Taika Waititi's Two Cars, One Night - eleven minutes, two parked cars outside a rural pub, kids trading insults into tenderness - earned an Oscar nomination and built the career that followed. Andrea Arnold's Wasp took the short-form Oscar and announced everything her features became. Martin McDonagh warmed up for In Bruges with Six Shooter's twenty-seven lethal minutes. Damien Chazelle, as you know from D-1, financed a feature with eighteen minutes of proof. The short is calling card, laboratory, and art form at once - and festival programmers, who watch thousands, will tell you the failures share one genome: features amputated to fit, ideas too big carried by scripts too small.

Roberta Munroe - who programmed Sundance's shorts for years - wrote the book on those corpses, and her first commandment applies before your first line: the short's question is not "what story do I cut down?" but "what idea is COMPLETE at ten minutes?" One situation. One turn. One resonance that outlasts its runtime. Hemingway allegedly bet he could break hearts in six words - baby shoes, never worn. The attribution is almost certainly false (flag filed); the standard is real.

Two chapters: economy - the craft of compression - and the contained idea: recognizing premises born short. Master this form and you'll never fear a page count in either direction.

Structure II - Alternative, Non-Linear & Deep Paradigms

The Trailer

Cannes, May 1994. The Palme d'Or jury - Clint Eastwood presiding - hands the prize to a film that answers a question with a foot massage debate, kills its most charismatic character in the middle, and resurrects him for the ending because the ending happens before the middle. Pulp Fiction did not invent non-linear storytelling - Kane, Rashomon, and half of world literature got there first - but it detonated it into the mainstream, and for a decade every spec script in Hollywood arrived scrambled, mostly for no reason. Which is the whole lesson of this module in one sentence: the order of telling is a meaning, not a garnish.

Meanwhile, a different gospel was conquering the studios by memo. In the mid-1980s, a Disney story analyst named Christopher Vogler condensed Joseph Campbell's The Hero with a Thousand Faces - the 1949 comparative-mythology tome George Lucas openly credited for saving Star Wars' drafts - into a seven-page practical guide. The memo photocopied itself through every executive suite in town, grew into The Writer's Journey, and made "the Hero's Journey" the most licensed structure on earth. The backlash is now a genre of its own: folklorists point out Campbell cherry-picked his myths; Maureen Murdock answered with The Heroine's Journey after Campbell told her, to her face, that women don't need the journey - "a woman is the place people are trying to get to." Dan Harmon flattened the monomyth into an eight-beat circle for sitcoms. And Japan raised a hand from the back of the room to note that a billion people grew up on stories with no central conflict at all.

McKee drew the whole battlefield as a triangle - classical archplot at the top, minimalism and anti-structure at the base corners - and John Truby dismissed three-act thinking entirely as "a lie that flattens organic stories." They can't all be right. They're all useful. That's Year 2.

Four chapters: scrambled time, the story triangle, the monomyth and its prosecutors, and Truby's twenty-two steps with the braided ensemble film. Structure as a menu - and you, finally, as the cook.

Chapters

Every story has two orders: the fabula (events as they happened) and the syuzhet (events as told) - the Russian Formalists' distinction that makes this whole chapter thinkable. Non-linear writing is the deliberate divergence of the two, and the taxonomy is learnable: scrambled time (Pulp Fiction's loop - the syuzhet ends mid-fabula so the film can close on grace instead of a corpse); reverse chronology (Memento - effect before cause, installing the protagonist's amnesia in the audience's own experience); repeated event (Rashomon - one fabula, four incompatible tellings; the structure IS the epistemology); parallel timelines (braided pasts and presents, The Godfather Part II's rise-and-fall counterpoint); frames and nests (tellers inside tellings); and achronology proper (Eternal Sunshine - memory's order, not the calendar's). The governing law: the audience always experiences YOUR order linearly - so design the order of revelation as its own dramatic arc, with setups and payoffs computed in syuzhet time. Nonlinearity that merely decorates gets un-scrambled by bored viewers; nonlinearity that MEANS (amnesia, memory, grief, testimony) cannot be straightened without destroying the film.

McKee's triangle maps design space itself. At the apex, archplot - classical design: a single active protagonist, external conflict, linear time, causality, a closed ending that answers the story's question. Down one side, miniplot - minimalism: the archplot's features turned inward and down - passive or internal protagonists, multiple leads, open endings that leave the question resonating, conflict as condition rather than combat (Ozu, the Dardennes, most of the festival canon). Down the other, antiplot - the deliberate contradiction of classical logic: coincidence over causality, inconsistent realities, time as a toy - absurdism's home (Buñuel, Godard, the Kaufman third act's whole debate). The critical teaching: these are audience contracts, not quality tiers. Archplot's contract promises answers and charges you formula-risk; miniplot promises truthful texture and charges patience; antiplot promises liberation and charges orientation itself. Mixing contracts unannounced is how films lose rooms: an archplot audience served an open ending feels cheated; a miniplot audience served a car chase feels condescended to.

In 1960, Alfred Hitchcock bought up every copy of a pulp novel he could find so audiences wouldn't learn the twist, forbade late admission to theaters - an unheard-of demand - and killed his movie star forty-seven minutes in. Psycho's marketing was genre-craft as much as its shower was: Hitchcock understood that a genre is a promise, that the audience arrives holding a contract they've signed at a thousand previous films - and that the deepest effects available to a storyteller come from honoring that contract in one hand while breaking it with the other.

The scholars caught up later with useful language. Rick Altman split genre into semantics (the furniture - six-guns, fedoras, haunted houses) and syntax (the deep grammar - what the western is actually ABOUT: wilderness versus civilization at the frontier of law), which explains every genre transplant that works: Kurosawa poured samurai semantics over western syntax; Leone poured it back; Lucas poured both into space. Thomas Schatz mapped genre lifecycles - classical confidence, baroque elaboration, parody, revision - which is why Unforgiven had to arrive AFTER four decades of westerns: Eastwood sat on the script for years, aging into it, then dedicated the film to "Sergio and Don," the two directors who taught him the conventions he was now unmaking. And Wes Craven's Scream proved the cycle's strangest turn: a slasher whose characters know the slasher rules - and die by them anyway.

John Truby's late-career book calls genres "the world's real story structures" - deeper than acts, older than paradigms: each genre a complete philosophy with its own hero, arena, and moral question. Whether or not you go that far, this module's premise stands: nobody transcends a genre they haven't mastered. The contract first. Then the beautiful breach.

Two chapters: the conventions and how to break them honorably - then the blend, storytelling's most dangerous cocktail. Sign the contract. Read the fine print. Rewrite it.

Chapters

A genre's conventions are its load-bearing walls: the obligatory scenes (the detective's visit to the morgue, the lovers' break-up at the low point, the final girl's last stand), the character functions, the arena, and - deepest - the core emotion the audience contracted for (horror's dread, romance's longing, crime's justice-hunger). Altman's split organizes the renovation options: change the semantics and keep the syntax (the western's grammar in space, in Sicily, in a boxing ring) or keep the semantics and invert the syntax (all the six-guns present, the frontier myth indicted - the revisionist move). Schatz's lifecycle tells you which renovations the audience can currently read: subversion is a conversation with expectations, so it requires expectations mature enough to converse - Scream is only possible after Halloween's grammar became folk knowledge; Unforgiven only after the classical western's funeral. The craft rules of honorable breach: know the obligatory scene you're refusing and refuse it visibly (the withheld shootout must be conspicuous, not absent); keep the core emotion even when breaking its delivery system (No Country denies the showdown but delivers dread's full payment); and never subvert what you haven't first made work - parody and revision are graduate programs; the audience must feel the contract honored before they'll enjoy watching it burn.

Blending is genre-craft's high wire: two or more contracts active at once, and every clause must still be honored. The working architecture: designate a dominant (the genre supplying structure - whose obligatory scenes anchor the spine) and treat others as modifiers (supplying tone, texture, or a second emotional register); braid the promises so each genre's payments arrive on schedule (a rom-zom-com owes laughs, longing, AND gore, each at its native rhythm); and manage the tonal transitions - the blend's true difficulty - with the same craft W-4 gave scene-joints: transitional beats that modulate register (comedy draining from a scene as dread enters) rather than jump-cuts between moods that read as accidents. The glue across every successful blend is a unified emotional through-line: Shaun of the Dead survives its hyphenation because every register serves one arrested-development love story; the genres are instruments, the feeling is the song. Bong Joon-ho - the blend's reigning master - plans tonal shifts as precisely as his storyboards (D-2's "Bongtail" at the typewriter): Parasite's descent from con-comedy through thriller into horror and grief is a staircase, each step a scene engineered to lower the floor a single degree - which is why a film that changes genre four times never once feels broken.

W-9 · Year 2 · Module Nine

Global & Regional Story Forms

The Trailer

Amar Akbar Anthony opens with three brothers, separated in childhood, unknowingly donating blood - simultaneously, in one frame, through three tubes - to the mother none of them knows is theirs. Screenwriting manuals would bleed out on the spot: implausible, on-the-nose, structurally promiscuous. The film ran for months, and Manmohan Desai - who made four hit films that single year, 1977 - knew exactly what he was doing: he was not writing a story with a flaw in it. He was writing in a different form, with different laws, for an audience that arrived holding a different contract - one that asks not "is this plausible?" but "is this complete? Did you feed every emotion I brought?"

This module is the curriculum's core promise kept: the world's storytelling forms taught as grammars, not exotica. The masala film - the interval as structural hinge, songs as load-bearing architecture, nine emotions as a design checklist. The song sequence itself - cinema's escape valve for everything censors, realism, and shyness forbid characters to say. Kishōtenketsu - the four-part East Asian structure that builds stories on a turn instead of a war, running from classical Chinese poetry through four-panel manga to (Nintendo's own designers confirm) the level design of Super Mario. And the great melodrama traditions - Sirk's Hollywood weepies, the telenovela's engineered 150-night addiction, anime's cour-structured escalations - forms that move billions while auteur cinema debates them.

Hold Kurosawa's koan over the whole module: when Hollywood remade Seven Samurai, Rashomon, and Yojimbo, the traffic proved the forms translate. When Hollywood remakes of masala films keep failing, the traffic proves something better: some forms are not broken versions of yours. They are complete versions of theirs.

Four chapters: the masala architecture, the song as script, the conflict-less structure, and melodrama's global empires. Hollywood structure is a dialect. Time to be multilingual.

Chapters

The masala film's design target is not a plot shape but an emotional completeness - the classical rasa palette (heroism, love, comedy, pathos, fury, wonder…) treated as a menu the film must serve in full, which is why "genre" never quite applies: the form predates and ignores the single-genre contract, blending as its baseline, not its innovation (W-8's blend chapter, meet its elder). The architecture that carries the buffet: an episodic-yet-moral spine (setpieces and digressions licensed, but the dharmic ledger - family, honor, justice - always settling exactly); the lost-and-found family engine (separation in act one, coincidence-rich reunion as climax - Desai's speciality, melodrama's oldest machine running at Bombay scale); song placement as structural beats (the love duet where courtship would drag, the sad song at the low point, the celebration where community must be felt - CH 2 expands); and the interval as the true hinge: a pre-interval detonation (revelation, reversal, the villain's atrocity) engineered to survive ten minutes of samosas, with the post-interval film effectively a second feature answering the first's question. Write masala as two connected films with one moral account.

The four movements: ki (introduction - a world, a character, a normal presented for its own interest, not as a "setup" awaiting disruption); shō (development - the normal deepened, elaborated, lived in); ten (the turn - the form's soul: not an escalation of conflict but a recontextualization, new information or a shifted vantage that makes everything before mean differently); ketsu (conclusion - reconciliation of the turn with the whole; the pieces settling into a larger picture). Descended from classical Chinese four-line poetry (qǐ-chéng-zhuǎn-hé), naturalized across Japanese and Korean narrative, drilled daily in four-panel manga - and, famously, adopted by Nintendo's designers as level-design grammar (introduce a mechanic, develop it, twist it, resolve it: Mario as structure seminar). The provocative claim attached - "plot without conflict" - needs adult handling: kishōtenketsu stories are full of tension, longing, and even antagonists; what they decline is conflict as the STRUCTURAL ENGINE. The story is not a war with a winner; it is a perception with a deepening. Western drama asks "who will prevail?"; this form asks "what is this, really?" - and the ten answers.

W-10 · Year 2 · Module Ten

Adaptation

The Trailer

Truffaut once put the accusation directly to Hitchcock: why do you only adapt pulp - why never a masterpiece? Hitchcock's answer is this module's cornerstone: a literary masterpiece is already complete - its form IS its content - "I'll have to read Crime and Punishment several times, and even then I won't film it," because to translate it he'd have to replace its essence with cinema, and Dostoevsky doesn't need him. Pulp, though - pulp has a great engine in a cheap chassis. Mario Puzo, who wrote The Godfather "to make money" and said so, watched Coppola strip his potboiler's subplots and find an American epic inside. The lesson lives at both poles: adaptation is not transcription; it is the transfer of an effect from one machine to another - and sometimes the lesser book makes the greater film precisely because there's room to build.

The form's paradoxes are the craft. Charlie Kaufman, hired to adapt Susan Orlean's plotless orchid book, failed so completely that he wrote his failure - and Adaptation won the Oscar the faithful version never could have. Ted Chiang's "Story of Your Life" - a linguistics novella narrated in a tense English doesn't have - became Arrival by inventing a cinematic equivalent for its time-grammar rather than its scenes. Stephen King spent decades publicly hating Kubrick's The Shining ("a big, beautiful Cadillac with no engine"), then produced his own faithful TV version, which proved the point nobody wanted: fidelity and quality are different axes. And in India, Devdas has been adapted for a century across a dozen languages - Barua, Bimal Roy, Bhansali, Anurag Kashyap's Dev.D - the same dying lover re-translated for each generation: adaptation as a culture talking to itself.

Two chapters: the transfer craft - novel, story, and true events - and the law and theology of rights and fidelity. You are not a copyist. You are a translator, and translation is authorship.

W-11 · Year 2 · Module Eleven

The Feature & the Rewrite

The Trailer

In November 1993, Pixar screened a reel of Toy Story for Disney - and Disney shut the production down on the spot. The "Black Friday reel": Woody as a sneering, sarcastic tyrant who hurls Buzz out the window on purpose, so unlikable the room recoiled. Most studios would have buried the film. Pixar asked for two weeks, tore the script to the studs, rebuilt Woody's jealousy as insecurity instead of cruelty - and the rewritten pages became the film that founded an empire. Every Pixar legend since repeats the same secret in different words: our films don't start good. They're rewritten into good - and, per the studio's own proverb, they're never finished, only released.

Rewriting is where screenwriting stops being typing and becomes engineering. Frozen's songwriters handed the story team "Let It Go" - written for Elsa the VILLAIN - and the song was so obviously a wounded heroine's anthem that the entire film restructured around it: villain to sister, and a rewrite cascade through every act. Robert Towne ground Chinatown through drafts for years (you've met the orange groves); Salim–Javed narrated Sholay's sequences aloud to rooms until every beat survived performance; and William Goldman, asked how many drafts a script takes, gave the working writer's answer - as many as it takes, and the first one is only you telling yourself the story.

The Hemingway line every workshop quotes - "the first draft of anything is [unprintable]" - is, our apocrypha flag insists, poorly sourced and probably polished by retelling. Keep the standard, lose the citation. Because the sentence underneath it is true and this module's whole law: nobody writes great screenplays. People rewrite them.

Two chapters: the ladder from logline to draft - outlining, the treatment, the pages - and the rewrite itself: passes, notes, and the development gauntlet. The draft is the ore. This is the smelter.

W-12 · Year 2 · Module Twelve

Writing for Television & the Room

The Trailer

You already know the pitch - you met it in W-3: "We're going to take Mr. Chips and turn him into Scarface." What that sentence bought Vince Gilligan was the thing features can't sell: sixty-two hours. Television is the long form - character change purchased one compromise at a time across years - and it is, in the industry's oldest aphorism, the writer's medium: film hands final authority to the director; TV hands it to a writer with a second job title. The showrunner. When David Chase ended The Sopranos on a hard cut to black, HBO didn't overrule him. Nobody could. That's the throne this module describes - and the ladder of folding chairs beneath it.

The room is the form's engine and its strangest gift: six to twelve writers around a whiteboard, breaking story out loud - pitching, arguing, "putting it on the board" - a collective intelligence that features almost never enjoy. Javier Grillo-Marxuach, a veteran of a dozen rooms, distilled running one into the "Eleven Laws of Showrunning," whose first law shocks every auteur instinct: your job is not to have all the ideas; it is to make the best idea win, whoever pitched it. The room's economics are also a battlefield: the 2007–08 WGA strike was fought substantially over new-media residuals nobody could yet price; the 2023 strike over streaming's shrunken "mini-rooms" and, for the first time in any labor contract on earth, protections against AI-written scripts. The whiteboard, it turns out, is worth striking for.

And the form is global now: Sacred Games put an Indian writers' room on the world's screens; Korea's sixteen-episode closed dramas (W-9's telenovela cousins) conquered every continent; the BBC's Writersroom reads unsolicited scripts from anyone on earth. The door has never been this wide - or this crowded.

Three chapters: the pilot and the bible, the room and its two great rhythms - episodic and serialized - and the path in: staffing, the Guild, and the long game. Welcome to the medium where writers run the set.
